## **Chapter 2 — The Art of Being Mediocre**

By the end of her second week, Tris had concluded that being a bad mage was the most demanding work she had ever done.

**Not *bad*, precisely \[prose: don’t walk it back, just say what you mean the first time\]**. Adequate. Adequacy was a razor's edge. Fail too often and she'd be sent home to **fictional Capchen \[canon-cont: Capchen is not fictional, and if it were, people would notice\]**; succeed too cleanly and someone would start to wonder. She had spent half her life learning control the way other people learned to breathe, and now control itself had become the danger. Her candle-flames sat too still. Her chalked circles closed too perfectly. When Master Yolenne set the first-years to holding a palmful of mage-fire steady for a count of one hundred, thirty-nine students produced guttering, spitting blobs, and Tris — before she caught herself — produced a sphere so motionless it might have been blown from glass.

"Amberlin." Yolenne's proctor, a bored fourth-year named Isha, paused at her bench. "That's very steady."

"It's stopped listening to me," Tris said, and made the sphere wobble, then flare, then die with a smell of scorched dust. "There it goes."

"Mm," said Isha, and moved on, but she looked back once from the end of the row, and Tris spent the rest of the lesson producing honest, ugly, novice fire and sweating in a way that had nothing to do with heat.

*Sloppiness*, she wrote to Niko that night, in the cipher he'd taught her, in a letter she would burn instead of sending. *You never taught me sloppiness. It turns out it wants as much precision as anything else. I have to fail by exactly the right amount, in the right direction, at the right speed for a girl who is diligent but not gifted. I am keeping a ledger of my mistakes so I don't improve too quickly. Do not laugh.*

He would laugh. **She burned the letter \[prose: we already know she burns this\]** and let Chime eat the flame.

The hostel dining hall sorted itself the way rooms full of young people always sorted themselves, which was to say: cruelly, within days, along lines nobody would admit to. Perin, who could talk to anything with a pulse, floated between tables. Tris, who could not, took her tray on the ninth evening to the end seat nobody wanted, across from the girl nobody sat with.

The girl had gold rings in her ears — Trader style, though she wore them with a Lightsbridge novice's coat — and she was reading while she ate, which Tris considered the mark of a sensible person. She looked up with the flat, braced expression of someone expecting to be told the seat was saved.

"Trisana Amberlin," Tris said. "I'm not going to talk to you. I'm at the good part."

She held up her own book. Something in the girl's face unlocked, one notch, like a warded door recognizing a key.

"Verusi Qais," she said. "Third bell found dead in the counting-house, I assume."

"If you tell me who did it, I'll pour your tea on your head."

They read in silence, companionably, for the rest of the meal. It was, by Tris's standards, a torrid beginning to a friendship.

The facts came out over a week of shared end-seats, in the unforced way Tris preferred: Verusi's mother was Third Ship Qais, her father a Karangi harbormaster's clerk; she'd grown up neither Trader nor *kaq* and been overcharged by both. She was at Lightsbridge on the strength of a merchant uncle's guilt and a genuine, needle-fine talent for calculation-magic. Half the first-years wouldn't work in a circle with her. "They think Traders steal luck," she said, dry as paper. "I keep telling them you can't steal what they haven't got."

"I grew up with a Trader," Tris said, before she could weigh it. "She's a smith now. The best I know."

Verusi's eyebrows went up a fraction — merchant girls from Capchen did not, as a rule, grow up with Traders — and Tris turned the page of her book and cursed herself, methodically, in Tradertalk, which would have made it worse if she'd done it aloud.

*One loose thread*, she reminded herself. The trouble with threads was that she lived among people now. People pulled.

The third friend she did not choose at all. The third friend came with the bread.

Havrik's family ran the bakery at the bottom of Spindle Lane, and Havrik ran the ovens before dawn and sat first-year lectures after, eyes red-rimmed, flour in his eyebrows, taking notes in a fist like a man strangling the pen. He was nineteen, built like a door, and failing.

He was failing, Tris saw within one shared practicum, because Lightsbridge was teaching him upside down. When Master Yolenne made him call fire with the Esrolat charm, the syllables fell out of his big hands like dropped crockery and nothing lit. But she had bought bread at his family's shop every morning for a month, and the ovens loved him. She'd felt it the first time she walked in — heat leaning toward the boy the way it leaned toward her, the fire banking and rising to his mood while he thought he was only judging the coals by eye. His loaves rose in weather that flattened every other bakery's. He was an ambient mage, oven-and-hearth, unmistakable as a shout, and this whole grey university was marking him a failure because he couldn't do with a charm what he did every morning by instinct.

Nobody had seen it. Nobody here knew how to look. Lightsbridge tested for academic magic and called everything else superstition, and so a mage was flunking out of a school of magic, and Tris walked home past the bakery with her jaw set so hard her teeth ached.

She couldn't say anything. Saying something meant explaining how a stolid, adequate girl from Capchen recognized wild ambient magic on sight. Saying something meant Niko's whole winter of concealments unpicked for a baker's boy she'd known a month.

She bought a fourth morning bun she didn't want. "Your oven runs hot on the left," she said gruffly.

"Everyone says that." Havrik wiped his hands. "It doesn't, though. Left side just... does what I mean better." He heard himself, laughed, embarrassed. "That's daft."

"Yes," said Tris, around the thing in her throat. "Daft."

The nearest of near misses, that first term, was not her magic at all. It was Chime.

The rule was simple: the dragon stayed at Dama Wilget's. A living glass dragon — the only one in the world, made of lightning and a glassblower's panic in a Tharios shop — was not a pet a stolid Capchen girl could explain. Chime had accepted the rule with the good grace of an empress accepting a curfew, which was to say she observed it precisely until she didn't.

She turned up on a Firesday evening in Snow Moon, flowing through the shutter-gap of the hostel study like poured water, while Tris and Perin sat over theory notes and Perin was mid-flight on the subject of ward harmonics. Tris felt the change in the air a half-second before — the little bow-wave of cool that Chime pushed ahead of herself — and did the only thing there was time to do, which was knock the entire ink bottle across Perin's notes.

The next minute was blotting and apology and Perin's genuinely heroic refusal to be upset, and under it, along the private thread of sense she kept for the dragon, Tris said things that would have blistered the shutters. Chime, wounded, withdrew to the eaves. She sulked there, invisible against the frost, until Perin left, and then poured herself down onto the desk and presented, in apology, a small perfect flame-flower of blue glass — the color of the candles Tris bought when she was homesick.

"You *cannot*," Tris told her, and then ruined it entirely by picking her up.

The flower she should have crushed to powder. Evidence, all evidence, had to go. Instead it went into the spelled pouch, against the medallion, where it clinked faintly sometimes when she moved — one more thing between her shoulder blades that she was not prepared to explain, and not prepared to give up either.

*You are getting sentimental*, she wrote to Niko, in cipher, in a letter for burning. *This place is making me worse. Yesterday I let Perin explain warding theory to me for a full hour, wrongly, because interrupting him felt like kicking a dog. In Summersea I'd have interrupted an archduke. I don't know what's happening to me and Chime is no help, she has decided the weaver's boy smells like candles.*

And then there was Master Ostrey.

Ostrey lectured in elemental theory to a hall of two hundred, and he had — Tris had to concede — a genuine gift: he could take the living, breathing world and render it into diagram until it lay still. He had one other quality, and it was worse than malice. He had a favorite.

"Observe Amberlin's working," he announced in the fifth week, projecting her chalked exercise onto the wall with a glance. "No flourish. No *intuition*." The word came out with tongs. "Procedure, correctly ordered, competently executed by a student of no unusual gift. This is what magecraft *is*, gentlemen and ladies. Not the fits and vapors of some village weather-hag dancing for rain — " two hundred students dutifully sniggered, and the banked lightning over Tris's ears turned over in its sleep " — but repeatable, examinable method. Talent is common. It is in every hedge and gutter, wasted. *Discipline* is rare. Amberlin, stand."

Tris stood, adequate, stolid, twenty-two years old, an accredited mage of Winding Circle with a medallion between her shoulder blades, and let two hundred people look at the shape of everything she was pretending to be.

"There is more true magic in this unremarkable young woman," Ostrey said warmly, "than in every storm that ever frightened a fishwife."

"Thank you, Master," said Tris, and sat, and did not set fire to anything at all — which was, she reflected, watching her own hands unclench, easily the second-finest piece of magic she had done that year.

## **Chapter 3 — Weather Sense**

The drought came up the valley in Rose Moon and stopped, like a cart with a broken axle, directly over Lightsbridge.

Tris felt it before the farmers did. She felt it the way one feels a splinter working deeper: a wrongness in the air's grain, moisture that should have swung in off the coast pooling instead behind the western ranges, pressure building and building with nowhere lawful to go. The river dropped a foot, then three. The millrace ran thin. By midsummer the city's timbers ticked and split in the heat, wells drew brown, and fire-watch bells rang somewhere every night — a hayloft, a chandlery, a tenement's shared kitchen.

And she could have fixed it. That was the splinter under the splinter. Give her a week, a hilltop, and permission to be herself, and she could reach west and *comb* that snarled weather straight — walk the rains over the ranges in decent order, fill the wells, set the millwheels turning. Instead she sat in lecture halls learning the academic theory of water — its four aspects, its seven lawful summonings — while the true water, the real actual water of the world, hung two hundred miles west like laundry snagged on a nail, and she was the only soul in the city who could feel it.

"You look like you're passing a kidney stone," Verusi observed.

"It's the heat."

"It's July. You've looked like this since May."

Meditation helped, some. She kept to the discipline Niko had drilled into her half her life — breath and counting, morning and night, the winds combed out and the lightning fed in thin threads to the little iron ground-rod she'd sunk, with apologies to Dama Wilget, in the boardinghouse garden. Chime helped more. On the worst nights the dragon lay across the back of her neck like a cool hand and sang, almost below hearing, the note glass sings before it's blown, and Tris held on to that note and did not go up to the roof and do something unforgivable to the sky.

*The weather isn't yours to mend*, Niko said in her memory. *It never was. Even at home, you ask leave.*

*There's no one here to ask*, she argued with the memory. The memory, being Niko, declined to find this convincing.

The fire-watch bells found her at last on a night in mid-Wort Moon, and gave her a look at exactly how thin her discipline had worn.

The blaze was in Tanners' Row — a hide-store gone up, three buildings involved by the time she reached the bottom of Spindle Lane, and the whole quarter out in the street passing buckets. She got into the line without deciding to, between a cooper and a night-soil man, and found Havrik already anchoring the pump crew, stripped to the waist and flour-pale under the soot, working the handle like a man who meant to break either it or himself.

Here was the thing she could not do: reach up-valley, find the wet air pooled over the river marshes, and drag it across the city in a blanket. Ten minutes' work. The fire would have died like a candle under a cup, and every mage on the hill would have known that something impossible had walked through their instruments.

Here was the thing she did instead, and it was more dangerous, and she did it anyway: she leaned on the wind. Not summoning — *denying*. The fire was pulling its own draft down the Row, sucking air along the cobbles to feed itself, the way big fires learned to breathe; and Tris, hauling buckets with her sleeves smoldering, quietly starved it. She stood in the bucket line and held the air of Tanners' Row still — a smothering stillness, an absence, nothing an instrument could point to — and the fire, bewildered, lost its roar, and slumped, and became merely a bad fire instead of a district-eater, and the pumps and the buckets did the rest.

It took four hours. Holding a wind is easy; holding a *no wind* against a fire's hunger is like standing on a hose. By the end she was trembling in a way she disguised as exhaustion, which was simple, because it was also exhaustion.

"You want water," Havrik said afterward, appearing beside her with a dipper. His voice was raw with smoke. He looked at her a moment too long, with the particular puzzlement of a man whose senses had told him something his education couldn't hold. "Funny thing. Fire like that — it makes its own wind, my da always says, that's what kills. This one never did." He drank, and passed her the dipper, and said, not quite a question: "Never did."

"Lucky," said Tris.

"Aye," said Havrik, in the voice of a man filing something under thorough, "lucky."

Three days later she passed Anselm Radcastle on the library stair, and he paused, and said pleasantly, apropos of nothing: "A cousin of mine on the fire-watch says the Tanners' Row blaze behaved oddly. No draft. His captain's never seen the like." He smiled. "You were in the bucket line, I hear. Public-spirited. Capchen raises such *civic* girls."

It was in Wort Moon, in short, that Anselm Radcastle began paying attention to her, which was precisely as pleasant as a stone in her shoe.

Anselm was a second-year, nephew to a chancellor's secretary, handsome in the well-fed way of boys who had never once carried their own luggage. He collected information the way Perin collected conversation, except that Perin gave it all away again and Anselm invested it. He had made himself unofficial registrar of everyone's origins — who was worth cultivating, who was scholarship chaff — and somewhere in his ledgers, Trisana Amberlin of Capchen had come up short a column.

"Amberlin," he said, dropping uninvited onto the library bench across from her. "I've cousins in Capchen. The copper Amberlins, the counting-house on Wave Street — that's your aunt? Odd. My cousin buys copper, and he doesn't know the name."

"It's a big city," Tris said, not looking up, "and your cousin should ask his own clerks who he buys from."

"Mm. And you sat trials at twenty-two. Educated first, clearly — your theory's too finished for a counting-house girl. Educated where?"

"Privately. Master Goldeye of Summersea." **A real name, a real man, a tutor who owed Niko a favor and would swear to it. \[canon-cont: anyone would assume Master Goldeye was Niko\]** They had built the lie with a spine of truth, but Tris felt the tug all the same — the thread, pulled.

"Summersea," Anselm repeated, pleasantly, and wrote nothing down, which was worse than if he had. "Funny, the people one turns out to know in common. I'll ask around."

She watched him go and thought, with cold clarity: *he doesn't suspect the truth. The truth is too big for him. He suspects something small — debts, a bastardy, a bought name. He'll dig for a scandal his own size.* The trouble was where the digging led. Letters to Summersea. Letters, if he was thorough, to Emelan. And Trisana Chandler's description had been in every broadsheet from Tharios to Namorn at one time or another: *stout, red-haired, spectacles, sharp-spoken. Four braids. Weather.*

That night she wrote three letters she couldn't send.

*Sandry — There's a boy here who's begun asking questions, and I find I can't even be properly angry, because the lie was my idea and it itches worse than he does. You'd know what to say to him. You'd have known what to say to all of them, all along. I'm learning what you've always known — that a name is a garment, and I've cut this one too tight under the arms.*

*Daja — There's a girl here the others won't circle with because her mother was a Trader. I sat with her. I didn't tell her why. I'm getting so tired, Daja, of not telling people why.*

*Briar — Your customs stamp has held up beautifully, you criminal. Everything else is coming apart at a rate you'd find hilarious.*

She sealed them, warded them, and put them in the box with the others — fourteen letters now, a whole correspondence with silence — because anything mailed could be traced, and the thread only had to be pulled once.

She took to walking the river path at dusk, those weeks, because the water was honest company. The river was down to its bones — old pilings showing, a drowned wagon surfacing mid-channel like a memory the water had decided to confess — and she walked its bank the way one sits with a sick friend, doing no good, refusing to leave.

Verusi fell in beside her on one of those walks, uninvited, which from Verusi was intimacy.

"My mother's people say weather has a ledger," she said, after half a mile of good silence. "Every drought is owed a flood. Sky keeps accounts like a Trader, pays like a *kaq* — late, all at once, and in the middle of the night." She glanced sideways. "You keep looking west like the debt's yours."

"I'm merchant stock," Tris said, which had the advantage of being true. "We notice unpaid accounts."

"Mm." Verusi walked on a few paces. "In Third Ship Qais, when a person watches the horizon that way, we ask if they've left something out there." Another pace. "You needn't say. I've noticed you're a private person, Trisana Amberlin." The alias sat oddly in her mouth that once — deliberate, weighed, like a coin she suspected of being shaved — and then she let it go, and talked about calculation-magic the rest of the way home, and Tris understood she had just been extended credit, on terms, by someone whose people did not extend it lightly.

That was the trouble with the lie, she thought that night, feeding lightning down the garden rod while Chime dozed on the warm stones. It had been designed against enemies. Nobody had warned her it would work best on her friends.

The end of her patience, when it came, came for Havrik.

Ostrey had taken to examining first-years viva voce, at random, for sport. In the last week of Wort Moon he stood Havrik up in front of the full theory lecture — Havrik, grey with a four-o'clock baker's morning, his notes in that strangled fist — and fed him the Esrolat sequence question by question, and let him fail, and *kept going*. Five minutes. Ten. The hall began to titter. Ostrey's voice grew kinder and kinder, which was his cruelty; he handled the boy like a specimen, turning him this way and that in front of two hundred people to display the anatomy of inadequacy.

"You see the difficulty," he told the hall, as Havrik stood there with his ears burning. "Admissions grow sentimental. They see an honest artisan's son and mistake sweat for aptitude. But magic is not bread, whatever — " the pause was delicate, practiced — "*rises* in his family's ovens. Some material simply cannot be taught. Sit down, Master Havrik. Bring your fees to the bursar by Longnight or spare yourself the—"

The lecture hall's forty windows shuddered in their frames — one long rolling buck, west wall to east, like a carthorse shaking off flies. Chalk dust leapt from the rails. Two hundred students looked up. High and far off, in a cloudless sky over a drought-locked valley, thunder said one short syllable and stopped.

Tris sat with her hands folded and her eyes down, heart slamming, hauling the reins of herself with both fists. *Stupid. Stupid. Ten years of control and you rattle windows for a baker.*

"Settle," Ostrey told the hall, irritated. "Heat spasm. Dry air is capacitive — we'll treat the theory in Barleymoon. Sit *down*, Master Havrik."

Havrik sat. Across the hall, his big shoulders came down half an inch, because every eye had gone to the windows and away from him, and he alone in the room looked grateful to the weather.

And four rows back, Anselm Radcastle was not looking at the windows at all. He was looking at Trisana Amberlin, folded hands, downcast eyes, a girl doing absolutely nothing — and he was smiling like a man who has finally found the loose thread, and means to enjoy pulling it.

## **Chapter 4 — The Storm**

The drought broke on the third day of Barleymoon, and it did not break gently.

Tris woke at midnight with her teeth buzzing. Two hundred miles west, the snarl behind the ranges had finally torn loose — three months of stalled ocean air let go all at once, and the whole vast debt of it was coming down the valley to be paid in a single night. She stood at her window in her nightgown and read the western sky the way other people read a death notice. Cloud to thirty thousand feet, green-black at the base. Hail in the upper vaults. The river already rising off snowfields of rain in the high country, hours ahead of the storm itself. And lightning — she could feel the charge building against her skin like wool on a dry day — more lightning banked in that one system than she carried in all her braids together.

Bearing down on a timber city that had spent all summer turning itself into kindling. On a university whose seven colleges kept their four hundred years of books under proud copper roofs. On a millrace that would take the flood-crest sideways.

*Now*, she thought, with a calm that surprised her, *I am going to have to ask someone's leave, and there is no one in this city with the authority to give it, and I am going to do it anyway.*

She dressed. She braided. She took the spelled pouch off the bedpost and hung the medallion around her neck for the first time in a year, under her shirt, where she could feel it lying over her heart like a hand. Chime flowed up her arm and settled at her collar, chiming a low, excited arpeggio.

"Yes," Tris told her. "Probably."

She went down Spindle Lane first, banging on shutters as she passed — the boardinghouse, the chandler's, the cobbler with the six children — with the flat authority of a merchant's voice that expected to be obeyed. At the bakery the ovens were already lit, and Havrik took one look at her face in the doorway's glow and put down his peel.

"Get the flour off the ground floor," Tris said. "All of it, up two stories, and your grandmother with it. The river's coming into the low streets by morning."

"Sky's clear," said Havrik's father, behind him, floury and reasonable.

"It won't be." She held the man's eye and let him see whatever there was to see in hers; behind her, away west, the horizon lit soundlessly, once, twice, like a signal answered. "Bank your fires by second bell. Wet the roof. Tell the Row."

"Do it," Havrik said quietly, already moving, and did not ask her how she knew, and Tris added that to the ledger of things she owed him.

The university, to its credit, was not asleep. Lightsbridge had weather-workers of the academic school — she'd sat their lectures — and its wards had felt the storm too, in their procedural way. Bells were ringing on the hill. Lamps moved along the colonnades of Initiates' Court. She arrived to find half the faculty in the quadrangle arguing about theory while the western sky ate the stars, and the first outriding gusts came down Spindle Lane smelling of river-bottom and cold iron.

"—the Varosh deflection, anchored on all seven college wards—"

"The Varosh is rated for squalls, Master, look at that base, you'll snap every anchor on the hill—"

They weren't wrong, either of them, Tris thought, standing at the edge of the lamplight in her drab coat. The Varosh deflection was a good procedure. It was a very good procedure for one-tenth of what was coming.

"Amberlin."

Anselm. Of course, Anselm — out of the crowd at her shoulder in the wind, and even now, even under that sky, his face had the bright covetous look of a man cashing an investment. He had a leather folder under his arm and he actually, she would remember this always, he actually opened it, in the rising wind, and held out a page for her to see. A broadsheet, Tharian, four years old, creased from traveling. An engraver's sketch of a stout young woman with spectacles and lightning crawling along her braids, above a column about the cleansing of Kadasep and the mage who had hauled the killer of the *yaskedasi* out of a glass prison of her own student's making.

"The Summersea letters came back three weeks ago," he said, pitched under the wind, just for her. "No copper Amberlins. But Master Goldeye's neighbor was chattier than Master Goldeye, and one thing led on to another, and — *Chandler*. I confess it's bigger than I hoped." His smile widened. "Weather-hag. Ostrey will die. What's it worth to you, kept quiet? Because from where I stand—"

Tris never did find out where he stood, because the storm arrived.

The front hit the hill like a shoulder against a door. Every lamp in the quadrangle went out at once. The first hailstones burst on the flagstones like flung crockery, and beneath the wind's shriek Tris felt — before she heard, feeling being faster — the charge in the cloud-base above the Great Library find its answer in four hundred years of proud copper roofing, and reach down.

She had one heartbeat. Enough to choose, if she was quick, between Trisana Amberlin and the library.

It wasn't close.

She took the strike out of the air twelve feet above the ridgeline.

It came down to her the way lightning had always come to her, joyfully, like a dog called home — a river of white fire bending away from the copper in defiance of every law in Ostrey's syllabus, pouring across the quadrangle sky and into the small drab girl by the colonnade, into her lifted hands, down into the four thin braids that leapt loose of her cap and stood glowing off her shoulders. Thunder arrived an instant later and knelt on the whole hill.

In the ringing dark that followed, in the light of her own hair, Tris looked at the faculty of Lightsbridge — forty masters, frozen mid-procedure, their faces a long row of white ovals — and at Anselm, sitting down hard in the hail with his folder pressed to his chest like a shield.

She spat his broadsheet page's worth of introduction onto the wind.

"Trisana Chandler," she said. "Accredited, Winding Circle. Storm's mine. *Move.*"

## **Chapter 5 — Trisana Chandler**

There was no time to enjoy their faces, which was as well, because there would be time later to be sick about it.

"The flood-crest reaches the mill bridge in under two hours," she said. Her voice came out the way it did when she worked — flat, quick, certain, the voice of the tide table and the barometer. "The race will throw it into Tanners' Row. I want every earth-mage you have raising the bank between the mill and the row — your Varosh anchors will hold *that*, it's only water at ground level. Fire wardens to the library roofs, all seven colleges; I'll keep the lightning off you, but hail's coming through in a quarter hour that I can't blunt and stop the rain too, and I need the rain. This valley is three months in debt and we are paying *tonight*, in installments, on my count. Questions?"

"You cannot possibly—" began an academic weather-master, by pure reflex.

"That one doesn't count," said Tris, and went up the library's outside stair to the roof.

What she did for the next six hours, no one at Lightsbridge had the vocabulary to describe, which did not stop them describing it for years. The university's instruments recorded it in their procedural way: a storm-front of catastrophic potential arriving, and then *behaving*. Rain in hard, steady sheets — never quite hard enough to slide the hills. The hail-core, that would have broken every skylight and glasshouse in the city, lifting inexplicably over the valley and spending itself against the eastern ridges. Lightning, all night, striking one place only: a small figure on the Great Library roof, over and over, that took each strike the way a clerk takes correspondence, filed it, and fed it in threads down her body into copper and stone and bedrock until the whole hill hummed. The river-crest arrived at the mill bridge and found new earthworks, forty exhausted academics, and a wall of wind lying across the water like a hand, and went where it was told.

From inside, it was simpler than any of that, and harder.

From inside it was bookkeeping — the oldest kind, the kind she'd been bred to before anyone knew what she was. The storm was a debt come due, and debts could not be canceled, only restructured. So she stood on the wet copper with her feet spelled to the ridgeline and her hair a live thing around her, and she negotiated. *This much rain, this hour, and no more. The hail east — the ridges can stand it; the glasshouses can't. That charge to me. That one. That one.* Each stroke of lightning was a shock like being born; each one she took, tamed, thinned, and paid out down the library's bones into the hill, and the storm, which was not angry — storms are never angry, that was the first lie she'd ever unlearned — kept offering, because offering was its nature, and she kept accepting, because accepting was hers.

Chime rode her shoulder the whole night, blazing brighter with every strike, singing the note glass sings, and somewhere in the third hour Tris understood that the dragon was doing more than keeping her company — she was holding pitch, one steady tone in all that shriek, and Tris tuned herself to it the way a drowning swimmer tunes to a shore light. In the fourth hour her knees went, and she worked kneeling. In the fifth hour she caught herself laughing — mad, whipped-away laughter, hail rattling off the wards she'd bent over the fire wardens on the roofs below — because eleven months of doing everything small, and *this*, tonight, was the truth of her, writ across a whole sky where every soul in Lightsbridge could read it, and the truth was: she had missed herself.

In the sixth hour there was only the count. Breath and counting, morning and night, Niko's voice under the thunder: *control, Trisana, is not a cage. It is a courtesy the strong owe the world.* She counted. She paid. The debt came down.

Toward dawn, the storm — combed, drained, spent down from monster to mere hard weather — trailed off east, grumbling, in decent order.

Tris came down the library stair at first light with her hair in ruins, steam rising off her shoulders, and Chime blazing at her collar like a lamp, and found the entire university waiting for her in the wet quadrangle, and thought, *now comes the difficult part.*

She slept eleven hours and woke to a changed street.

It announced itself before she reached the window: the pitch of Spindle Lane was wrong. She had spent a year learning the lane's morning voice — cartwheels, the cobbler's children, Dama Wilget's war with the milk-seller — and now under all of it ran a hush that traveled with her shadow when she moved past the glass. She dressed and went down, because the one thing ten years of being feared had taught her was that hiding from it fed it.

Dama Wilget met her at the foot of the stairs with her chin up and her hands knotted in her apron, loyal as a landlady defending her own respectability, which in a sense she was.

"They've been coming by all morning," she said. "Gawpers. I've swept the step at them twice." A pause; the knuckles whitened. "The dragon. When she makes the glass. That'll be — that was magic too, then. All along. In my parlor."

"Yes," said Tris.

"And you'd have told me, if she was a danger to the house."

"She's a danger to your candle budget. Nothing else under this roof is, either." Tris put her spectacles straight. "That includes me, Dama. It included me yesterday, and last month, and in the spring when you had the ague and your fever broke overnight and the herbalist was so pleased with his tea."

The landlady stood absorbing that — the fever, the tea, the year of small unbilled mendings suddenly acquiring an author — and then, being at bottom a practical woman of the class Tris had been born to, she arrived where practical women arrive.

"Rent's the same," she said with finality. "But you'll take the good room at the front, because if the quality are coming to leave cards on a great mage, they're not doing it in my back hall. And the dragon makes me a dozen blue ones for the mantel, for my trouble."

"Done," said Tris gravely, and did not smile until the kitchen door had shut.

The walk up the hill was worse. Word had outrun her: students stopped on the colonnade to watch her pass, and a knot of them actually applauded, raggedly, until her look put it out. At the bakery, at least, the queue parted for her not out of fear but because Havrik's father — floury, reasonable, his ground floor dry and his flour two stories up — came around the counter, took her hand in both his enormous ones, and said nothing at all, which was the first thing all morning she could bear.

The chancellor's interview she had expected. Perin, she had not.

He was sitting outside her hostel room when she finally came down the hill, hugging his knees, and the look on his face hurt worse than the lightning-burn on her palms — not anger. Arithmetic. She watched him re-adding a year of shared meals and lecture notes and complaints about proctors, every sum coming out different now.

"You let me tell you about *storms*," he said. "I told you about Ostrey failing that girl for lighting a candle too fast. I explained *rain* to you once. In April. For twenty minutes."

"It was a very academic account of rain," Tris said. "I learned things. Mostly about you."

"Everyone will say I knew. I'm your *roommate's neighbor*. Do you know what this does to my — " and then his own engine caught up with him, and he was off, horrified and glowing: "You're the Kadasep mage, you're the — the empress's border, that was you, there are four of you and you're one of the *four* — I have eaten toast with — I have *corrected your theory citations* — "

"You were right about two of them," said Tris. "Even Niko misses citations." And watching him inflate, she understood that Perin, at least, was going to be fine; she had merely become the best story he would ever own.

Verusi was harder, because Verusi said nothing at all until evening, and then sat down across from her at the end table with her book and did not open it.

"Trader-blooded girl, unlucky to circle with," she said at last. "You sat down anyway. I thought — *there's one person here who doesn't care what I am*." A long pause. "And it turns out you cared less than I could have dreamed, because you outrank everything in this valley, so what was I to you? Charity? Cover?"

"You were the only person in that hall reading at dinner," Tris said. "I have three siblings by spell and by choice. One of them is a Trader smith who'd take your part in this argument, and I am *not* looking forward to her letter." She turned her teacup around. It was as close to pleading as her machinery went, and Verusi — who had her own machinery, of a similar make — studied her for a long moment and then, one notch, like a door, unlocked.

"You hid a medallion," she said, opening her book, "for a *year*, in a school of mages. I want the wards. Every one. Drawn out."

"Done," said Tris, weak with something she declined to name.

Havrik came to Dama Wilget's the next morning with a basket of morning buns and stood in the parlor turning his cap in his hands while Chime showed off shamelessly on the mantel.

"They're saying you're the greatest weather mage in the world," he said.

"They exaggerate. There's an old woman in the Cape of Grief who—"

"You knew," Havrik said. "About my ovens. The left side. You've known for months." He wasn't a quick boy, people said, and people were wrong; he was a thorough one, and he had spent the night being thorough. "You're — what you are. Great Circle mage, medallion, all of it. And *you* had to sneak in here under a false name to get treated fairly." His big hands stilled on the cap. "So what chance do I have?"

And there it was — the question under the whole year, asked in a boardinghouse parlor by a baker's son with flour still in his eyebrows.

"None," Tris said. "Alone, none at all. That's why you're coming with me to see the chancellor."

The chancellor of Lightsbridge was a small, round, terrifyingly pleasant woman named Master Edlin Coper, who had been in the quadrangle all that night and had spent it, unlike her colleagues, watching and revising. She received Tris in a study still smelling of wet books being dried three floors down, waved off the medallion when Tris moved to present it — "I saw its credentials at some length, dear, from the west window" — and came to terms with a speed that suggested she had drafted them before breakfast.

The university's position was delicate. A fraudulent matriculation had occurred; alternatively, a visiting master of historic power had spent a year conducting an unauthorized but frankly devastating field study of Lightsbridge admissions, and had then saved the city, the library, and four hundred years of institutional pride in a single night. The chancellor greatly preferred the second version, and was prepared to document it.

"You'll finish your license," she said, "under your own name — openly, and by our examinations, which I gather is the part that matters to you. Standard candidacy, no favors. Heaven help the proctor who offers you one."

"And the rest," said Tris. It was not a question.

The rest was Havrik, standing planted on the carpet like a door someone had left in the middle of the room; and behind him, invisible, a scrubby merchant girl on a Capchen dock whom four families had passed around like a bad coin because no academic test could see what she was.

"An assessment protocol for ambient magic," the chancellor said. "Winding Circle's methods, adapted; your Master Goldeye — the *real* one, Niklaren Goldeye — to consult, since I understand he makes rather a specialty of finding what examiners miss. A pilot program." She looked at Havrik over her spectacles. "Beginning, I'm told, with bread."

Which left only Ostrey.

He caught her in the colonnade outside — waiting, she realized, the way he'd wait to make a point after lecture — and for one heartbeat she saw him take in the medallion, now worn openly, and watched the whole apparatus of the man grind, brake, and swing hard onto a new track without ever once touching *sorry*.

"Chandler," he said warmly. "Well. You understand this changes nothing in my syllabus — and indeed, *indeed*, it proves the thesis. A year of procedure, and look what it made of even you\! Discipline harnessing raw talent — you'll be my finest demonstration that the academic method can polish *any*—"

"Master Ostrey." Tris looked up at him, and let him see her eyes, and somewhere over the eastern hills the spent storm turned in its sleep. "I spent ten years learning discipline from a man you couldn't examine on your best day, and one year in your lecture hall learning to do everything slower, worse, and in three unnecessary corners, so that people like you would call it magic. Your method didn't polish me. I *impersonated* it." She settled her shawl, merchant-respectable to the last. "But I'll be in lectures on Monday, because your diagrams are good and your manners are the worst I've met on two continents, and I can use the one and survive the other. My sister once told an empress no. You," she said, with terrible kindness, "are not even my worst professor. There was a woman in Tharios who threw a slate."

She left him in the colonnade, opening and shutting like a ward with no key, and went down the hill in the rain — light, ordinary, well-behaved rain, falling on a river valley out of debt — to write a letter she could finally send.

## **Epilogue**

*Lightsbridge University, 12 Barleymoon*

*Dear Sandry, Daja and Briar —*

*You will have heard. If you haven't, buy any broadsheet in Karang; they've drawn me with a heroic jaw I do not own, riding lightning like a horse, which is not how it works, as I have told three engravers now to no effect whatever.*

*The alias lasted eleven months, which is ten more than Briar wagered, so he owes Daja money. I mind less than I expected. The girl I invented was restful, but she couldn't send letters, and her friends didn't know her, and I find I've developed expensive habits in the way of being known. Blame yourselves.*

*I'm to finish the license under my own name. Four winds mages have transferred out of my practicum. The chancellor is starting an assessment for ambient students, and the first of them, you'll like this, is a baker who's been flunking theory all year while doing hearth-magic every morning that Frostpine would stop to watch. I sat in his ovens' warmth for a month, saying nothing, and it was the hardest thing this place asked of me. Niko is coming in the spring to consult, which means he knew how this would end, which means he let me do it anyway. I haven't decided whether to thank him or rattle his windows.*

*There is a girl here, Verusi, who reads at meals — Daja, half Trader, and worth ten of anyone who won't circle with her — and a boy, Perin, who talks the way rain falls, and it turns out that four years of saving fees bought me exactly what I ordered: a license underway, and people who chose me before they knew what I was. It's only that I had it backwards. I thought the license would make me ordinary. It won't. Nothing will. Some material — I have this on faculty authority — simply cannot be taught.*

*Chime is asleep on the windowsill. It's raining, because I let it.*

*Come and visit. I'll introduce you as ordinary merchants; you have four braids' worth of my word that it's wonderfully funny for as long as it lasts.*

*All my love —*

*Tris*

*— Trisana Chandler, Lightsbridge University, candidate for the mage's license, class of nobody's business but her own.*

---

*The End*

## **Plot Summary**

**Premise:** Tris arrives at Lightsbridge University under the alias "Trisana Amberlin," a merchant's niece from Capchen, to earn an academic mage's license — credentials that will let her work as an ordinary, unfrightening mage-for-hire instead of the weather witch people cross streets to avoid. The problem: she must pass as a novice with modest raw talent, when she is in fact one of the most powerful ambient mages alive, medallion and all.

**Chapter 1 — Matriculation (\~2,000 words).** Tris arrives in Lightsbridge, installs Chime with a discreet landlady, banks her lightning, and sits the entrance trial — where the hardest magic she works all day is doing *less*. She meets her hostel roommate, Perin fa Slyme, a chatty scholarship student, and makes an enemy of Master Ostrey, an elemental-theory lecturer who declares ambient magic "hedge-craft for midwives and hearth-tenders."

**Chapter 2 — The Art of Being Mediocre (\~1,800 words).** Classes begin. Tris discovers that pretending to fumble beginner charms is harder than mastering them; her control is *too* clean and a proctor notices. She befriends Verusi, a Trader-blooded student shunned by classmates, and Havrik, a baker's son whose "knack" with ovens Tris privately recognizes as unschooled ambient magic the university refuses to credit. Master Ostrey adopts her as his favorite example of "proper, teachable, academic talent" — excruciatingly.

**Chapter 3 — Weather Sense (\~1,800 words).** A drought settles over the river valley. Tris feels the sick, stalled weather like a toothache and must not touch it. A rival student, Anselm — ambitious, well-connected, suspicious of the too-composed Capchen girl — starts asking questions and writing letters. Ostrey publicly humiliates Havrik; Tris's temper nearly cracks her disguise. Quiet scenes: letters home to Sandry, Daja and Briar, none of which she can send unwarded.

**Chapter 4 — The Storm (\~2,000 words).** The drought breaks all at once: a monster storm line rolls down the valley toward a city of dry timber, book-crammed libraries, and a swollen millrace. Anselm confronts Tris with evidence — a sketch from a Tharios broadsheet of the mage who cleaned the Kadasep district's air. The storm hits mid-confrontation. Lightning finds the Great Library's copper roof.

**Chapter 5 — Trisana Chandler (\~2,200 words).** Tris stops hiding. She takes the storm — grounds the lightning through her own braids, walls the flood, breathes the fire out of the library stacks — in front of half the university. Fallout: Perin and Verusi wrestle with the lie; Havrik asks the question that matters ("If *you* had to sneak in here, what chance do I have?"). Ostrey attempts to claim her as proof of academic methods and is flattened in the best Tris tradition. The chancellor offers a quiet bargain: finish her license under her own name, and Lightsbridge will pilot an assessment for ambient students — starting with Havrik.

**Epilogue (\~400 words).** A letter actually sent: to Sandry, Daja and Briar, in her own name, with Chime asleep on the windowsill.
