# **Hermione Granger and the Inconvenient Sorting**

*by Emma*

# **Chapter One: The Hat Makes a Point**

Hermione Granger had prepared for the Sorting the way she prepared for everything: exhaustively, and with a backup plan.

She had read *Hogwarts: A History* twice — the second time to be thorough — and cross-referenced it with *Great Wizarding Events of the Twentieth Century*, three memoirs of dubious reliability, and a pamphlet titled ***So You're Magical: A Guide for the Recently Informed*** **\[+canon-cont: nice, very much in the style of the naming of things like this in canon\]** that the Ministry had posted to her parents along with her letter. She had concluded, **with the quiet confidence of a girl who had never once been wrong about a syllabus \[prose: I’ve never really associated correctness with syllabi with a particular level of confidence. Also, do elementary schoolers get syllabi?\]**, that she would be in Gryffindor. Dumbledore had been in Gryffindor. It was plainly the best one.

She had a second-choice house, obviously. Ravenclaw. One should always have a second choice.

She did not have a third choice, because having a third choice would imply the first two might fail, and that was not a contingency worth planning for.

**This, the Hat would later observe, was exactly the sort of thinking that got her into trouble. \[+prose: I like the foreshadowing here\]**

---

"Granger, Hermione\!"

She walked to the stool at a brisk, businesslike pace, which she had practiced, because walking slowly looked frightened and running looked deranged. She sat. **She had one glimpse of the Great Hall — a thousand candlelit faces, the Gryffindor table she'd already mentally moved into — and then the Hat dropped over her eyes and the world went dark and woolly.** **\[+prose: I like the pacing of this description\]**

*Well,* said a small voice in her ear. *Well, well, well.*

**"Gryffindor, please," said Hermione politely, because there was no reason the process couldn't be efficient \[+prose: nice, effective way to establish her personality in action\]**.

*Please?* The Hat sounded amused. *You've researched me.*

"Chapter four," she said. "Also the founders' biographies. **I know you take preferences into account. Harry Potter went to Gryffindor just now \[canon-cont: aside from P coming later in the alphabet than G, I don’t believe Harry specifically wanted Gryffindor, just “not Slytherin”, or that Hermione would know that\]**, and while that isn't a *factor*, statistically speaking the house does seem to attract—"

*Let's have a look, shall we,* said the Hat, **in the tone of a doctor who has stopped listening to the patient's self-diagnosis \[prose: this clause is redundant, everything it adds has already been conveyed by the dialogue\]**.

There was a sensation like someone flipping through a card catalogue at great speed. Hermione sat very straight and tried to project bravery. She thought about the time she'd told off a boy twice her size for stepping on a snail. She thought about coming to a magic school *alone*, with parents who still thought **"Diagon Alley" was a speech impediment \[canon-char: I feel like this is an attempt to reference her dentist parents, but why would they think Diagon Alley was a speech impediment?\]**. That was brave. **That was *demonstrably* brave. \[+char: I like this, feels like a realistic reach for bravery from a child\]** 

*Hm,* said the Hat. *You memorized the school bylaws.*

"In case of disputes."

***You've planned your O.W.L. subjects. You're eleven. \[+canon-char: this is totally Hermione\]***

"It's never too early—"

***You've planned your career. Two careers. There's a flowchart. \[+canon-char: this too\]***

"The second one is a fallback—"

*You researched the Sorting itself so you could influence the outcome.* The Hat's voice had gone rich with something that, **in a hat, could only be called delight \[prose: what would it be if it weren’t from a hat?\]**. *You walked in here with a preferred result, a contingency result, and a **prepared argument \[prose: there are a lot of items that could have gone here that would be stronger evidence for ambition\]**. My dear girl. Do you know what we call that?*

"Diligence," said Hermione firmly.

***Ambition,*** **said the Hat. \[+prose: nicely landed, and despite my earlier quibbles about word choice, this is overall a good argument for why the Hat might sort Hermione into Slytherin\]**

The word landed in her stomach like a swallowed ice cube. "No," she said. "No, I don't think you've understood. I want to *learn* things. That's Ravenclaw at worst—"

*Ravenclaws want to know things because **knowing is lovely \[canon-cont: I don’t think this is true, and the phrasing’s weird\]**.* The Hat rummaged, unhurried, somewhere behind her eyes. ***You want to know things because knowledge is useful. \[canon-char: nah, Hermione just likes to know things\]*** ***Because the girl who knows everything can never be caught out, or laughed at, or left behind. \[canon-cont: I would argue that Hermione had plenty of experience with the fact that the girl who knows everything can be laughed at\]** **You don't love the library, Hermione Granger. You love what the library makes you. \[prose: this is so overdramatic lmao\]***

**"That is a gross mischaracterization," said Hermione, "of a perfectly healthy relationship with reference material." \[+prose: I do really enjoy the snappiness of this reply after the Hat’s melodrama, made me laugh, and that makes me willing to accept this characterization of Hermione\]**

*You want to be the greatest witch of your age.*

"Everyone wants—"

*They really don't,* said the Hat. ***Most children under this brim want lunch, and for their new shoes to stop pinching. You sat down and opened negotiations. \[canon-cont: as known in canon and referenced earlier, Harry also had a specific preference. It seems really unlikely that Hermione is unique in her opening. Given the people we encounter in canon, it also seems unlikely that other eleven-year-olds haven’t wanted to be the greatest witch or wizard of their age\]***

Out in the hall, this was the point at which people began to murmur. Hermione couldn't see them, but she could hear it: the shuffling, the whispers, someone at a staff table pouring a second goblet of something. She was dimly aware she had been under the Hat for a very long time. The record — **she had *read about the record* \[prose: of course she has, you don’t need to spend words establishing it\]** — was four minutes.

"Fine," she said, dropping her voice, as though the Hat and she were the only two parties at the table, **which in a sense they were \[prose: if two people are the only parties at a table, why would they need to lower their voices?\]**. "Fine. Suppose you're right. Suppose I'm ambitious. Ambitious people do very well in Gryffindor. Look at—"

*You'd wither there,* said the Hat, and its voice was suddenly not amused at all; it was old, and it was kind, which was worse. *They'd love you for your courage and roll their eyes at your corrections. You'd spend seven years shouting the right answer* ***into a house that finds the right answer charming \[canon-cont: this is a weird characterization of Gryffindor; it’s also not consistent with the earlier note that they would roll their eyes at corrections\].*** *I've done this for a thousand years, girl. I know where a mind like yours sharpens and where it rusts.*

**"And you think it sharpens in the house that won't *want* me?" The words came out before she could file them under Things We Don't Admit. \[canon-char: this is a lot of emotion when at this point she’s barely met wizards and is mostly excited for everything; canon eleven-year-old Hermione trusts the system\]** "I know what Slytherin is. I read about that too. The blood prejudice. The— there are people at that table whose parents think I stole my magic. You'd send me down there alone because I make *flowcharts*?"

The Hat was quiet for a moment.

*It will be hard,* it agreed. *Harder than you know, and you already suspect quite a lot. But you asked the wrong question. You asked where you'd be wanted. The interesting question is where* ***you'll be necessary. \[prose: this is a nit, but fascinating that it used “necessary” rather than “needed”, which is more symmetric\]***

"Those are not the only two options—"

*Slytherin house has been eating itself for a generation,* said the Hat, softly now, **quickly now, the way one speaks when a decision has already been made and the rest is mercy \[prose: this is entirely unnecessary to spell out\]**. *It has forgotten that **cunning was ever meant to build anything. \[prose: what?\]** And here you are: proof walking. A Muggle-born with more pure Slytherin in her than the lot of them. They'll hate it. Then they'll owe you. Then — well.* A pause. ***Then it gets interesting. \[cont: we’re at the end of the conversation, and the Hat hasn’t really said anything that could tie back to the observation it was foreshadowed to make about her lack of a third contingency plan earlier\]***

**"I want to register a formal objection," said Hermione. \[+char: I really am enjoying Hermione’s lines\]**

***Noted,*** **said the Hat, *and overruled —* "SLYTHERIN\!" \[+prose: nice use of punctuation/formatting\]**

---

The silence was total.

Later, the silence would become famous. **Hufflepuffs who had been examining their forks would claim to have seen the whole thing \[cont: given that the main things to notice are auditory, this seems like a reasonable claim\]**. But in the moment it was simply the loudest quiet Hermione had ever stood inside. Eleven minutes she'd been under the Hat — **a school record, she'd learn \[cont: from earlier, we know that she would have immediately known that eleven minutes was a new record\]**, breaking one held since 1879 — and the hall had been coiled tight waiting for the punchline, **and *Slytherin* was apparently not it \[prose: yes, obviously\]**.

She took the Hat off and set it neatly on the stool. **Her hands were steady. That took everything she had, so her face was on its own, and she suspected it had gone the color of a fire alarm. \[+prose: I like the way this intersperses characterization and description\]**

Professor McGonagall was staring at her with the expression of a woman rerunning a calculation. At the Gryffindor table, the boy with the scar — Harry Potter, **sorted twenty minutes prior \[canon-cont: again, Potter would have come after Granger\]** — was watching her with open confusion. **Beside him, a redheaded boy whispered something \[canon-cont: Ron Weasley definitely shouldn’t have been sorted yet.\]**. She was too far away to hear it. She would spend an unreasonable number of the next several years not-wondering what it was.

The Slytherin table did not applaud.

To be precise — and Hermione was always precise — one person at the Slytherin table began to applaud: a tall seventh-year girl near the end, three slow claps, before a prefect's glare shut it down. Everyone else simply watched her come, two hundred feet of polished silence, **with expressions ranging from affront to arithmetic \[prose: properly, these should use a participle form. “Arithmetic” maps most naturally to “calculating”, which is a repeat of the description for McGonagall\]**.

She chose the **arithmetic end of the table \[cont: it hasn’t been established that there’s a side of the table where most people have “arithmetic” expressions\]**.

There was an open seat beside a girl with a brown bob who was watching the whole spectacle with her chin in her hand, as if it were a moderately good **Quidditch match \[canon-cont: doesn’t make sense for Hermione’s internal narration to use Quidditch analogies at this point\]**. Hermione sat. Across the table, a blond boy she recognized from Madam Malkin's — **he'd talked at her for four minutes about broom regulations without asking her name \[canon-char: that’s so unlikely, Draco always wants to know people’s last names\]** — leaned forward.

"There's been a mistake," he announced.

**"I agree," said Hermione. "I argued Gryffindor for eleven minutes." \[+prose: I continue to enjoy Hermione’s retorts\]**

This was apparently not the answer he'd prepared for, because he blinked and had to start over. "You're Muggle-born."

"And you're observant. They'll be thrilled about you in Magical Law Enforcement." She unfolded her napkin. Her heart was going like a rabbit's, but she had discovered something in the last ninety seconds, a thing that would quietly reorganize her entire life: ***fear and fury feel identical, and nobody watching can tell which one you're running on \[prose: aside from my skepticism about whether this is true, it doesn’t seem like the people around her are responding as if she were furious\]**.*

"My father will hear about this," said the blond boy.

"Is he on the Board of Governors?" Hermione asked. "Because the Hat's authority in Sorting disputes is absolute — *Hogwarts: A History*, page 214, and it's reaffirmed in the school bylaws, section nine. There's no appeals process. **I checked twice. While arguing. \[cont: I enjoy this line, but she only tried to register an objection\]** If your father would like to hear about something, he can hear about that."

Somewhere down the table, someone snorted into their pumpkin juice.

The girl with the brown bob **turned her chin-in-hand posture \[prose: I wouldn’t usually say you turn your posture\]** toward Hermione, examined her for a moment, and said: "Tracey Davis. Half-blood. They only hate me *half* the time; you're going to be a nice change of pace for me."

"Hermione Granger."

"Yes," said Tracey, "**we all watched the hat yell at you for a fortnight \[+char: since Tracey’s being set up to be Hermione’s main friend, I like that she’s able to retort back to Hermione\].** Pass the potatoes?"

Hermione passed the potatoes. It was, though neither of them knew it yet, the founding transaction of what the *Daily Prophet* **would one day call "the most quietly terrifying alliance in modern magical Britain." \[exec: this foreshadowing feels unnecessary, it doesn’t add much\]**

At the staff table, Professor Snape had not moved for some time. He was gazing at the new addition to his house the way a man gazes at a ticking parcel that has been delivered to his home address by mistake, and **yet which cannot, according to page 214 and section nine, be returned \[prose: I’m pretty sure if I had a ticking parcel, I wouldn’t try to return it. The callback to the section reference also doesn’t land for me; it wasn’t that important\]**.

The Slytherin common room was in the dungeons, under the lake, and Hermione's first professional opinion was that it was a criminally poor environment for books. The damp alone. **She would need to look into preservation charms immediately \[canon-cont: this surprises me from eleven-year-old Hermione, I wouldn’t expect even her to be so confident that she would need preservation charms with no magical education\]**.

Her second professional opinion was that it was beautiful — **long and low, all green-black stone and silver lamps \[+prose: I’m a sucker for this style of description\]**, windows that looked out into deep lakewater where things occasionally moved. Grand. Cold. **Designed by someone who wanted visitors to feel they had been granted an audience \[prose: there’s got to be a better way to express this sentiment\].**

The prefect — Gemma Farley, fifth year, **delivered the welcome speech with the tone of a woman who'd given it before but never to *this* \[prose: a bit out of place, it’s not part of the description of the prefect. It’s also very on the nose\]** — concluded her remarks with: "Whatever happens inside this house, stays inside this house. We defend each other in public. That is the *first rule*."

"And in private?" Hermione asked.

Gemma looked at her for a long moment. "In private," she said, "I'd sleep with your wand under your pillow, Granger."

"I'd planned to anyway," said Hermione. "Thank you for **confirming the threat model \[prose: nah absolutely not with this software engineer shit\]**."

**Nobody laughed \[prose: I can’t blame them\]**. **But nobody laughed at *anything* in Slytherin \[cont: someone snorts literally twelve paragraphs earlier\]**, she was beginning to gather; **the local currency was the almost-imperceptible pause \[prose: why would it be the local currency?\]**, and she'd just collected several. **She catalogued that too \[char: I really dislike this whole interaction between Hermione and Gemma, Hermione is being really combative to someone who is supporting her\]**.

Hermione lay in the green dark under the lake and **did not cry, on a technicality \[prose: I like the stubborn pretending she’s fine, but this misses an important alternative moment to let us feel her emotion\]**: she had decided that anything under ninety seconds constituted "eyes watering" and was attributable to the dungeon damp.

**REASONS THIS IS ACTUALLY FINE**

*1\. The Hat has a thousand years of data and no incentive to lie. (Check whether hats can lie.)*

*2\. Slytherin has produced more Ministers for Magic than any other house. It's in the pamphlet. Nobody hates the pamphlet.*

# **Chapter Two: Terms and Conditions**

On her second morning as a Slytherin, Hermione woke to find her toothbrush had been transfigured into a slug.

It was, she noted with the detachment of a scientist observing a rival's shoddy methodology, a poor transfiguration. **The bristles were still visible along the slug's back, which meant partial conversion only, which meant a first-year had done it \[canon-cont: first-years on the second day of school usually can’t transfigure at all\],** which narrowed the suspect pool to four, which — given that Millicent couldn't transfigure a sneeze, Daphne wouldn't bother, and Tracey had spent breakfast yesterday loudly declaring toothbrush-tampering "derivative" — meant Pansy.

Hermione picked the slug up, carried it to the window slit that vented into the lake shallows, and released it with dignity. Then she brushed her teeth with her backup toothbrush, because of course she had a backup toothbrush, and wrote in her notebook:

*Day 2\. Toothbrush. Partial transfiguration, amateur wandwork. No response warranted. **A response is what she's purchasing with my toothbrush. Refuse the sale. \[prose: in addition to being an awkward use of the passive voice, this is so direct\]***

This philosophy lasted exactly four days, which by dungeon standards was heroic.

---

The first week established the rhythms of her new life with the efficiency of a well-run detention.

In Charms and Herbology, the other houses treated her like a museum exhibit: *Muggle-born Slytherin, believed extinct, do not tap glass.* Hufflepuffs gave her seats a wide, apologetic berth. Ravenclaws watched her the way one **watches a chess problem \[prose: does one watch a chess problem?\]**. The Gryffindors were worse, because the Gryffindors had *decided* things about her. She heard a sandy-haired boy — Finnigan — explain to Harry Potter, in the loud whisper of the profoundly unsubtle, that the Hat had probably "smelled the dark magic on her." Potter, to his credit, looked unconvinced. Weasley, not to his, did not.

She raised her hand in every class anyway. She'd made a decision on night one, somewhere between items four and five of the list: they could have her house, her table, and apparently her toothbrush, but the record would show — permanently, in every professor's gradebook — exactly who knew the material.

In Transfiguration, McGonagall called on her four times, got four flawless answers, and awarded Slytherin ten points with the facial expression of a woman performing dental surgery on herself. The Slytherin first-years, who had planned to freeze Hermione out completely, encountered their first structural problem: she kept *winning them points*. Malfoy tried sneering "we don't need points from a Mudblood," and Gemma Farley — who tracked the house standings the way brokers track markets — informed him without looking up that points had no blood status, and neither did the House Cup.

Hermione wrote in the notebook: *The house has one god and it is the hourglass. Useful.*

---

Then there was Potions.

Double Potions with Gryffindor came on Friday, in a dungeon classroom two doors from the common room, which meant Hermione arrived first, sat front and center, and had her ingredients laid out in alphabetized rows before anyone else was through the door.

Professor Snape swept in, delivered a speech about bottled fame and brewed glory that she transcribed in shorthand for later analysis, and then began interrogating Harry Potter on material from page fifteen of *Magical Drafts and Potions*, which Potter plainly had not read.

Hermione's hand went up. It was involuntary, like a knee reflex.

Snape ignored it with such totality that the ignoring became its own presence in the room, a fourth wall she was on the wrong side of. He asked Potter about bezoars. Her hand strained higher. He asked about monkshood and wolfsbane. She was nearly out of her seat.

"Sit *down*," said Snape, without turning his head.

She sat. She seethed. Then — and she would later mark this as the first genuinely Slytherin act of her academic career — she stopped raising her hand, took out a spare bit of parchment, and simply wrote the answers in large, tidy letters, angling the page with the serene innocence of someone merely reviewing her notes: **A BEZOAR IS A STONE FROM THE STOMACH OF A GOAT.** **THEY ARE THE SAME PLANT. IT IS ALSO CALLED ACONITE.**

Potter, squinting past Weasley's shoulder, read them off almost correctly.

Snape's black eyes traveled from Potter, to the parchment, to Hermione, who gazed back with the expression of a girl who had violated no rule that appeared in any published document. She watched him consider making it a rule. She watched him calculate what it would cost to publicly acknowledge that the new Muggle-born had just fed answers to Harry Potter under his nose, in his own dungeon, **using only stationery \[prose: this whole thing is so dramatic for just showing Harry answers\]**.

"Five points from Gryffindor," Snape said at last, "for Potter's *wandering eyes*," and moved on.

It was not approval. It was something rarer: it was *accounting*. He had chosen the version of events that cost him nothing, and they both knew it, and Hermione **understood with sudden clarity \[prose: redundant to say both “understood” and “with sudden clarity”\]** that she had far more to learn in this dungeon than potions.

Her cauldron work that afternoon was, even by her own exacting standards, perfect. Snape stalked the rows praising Malfoy's mediocre stewing of horned slugs, paused at her flawless Cure for Boils — correct color, correct consistency, faint silver steam curling in the regulation spiral — and said nothing at all for the length of three full seconds.

Tracey, at the next cauldron, whispered: "That's the nicest thing I've ever seen him do."

---

The toothbrush campaign escalated on Thursday of week two, when Hermione returned from the library to find her copy of *Hogwarts: A History* — the good one, the annotated one — sodden in the bottom of the lake-window alcove, pages bloated, ink running like mascara.

She stood very still for a while.

Tracey found her there and looked from the book to Hermione's face and said, "Right, so, normally I stay out of house politics on account of politics being for people with energy, but I feel you should know it wasn't Pansy this time. Pansy's escalation ladder goes toothbrush, hairbrush, *then* books. This skipped rungs. This was the second-years. Flint's little cousin and that lot. They had a meeting about you."

"A meeting."

"In the boys' corridor. Very solemn. Item one: the Mudblood problem." Tracey said the word with the flat distaste of someone handling a dead mouse by the tail. "Item two was Quidditch trials, if it helps."

"It doesn't." Hermione picked up the corpse of her book. Somewhere under her ribs, in the place where *that word* had lodged on the first night, something was quietly rearranging itself — grief moving out, architecture moving in. "Tracey. Hypothetically. If someone wanted to know everything about everyone in this house — allegiances, debts, feuds, who's frightened of their parents, who's behind on homework — how long do you think it would take?"

Tracey considered. "For a normal person? Years. It's all in the walls down here, but nobody writes it down."

"Nobody writes it down," Hermione repeated, in the tone Newton probably used about the apple.

"...**I've said something load-bearing \[prose: not the “load bearing”\]**, haven't I," said Tracey.

That night, Hermione began a second notebook. The first notebook was *Reasons This Is Actually Fine*, and it was for surviving. The second had no title at all, because titles were evidence, and it was for *winning*. Its opening pages were modest: seating patterns at meals, who copied whose Charms homework, the fact that Millicent Bulstrode had received a Howler from her mother about her marks and had looked, for one unguarded moment, like she might cry into her porridge.

Modest. Seeds usually are.

---

The seed sprouted the following Tuesday, when Millicent Bulstrode's potion attempted to leave its cauldron.

They were meant to be brewing a simple herbicide. Millicent, whose relationship with sequential instructions was adversarial, had added the flobberworm mucus before the nettles, and the result was climbing over the rim with slow, gelatinous ambition while Millicent stared at it in dawning horror and Snape's footsteps grew nearer along the far row.

Hermione ran the calculation in about a second and a half. Cost: two ounces of powdered gingko, thirty seconds of stirring, and being seen helping. **Benefit: unknown, long-dated, and — this was the interesting part — *compounding*. \[prose: why would it be compounding? This is said like it’s self-evident from the situation\]**

She leaned over. "Gingko. Now. Three counterclockwise stirs, then take it off the flame and put it back on. It'll read as deliberate — that's the Hobart variation, it's in the appendix nobody reads."

Millicent, out of options, obeyed. The potion sulked back into its cauldron and turned the correct shade of muddy green four seconds before Snape's shadow fell across it. He inspected it. He inspected Millicent, **who had never produced an acceptable potion in her life \[cont: hasn’t it been, like, three weeks?\]**. His gaze slid — briefly, involuntarily — to Hermione, who was industriously stirring her own cauldron and had never looked more innocent of anything.

"Adequate, Bulstrode," said Snape, and moved on.

Millicent sat in stunned silence for the rest of the lesson. In the corridor afterward, she planted herself in front of Hermione like a wardrobe with a grudge, worked her jaw through several practice starts, and finally produced: "Why."

"Because you needed it," said Hermione. "And because someday, probably at the least convenient moment imaginable, I'll need something. And you'll remember today. That's all. No trap. No trick. It's just" — she reached for a word the dungeon would respect — "a *balance*."

Millicent thought about this for a long moment. Slytherins, Hermione was learning, were never insulted by being offered a transaction. It was the one language everyone down here was raised speaking.

"The book was Harper," Millicent said abruptly. "Second-year. Threw it in the alcove himself, bragged at dinner." A pause. "He keeps sneakoscopes he hasn't paid for under his mattress. Owes Borgin's eleven galleons. His mum doesn't know."

And she walked off to lunch, debt discharged, leaving Hermione standing in the corridor holding the exact shape and weight of her future in her hands.

She wrote it in the untitled notebook that night — *Harper, R. Debt: Borgin & Burkes, 11G, concealed from family* — and beneath it, a general principle, underlined twice:

*In this house, information is currency, favors are banking, and I am about to be the only person down here who understands compound interest.*

Then she opened the first notebook, because rituals matter, and added:

*7\. My toothbrush has a backup. So do I, now. Her name is Millicent, which nobody will see coming, least of all Millicent.*

Somewhere above, the lake pressed its vast green weight against the windows, and for the first time since the Hat's verdict, the sound of it was almost companionable.

# **Chapter Three: The Favor Economy**

By Halloween, Hermione had catalogued forty-one distinct social debts within Slytherin house, repaid three of her own, extended eleven lines of homework credit, and been called *that word* fourteen times, each instance logged with date, speaker, and witnesses in the untitled notebook, under a column headed — with what she felt was admirable restraint — *Pending*.

She had also not made a single actual friend, unless one counted Tracey, and Tracey resisted the term.

"Friendship implies effort," Tracey explained, upside down off the end of her bed, watching Hermione color-code a revision timetable in October. "What we have is more of a mutually beneficial non-aggression pact with snacks."

"That's what friendship is."

"Take it back."

The isolation had a shape to it now, a daily architecture. Slytherins spoke to her when they needed something — increasingly often — and fell silent when she sat down. The other houses had settled into their positions: Hufflepuff pitied her, Ravenclaw had entered her in some kind of internal betting pool, and Gryffindor had constructed an entire mythology. The current version, Tracey reported, was that Hermione had *asked* for Slytherin. That the Hat had begged her to reconsider. Somewhere in the retelling, the eleven-minute argument had inverted itself completely.

The mythology had a high priest, and it was Ron Weasley, and on Halloween afternoon, after Charms, Hermione finally heard him deliver the sermon in person.

She hadn't meant to. She'd stayed behind to ask Flitwick about wand-weight differentials in the Levitation Charm — she'd managed it first in class, obviously, feather up on the initial attempt while Weasley behind her was still pronouncing the incantation like a sneeze — and she came around the corner into the courtyard just in time to hear him, holding court:

"—of course she got it first try, it's *Slytherin*, they're all half dark already. My dad says there wasn't a witch or wizard who went bad who wasn't in Slytherin. And she's not even — I mean, a *Muggle-born* in there? The Hat doesn't make mistakes, does it. It saw something. Everyone knows it saw something. That's why nobody sits with her. Even the snakes won't touch her. She's got no friends in her own house, no friends anywhere—"

The courtyard had the particular acoustics of every courtyard in every school in the world, designed by some malicious architect so that the target always hears.

Hermione did not stop walking. She had trained for this; she had a whole page on it — *Responses to Provocation, Ranked by Cost* — and the correct response to Weasley was priced at zero, which meant silence, which meant chin level and pace steady past the entire suddenly-quiet knot of Gryffindors, past Harry Potter, whose face was doing something complicated, and onward down the corridor with her spine straight as a wand.

She made it to the second-floor girls' bathroom before the ninety-second technicality collapsed entirely.

It wasn't Weasley, she told herself furiously, locked in the end cubicle through the afternoon, through the dinner she couldn't face — the Halloween feast, floating pumpkins, a thousand people who'd sorted her into *evil* faster than the Hat had sorted her into anything. It wasn't Weasley, who was an idiot of no consequence. It was the arithmetic. *No friends in her own house, no friends anywhere.* She had run the numbers herself often enough; she simply hadn't heard them read aloud before, as a proof, to an audience, to *applause*.

The bathroom door banged open at half past seven.

"There you are," said Pansy Parkinson's voice, with profound irritation. "Farley's doing a headcount at the feast, you're making the house look — why does it smell like a *bin* in—"

Which is when the troll came in.

---

Afterward, in her formal (private, notebook-only) incident report, Hermione recorded the sequence as follows.

Seconds one through three: Pansy Parkinson screamed at a pitch that, in fairness, most trained sopranos could not reach, and achieved nothing else.

Seconds four through six: the troll — twelve feet, gray, an odor that made the eyes water at a cellular level, dragging a club the size of a rowboat — demolished the first three sinks with the absent-minded thoroughness of bad weather.

Seconds seven through ten: Hermione's brain, which had spent two months filing everything it touched, retrieved three facts in rapid succession. Fact: mountain trolls have skulls four inches thick and a magical resistance that made direct spellwork pointless — *Fantastic Beasts*, page 41\. Fact: they are stupid enough to be led. Fact: the heaviest object in the room was the one in the troll's own hand.

"Parkinson," she said, dragging Pansy down behind the cubicle wall as the club took out the mirror. "When I say now, throw a tap handle at the far wall."

"*What?*"

"Trolls track noise, it's why they're drawn to plumbing, THROW IT—"

Pansy — and Hermione would give her this forever after — threw it. Brass rang off stone in the far corner; the troll wheeled toward the sound with its whole ponderous tonnage; and Hermione stepped out from cover, pointed her wand at the club as the troll raised it, and said, in the crisp Received Pronunciation of a girl who had done this once with a feather and saw no reason a larger object should behave differently:

"*Wingardium Leviosa.*"

The club stopped at the top of its arc. The troll pulled. The club declined. The troll looked up at its own hand with an expression of dim philosophical crisis — and Hermione let the charm go.

The crack of impact brought dust off the ceiling. The troll swayed, took one thoughtful step to nowhere, and came down like a condemned building, half through the cubicle partitions, out cold.

Silence, except for water jetting from three broken sinks.

"You," said Pansy Parkinson, from the floor, in a voice stripped of every affectation she'd worn since September the first, "levitated. The club."

"It's a first-year charm," said Hermione. Her hands had begun shaking now that it was over, so she folded her arms to hold them still. "Swish and flick. Honestly, if Weasley can nearly do it—"

The door burst open: McGonagall, Snape, Quirrell trembling in the rear. What followed was the standard adult aria — horror, relief, *what were you thinking*, the troll snoring through all of it — and then McGonagall's inevitable, dangerous question: what, precisely, two first-year girls had been doing in a bathroom during a school-wide troll emergency instead of at the feast where they'd been counted absent.

And Hermione discovered her second Slytherin instinct, the one that lived a layer below the notebooks.

The truth was available. The truth was: *I was crying in a toilet because a Gryffindor said out loud what everyone thinks, and Parkinson was sent to fetch me, and if you want to know why I was crying you can ask your own house, Professor.* The truth would cost Pansy nothing and Hermione everything — it would make the story of Halloween *the friendless Mudblood weeping in the loo*, forever, instead of what had actually just happened here in front of witnesses.

"My fault entirely, Professor," said Hermione, with the exact calibrated contrition of a model student confessing a first offense. "I read about mountain trolls over the summer and I thought I could handle it. I came looking for it. Parkinson followed to stop me — it was extremely brave of her — and if she hadn't created the diversion at the critical moment, I expect we'd both be dead."

Three things happened at once. McGonagall's face cycled through disbelief toward reluctant arithmetic. Pansy Parkinson's head turned with the slow, greased horror of someone watching a stranger repay a debt they hadn't invoiced. And Snape — who had looked from the levitated-club-shaped dent in the floor, to the wand still in Hermione's hand, to her face, and who alone in the room could evidently read the entire transaction like sheet music — made a sound that might, in a different man, have been the distant ancestor of a laugh.

"Five points from Slytherin for outrageous recklessness," he said. "And ten points to Slytherin. Apiece. For sheer dumb luck," he added, in a tone which specified that luck had had nothing to do with it, that he knew it, and that she should know he knew it.

Net plus fifteen. The house god was fed.

---

The dungeon was waiting up when they got back, because dungeon news traveled faster than owls, and the version that had arrived ahead of them was already luridly wrong in Hermione's favor. She let it stand. Editing your own legend downward, she was beginning to understand, was a Gryffindor hobby.

Pansy stopped her in the dormitory doorway, arms crossed, looking at Hermione the way you look at a chessboard that has just done something the rules should not permit.

"I've been vile to you," Pansy said. It wasn't an apology. It was an audit.

"Systematically," Hermione agreed.

"You could have told McGonagall the truth. It would have been" — Pansy searched for the term — "*devastating*, actually, in the right hands, and don't pretend you don't know it, because apparently you're the only person in first year who knows anything." A pause. The audit reached its bottom line. "What do you want?"

And there it was — the question the whole house had been circling since September, arriving at last through the unlikeliest courier. Not *who are you* or *how dare you* but *what do you want*: the door in the wall, the opening of formal relations.

Hermione considered her answer for a long moment, because she would only get to set the exchange rate once.

"Right now? Nothing," she said. "The rate's better if I let it mature. But you can tell people to stop pretending I don't exist, because it's inefficient — half this house owes me homework help already and they have to arrange it through Millicent like a *séance*. And Parkinson —" she paused at her bed, and allowed herself, for the first time in two months, the very smallest edge of a smile — "the next time there's an emergency? Congratulations on your throwing arm. That part I said to McGonagall was true."

That night, the untitled notebook received its first entry in an entirely new section, headed *Assets*.

And the other notebook, the surviving one, got:

*12\. There are things you cannot get from any library. Apparently one of them weighs twelve stone, screams in B-flat, and owes me her life.*

*13\. He said I have no friends anywhere. Noted, Weasley. Filed. Pending.*

# **Chapter Four: Twelve Uses of an Enemy**

The problem with Draco Malfoy, Hermione decided over the winter, was that he was the only person in Slytherin house who could not be balanced.

Everyone else fit the ledger. Millicent was an asset. Pansy had migrated from *Pending* to something like a wary trading partner, and had privately conceded that Hermione's hair "could be fixed, theoretically, with enough product and a priest." Daphne Greengrass had begun sitting near Hermione at meals — never *with*, always *near*, maintaining the plausible deniability of a girl whose parents read the society pages. Even the second-year Harper had gone abruptly, mysteriously polite in November, following an anonymous note that consisted, in its entirety, of the figure *11G* and a small drawing of a sneakoscope.

But Malfoy ran at a permanent loss. He didn't want anything she could supply, because the thing he wanted was a world in which she didn't exist, and **Hermione had checked every catalogue and that item was not in stock \[prose: this is so melodramatic\]**.

So he escalated. It was toothbrushes at first, spiritually speaking — hexes in corridors that never quite landed, ingredients that migrated during Potions, the word, always the word, deployed now less like a knife and more like punctuation. **Through the winter she absorbed it, logged it, priced it at zero. \[cont: it sounds like she would have recorded it as pending\]**

Then in February he found the letter.

Her mother wrote weekly — cheerful Muggle envelopes with cheerful Muggle stamps, full of dental news and cautious questions about whether the other children were being nice, questions Hermione answered with the strategic fiction that was becoming her second language. Malfoy intercepted one at breakfast, and read it aloud to the table.

He did the voice. That was the thing she filed most carefully afterward: he did a *voice* for her mother — high, dim, bleating — and read out the part about how proud Mum and Dad were, how they'd put her letter about the levitation charm up in the surgery for the patients to see, and the Slytherin table laughed, not all of it, but enough of it, and Hermione sat and watched him wave her mother's handwriting around like a dead thing he'd found, and understood that the zero-cost policy had just been repealed.

"Give it back," she said, once.

"Or what?" said Draco Malfoy, eleven years old, heir to everything, safe as houses. "You'll write a *report*?"

"No," said Hermione, and took her seat, and ate her breakfast, and that was somehow the worst thing anyone at the table had seen all year, because by February they knew her, a little. Tracey put down her fork and said, "Oh, you absolute *idiot*, Malfoy," in a tone of scientific pity.

---

She did not hex him. Hexing was a confession with a wand. Instead, over the following three weeks, with the patience of a very small glacier, Hermione Granger conducted an audit.

She was uniquely positioned for it. She had spent six months as the house's homework bank, which meant six months of every first- and second-year's essays passing across her desk for "checking" — and one did notice things, checking. She noticed that Malfoy's Potions essays were competent and his Charms essays were not, and that the Charms essays were in two alternating hands, one of which belonged to a second-year named Wilkes who owed her for the entire month of January. She noticed, from Millicent — who had shifted from asset to something dangerously close to henchwoman and had opinions about Malfoy dating to their toddler years — that the famous Nimbus Two Thousand and One deposit Draco kept bragging about did not exist, that his father had in fact refused it after the Christmas marks came home. She noticed from Pansy, who noticed everything about Draco with a diligence Hermione found frankly clinical, that he wrote to his mother twice a week and told everyone it was once a month.

None of this was a weapon. That was what the house never understood, before her. Each fact was a coin; the weapon was the *account*.

She chose Potions for the venue because Snape's dungeon was the one room in the castle where Malfoy believed himself invincible, and beliefs, like potions, are best corrected at the source.

They were brewing the Wiggenweld draft, in March, when Malfoy — bored, coasting on Snape's indulgence — flicked a shrivelfig stem into her cauldron at the critical simmer.

Or rather, into the cauldron where her Wiggenweld had been until ninety seconds earlier, when Hermione — who had watched him palm the stem two minutes before, because she now watched his hands on principle — had quietly swapped her cauldron's position with the spare, and was brewing on at the next station while the sabotaged decoy turned a spectacular arterial crimson and began to whistle.

"*Sir,*" said Hermione, hand up, voice ringing with civic concern. "Something's wrong with the spare cauldron. It looks like shrivelfig contamination — post-simmer introduction, you can tell from the color cascade, it's exactly like the case study in *Moste Potente Potions*, the 1872 poisoning trials, where they proved deliberate interference because shrivelfig can't enter a Wiggenweld accidentally, it's not on the ingredient list."

The dungeon went very quiet. Snape crossed to the whistling cauldron and looked into it for a long moment. Post-simmer shrivelfig introduction. Not on the ingredient list. Both true, both checkable, both now spoken aloud in front of thirty witnesses by the girl whose station adjoined it, whose own potion sat two feet away in flawless leaf-green mockery of the whole enterprise.

He did not look at Malfoy. That was how she knew he knew.

"Precisely so, Miss Granger," said Snape at last, in a voice like a door closing on someone's fingers very slowly. "Ten points to Slytherin for textbook diagnosis. The house is fortunate its points cannot be *contaminated* by carelessness." The final word was delivered to the room at large and landed on Malfoy alone.

And then it happened. As Snape turned from the crimson cauldron, his gaze crossed Hermione's — she was already back at her stirring, radiating the industrial innocence she had been perfecting since September — and his left eyebrow rose. Perhaps a quarter of an inch. Perhaps less. It was there and gone in the time it took her heart to do one strange, gratified thump.

Tracey, who missed nothing, leaned over afterward. "Did Snape just—"

"Yes."

"That's the eyebrow. My mum told me about the eyebrow. She says he did it twice in her whole seven years and one of those was for a boy who later invented a potion." Tracey looked at Hermione with something approaching awe. "You've been *eyebrowed*."

---

Malfoy came for her in the corridor that evening, alone, which was itself informational — he'd stopped wanting witnesses. He was white with a rage that made him, for once, look like a person rather than a rehearsal.

"I know that was you."

"That's a serious accusation," said Hermione. "What was me?"

"You switched the — you *knew* — " He mastered himself. "You think you're clever. My father—"

"—will hear about it, yes, I have the pamphlet." Hermione stopped walking and turned to face him, and something in her stance made him take a half-step back, which she filed. "Malfoy. I want to explain something to you, once, as a courtesy, because we're going to live in the same dungeon for seven years and I believe in clear terms.

"You read my mother's letter to the table. I've done nothing about that, you'll notice. Not because I can't. Because everything I know about everyone accrues *interest*, and I am in no hurry whatsoever. I know things about your Charms essays. I know things about your broom deposit. I know things about your letters home — no, don't tell me what your father will hear about, Malfoy, I know precisely how often your father hears about things, and from whom, and it's your *mother* you write to, which is, incidentally, the only thing I've learned about you all year that I like."

His mouth opened. Nothing emerged.

"Here are the terms," said Hermione. "You may hate me. You may sneer, posture, and vote against me in whatever committees this house convenes in the boys' corridor. That's all free. But my family is off the table, my cauldron is off the table, and if you ever do the *voice* again, I will stop letting the interest accrue and call the whole balance due at the worst possible moment of your life. I won't tell you when. That's rather the point of me."

She resumed walking. Behind her, Draco Malfoy stood in the torchlight, opening and shutting his mouth, experiencing — though he lacked the vocabulary for it — the precise sensation the troll had felt when the club stopped mid-swing.

---

The rest of the year happened mostly to other people, which suited her.

Something was afoot with the third-floor corridor; she'd deduced Fluffy (Hagrid, bless him, could not keep a secret in a bucket), traced the Flamel connection in an afternoon that Potter and Weasley, by rumor, had spent *weeks* on, and concluded someone was hunting the Philosopher's Stone. She considered investigating personally for the length of one cup of tea, then made the Slytherin choice: she wrote it up — suspect, motive, method, the Quirrell irregularities she'd noticed since Halloween, *nobody's stammer is that inconsistent* — and delivered it anonymously to the desk of the one person in the castle with both authority and a functioning brain.

Professor McGonagall received an unsigned memorandum of such thoroughness, such footnoted, cross-referenced density, that she reportedly stared at it for ten minutes before walking it directly to the Headmaster. Whatever happened in the third-floor corridor at the end of June happened quietly, involved several teachers lying in wait, and put Quirinus Quirrell in Ministry custody with a story attached that no student ever got fully straight. Harry Potter spent those same June nights asleep in Gryffindor tower, entirely unaware of the adventure he had been edited out of, and lived.

Hermione noted the outcome in the untitled book — *resolved; efficient; no fatalities; no credit* — and discovered that the *no credit* stung considerably less than expected. Credit was Gryffindor currency. She was learning to bank elsewhere.

Slytherin won the House Cup on raw points, a frankly obscene share of them earned by one bushy-haired first-year, and when the banners came down and the train pulled out, Hermione Granger went home to her parents having achieved the highest marks in her year in every single subject, a fact the *examiners* couldn't ignore even if the school found it socially inconvenient.

Her mother's letter went back up in the dental surgery, next to a new one.

*Final entries, Year One:*

*19\. Malfoy has not done the voice again. Terms hold.*

*20\. The Hat sent a note with end-of-term post. It says, in full: "Told you it would get interesting." I have filed it under Correspondence, Smug.*

*21\. Reasons this is actually fine: see marks, see Cup, see the way Farley said "next year" to me at the leaving feast — as if I were already infrastructure.*

*22\. I am already infrastructure.*

# **Chapter Five: The Heir Apparent-ly Not Her**

The second-year welcome feast delivered three items of note to Hermione's table.

Item one: a new Defense professor, Gilderoy Lockhart, whose entire body of published work Hermione had read in August and cross-referenced by September the first, discovering in the process that the man had been in two places at once on five separate occasions, had misidentified a banshee's gender, and described a wand movement in *Wanderings with Werewolves* that would, if actually performed, dislocate the caster's shoulder. She had brought the annotated evidence to school in a folder. She hadn't decided what the folder was *for* yet. That was normal now; she had a lot of folders like that.

Item two: an envelope, delivered by school owl, containing a note in cramped, ancient handwriting:

*Miss Granger — On reflection, and in view of certain remarks made during our interview, I wish to convey that the strength of your objection was noted and is, annually, revisited. — S.H.*

"Is the Sorting Hat sending you *apology letters*?" Tracey asked, reading over her shoulder.

"It's not an apology. There's no apology performed anywhere in the text. It's a *receipt*." Hermione filed it — Correspondence, Smug, item two — and privately noted that she had read it four times.

Item three arrived six weeks later, written on a wall in something that was probably blood:

**THE CHAMBER OF SECRETS HAS BEEN OPENED. ENEMIES OF THE HEIR, BEWARE.**

Mrs. Norris hung stiff as a kippered herring beneath it. The school gathered, gawped, and then — with the unerring collective instinct of a large institution reaching for the wrong conclusion — turned as one body and looked for someone to suspect.

They looked at Harry Potter first, because he'd been found at the scene. That lasted about a week.

Then somebody remembered that the Heir of *Slytherin* would logically be *in* Slytherin, and that Slytherin contained exactly one student who was famously, spectacularly anomalous, and the school's entire deductive engine — a machine powered by rumor and lubricated by idiocy — ground into motion and produced its verdict.

"They think it's *me*," Hermione said, arriving at breakfast to a Great Hall that went quiet in sections, like stadium lights shutting down. "The *Muggle-born*. Is the Heir of Slytherin. Whose entire documented platform was the *removal of Muggle-borns*."

"To be fair," said Tracey, buttering toast, "nobody's read the documentation but you."

"The founder wanted to purge people *like me*. I would be attacking *myself*. I'm the demographic\! I'm first in the queue\!"

"Hufflepuff's running a theory that that's the twist," said Daphne Greengrass serenely, from her seat that was, this year, no longer *near* but simply *beside*, negotiations having quietly concluded over the summer via three letters and one visit to the Greengrass box at a Puddlemere match. "Double agent. Deep cover. Nobody would suspect the Muggle-born, ergo the Muggle-born. Fourth-years are calling you the Serpent's Bride."

"The *what*—"

"I didn't name it, Granger, I'm just reporting the market."

The logic was unfalsifiable, which made it indestructible. She was suspicious *because* it made no sense; the making-no-sense was held to be the cunning part. Corridors emptied ahead of her with new urgency. A Ravenclaw second-year burst into tears when Hermione asked to borrow a quill. Meanwhile — and this was the part that achieved a purity of irony she almost admired — half of Slytherin was *also* wary of her, on the grounds that if anyone in the house were secretly running an ancient monster on a schedule, it would obviously be the girl with the notebooks.

"You do have a lot of notebooks," said Pansy.

"They're *ledgers*, Parkinson."

"Oh, well, that's much less sinister."

---

Then Justin Finch-Fletchley was petrified, and it stopped being funny at all, because Justin Finch-Fletchley was Muggle-born, and so was whoever was next, and the school's list of Muggle-borns was short and Hermione was on it — top of it, if the attacker read the *Prophet's* coverage of last year's exam results.

She lay awake in the green dark and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding. She was the most exposed target in the castle. She slept forty feet from the families most likely to applaud. And the professors' investigation, as far as she could determine, consisted of confiscating Zonko's products and doubling corridor patrols, which was like responding to a shark by banning running at the pool.

So: fine. As usual, if she wanted it done properly.

She had one asset nobody else in the castle had, and its value had inverted overnight. She was *inside*. Whatever the house knew — whatever its parents whispered, whatever the Heir's supporters bragged when the common room felt safe — flowed through the dungeon, and the dungeon no longer edited itself around Hermione Granger. She was furniture now. Infrastructure. People forget the walls can hear.

The intelligence came in fragments through November and December. Fact: Malfoy, swaggering after the first attack, dropped hints about the Chamber's last opening — *fifty years ago, a Mudblood died, someone was expelled* — but under Hermione's direct questioning (conducted via Pansy, at cost of one Charms essay) admitted he had no idea who the Heir was and that his father had told him to "keep his head down and let it happen," a phrase Hermione copied out twice and stared at for a long time. Fact: fifty years ago was 1942–43. Fact: the school kept award records, and a Special Award for Services to the School had been granted in exactly that window to one T. M. Riddle, a name that appeared nowhere else in any history of anything, which for a services-to-the-school recipient was itself a screaming anomaly. People who save schools get chapters. People who get *erased* got something else entirely.

And then, in late December, the single most clarifying event of her year: she caught Harry Potter and Ron Weasley, disguised as Crabbe and Goyle, attempting to infiltrate her common room.

She knew within ninety seconds. Crabbe and Goyle did not hover politely at the dungeon entrance failing to remember the password; Crabbe and Goyle did not say "er"; and neither of them had ever once looked at the lake windows with open-mouthed wonder, because you can't wonder without wondering equipment. She let them in anyway — vouched for them at the wall, in fact, to the visible confusion of the actual password — steered them to the corner where Malfoy was holding forth, and stood quietly by while they extracted precisely the nothing she already knew, at enormous personal risk, with under an hour on their Polyjuice, because she'd clocked the sweating and the clock-watching too, and there was only one potion in *Moste Potente Potions* that produced that particular deadline panic.

She intercepted them at the exit.

"Goyle," she said pleasantly. "A moment. You've got something on your — ah. There it is." A patch of Weasley-red was surfacing at the borrowed hairline like a sunrise. "Lacewing flies. You either stewed them twenty days instead of twenty-one, or you kept the brew somewhere with fluctuating temperature. A *bathroom*, say."

Two pairs of not-quite-right eyes went round with horror.

"Corridor. Now. You have about eleven minutes."

In an empty classroom, as Crabbe deflated into Ron Weasley and Goyle collapsed into Harry Potter, she delivered her review: "The brewing was genuinely impressive for second-years — I assume it was mostly you, Potter, no offense, Weasley, but I've *checked your homework*. The tradecraft was abysmal. You infiltrated the most paranoid organization in Britain to ask its stupidest member a question I could have answered in September, which I would have done, if either of you had ever once *asked me*."

Silence. Weasley had gone the color of his hair. Potter looked at her — really looked, possibly for the first time since the Sorting — and said: "You're not the Heir, are you."

"Astonishing. A Gryffindor has reasoned his way to the position of *every available fact*." But there was no acid in it, or less than she'd budgeted, because he'd said it as a conclusion, not a question, and something in her chest that had been braced for two years eased half a degree. "No. I'm not the Heir. I'm the primary *target*, which is why I'm the only person in this castle treating the problem like a problem instead of a house rivalry. Sit down. Both of you. I'm going to tell you about a boy called Tom Riddle, and you're going to tell me everything the walls have been saying to you, Potter — yes, I know about the voice, you went white as milk in the corridor before the Finch-Fletchley attack and you were *tracking* something along the wall. Nobody else noticed. People don't notice things. It's my whole competitive advantage."

They talked for two hours. It was — though all three of them would have disputed the word at wandpoint — the first meeting of a partnership.

*Year Two, entry 31:* she wrote that night. *Potter hears the monster in the walls. Nobody else does. A voice only he can hear, in the school of the Serpent's founder, moving through walls.*

*Through* walls\*.\*

*Entry 32: What travels through the walls of a castle?*

*Entry 33 (2 a.m.): Oh.*

*Entry 34 (2:01 a.m.): PIPES.*

# **Chapter Six: Mirror, Mirror**

By the second week of January, Hermione had it. All of it. The whole shape of the thing, laid out across six pages of the untitled notebook in her smallest, most triumphant handwriting.

A monster that spoke in a voice only a Parselmouth could hear — and Potter, it had emerged over Christmas in the second and considerably more awkward meeting of the partnership, *was* one, a fact he'd confessed in the tone of a boy admitting to a criminal record and which Hermione had received with a delighted "oh, that's *enormously* useful" that seemed to unsettle him more than the condition itself. A monster that traveled through the plumbing. A monster that killed with a look — except that nobody had *died* this time, and every victim came with a mitigating circumstance: Colin Creevey through a camera, Justin through Nearly Headless Nick, who had taken the full blast and could hardly die twice, Mrs. Norris in a corridor with an inch of water on the floor from a perpetually flooding bathroom.

Reflections. Every single one, a reflection or a lens. Nobody had met its eyes.

A giant serpent, centuries old, killing by eye contact, master of pipes: *basilisk*. Torn out of an ancient copy of *Most Macabre Monstrosities* and confirmed in four other sources, because Hermione Granger did not go to a professor with a single-source claim. King of Serpents. Spiders flee before it — and the spiders *had* been fleeing, filing out of the castle in orderly columns since November, a detail logged in her notebook under *Irregularities, Unexplained* that now slotted home with an almost audible click.

And the flooding bathroom — Moaning Myrtle's bathroom, out of order these fifty years — sat directly above the trajectory of half the attacks, and Myrtle, on patient questioning, turned out to have died in that bathroom, in 1943, upon seeing *a pair of great big yellow eyes* by the sinks. The entrance. She'd found the actual entrance. The dead girl had been haunting the door the whole time and nobody in fifty years had thought to interview her, because nobody interviews crying girls in bathrooms.

Hermione had strong personal feelings about that, which she filed.

"So we tell a teacher," said Harry, in the abandoned classroom that had become the partnership's registered office.

"We tell a teacher *everything*," Hermione agreed. "In writing. With citations."

The memorandum she produced over the following two days was, she would maintain into old age, the finest document of her school career: fourteen pages, indexed, with a fold-out diagram of the relevant plumbing traced from the castle's original architectural records, a victim-by-victim reflection analysis, the Riddle timeline, the Myrtle interview transcript, and a cover page reading **RE: IDENTIFICATION, LOCATION, AND PROPOSED CONTAINMENT OF THE MONSTER OF THE CHAMBER OF SECRETS (URGENT)**.

She submitted copies to McGonagall, to Snape, and — after a brief internal struggle — to Lockhart, on the grounds of protocol, he being nominally the Defense professor.

What happened next taught her more about institutions than the rest of her education combined.

McGonagall, to her lasting credit, read it, went pale, and took it to Dumbledore — who was, that very week, voted out of the castle by a Board of Governors operating under the twin persuasions of Lucius Malfoy's threats and Lucius Malfoy's chequebook. The memo went into a drawer pending his reinstatement. Snape read his copy twice, summoned Hermione, interrogated her for forty minutes on her sourcing with the intensity of a man triple-checking a potion he suspected of being perfect, and then said, quietly, "The staff will handle this. You will *not*," which was both the highest compliment he had ever paid her research and a direct order she had every intention of interpreting creatively. And Lockhart's copy came back with a jaunty note suggesting that she had "quite the imagination\!" and might enjoy a career in magical journalism, and that the real monster was almost certainly a Transylvanian ghoul, which he had "handled before."

Institutions, she recorded that night, *do not fail because nobody knows the answer. They fail because the answer is filed somewhere, waiting for a meeting.*

So she prepared for the meeting to fail. She researched basilisk countermeasures — roosters, all mysteriously strangled since autumn, *someone was reading the same books*; the fatal-versus-petrifying optics of direct and indirect gaze; the acoustic properties of cast iron. She acquired, from Argus Filch's own supply cupboard (at the price of one flawless anonymous tip regarding which students had been vanishing his chalk), a set of small round mirrors. She distributed them to the Muggle-born students she could reach, with instructions: *corners, mirror first, always.* Most of them were too frightened of the Serpent's Bride to ask why the prime suspect was handing out safety equipment. All of them took the mirrors.

She carried her own everywhere, along with the memo's master copy, because the staff would handle it, certainly, but the staff also thought a Transylvanian ghoul had been handled before.

---

It happened on a Thursday in April, in the corridor outside the library.

She'd gone with Penelope Clearwater — a Ravenclaw prefect, Muggle-born, one of the mirror-carriers, and lately something adjacent to a friend, in the way of people who have been assigned the same target profile — to check a reference on pipe junctions in the east wing. And the torches had guttered oddly, and somewhere in the wall came a sound like a hawser dragging through wet stone, and Hermione's entire nervous system executed the drill she'd rehearsed in her head forty times.

"Mirror," she said. "*Corner.*"

She never regretted the drill. The drill worked. It was the whole reason she was alive to be furious about everything that came afterward: the great yellow eyes came around the junction ahead and met two small round mirrors instead of two pairs of human eyes, and the world went white, and the last thing Hermione Granger's brain recorded — with, even then, a flicker of vindication — was:

*Confirmed: basilisk. Adjust memo to past tense.*

---

Being petrified, it turned out, was not like sleep. It was like being a document in a drawer. She would attempt to describe it only once, years later, to Tracey: "Pending. I was *pending*, for six weeks."

They found her rigid on the corridor floor beside Penelope, one arm flung toward the corner, and her other hand clenched at her side in a fist that would not be opened — Madam Pomfrey tried, the mandrake harvest being months away, and eventually gave up and let the fist wait.

Inside the fist, folded to the size of a stamp, was the final page of the memo. She'd been carrying it for weeks, annotated to lethal density, and in the last thirty seconds before the corner — while Penelope fumbled her mirror up — Hermione had scrawled one addition across the bottom and crushed it into her palm, on the theory, later celebrated in three separate books about the war, that *information must survive its custodian*. The addition read:

***Basilisk. In the pipes. Entrance: Myrtle's bathroom, the tap with the snake scratched on it. It's a Parselmouth lock, Potter — it has to be you. DO NOT LOOK. Take the rooster. Yes, that's what the rooster is for. — H.G.***

There was, of course, no rooster in evidence when they found her. There had been. Its whereabouts that Thursday — she'd been keeping it in a soundproofed crate in the greenhouse under an arrangement with Professor Sprout that neither of them ever fully explained to anyone — became a matter of school legend with seventeen competing versions.

Harry got the note. That part every version agrees on: Tracey Davis walked into the hospital wing on the second evening, looked at her friend laid out like a museum exhibit, at the fist Madam Pomfrey couldn't open, and said, "Oh, you *didn't*," with enormous affection, and then went and fetched Potter, because Tracey had sat in on enough registered-office meetings to know whose name would be on whatever was in there. It took Harry, Tracey, and a warming charm suggested by Penelope's boyfriend to relax the fingers.

And the rest of it happened the way these things happen, which is to say: the professors finally moved, and Ginny Weasley was carried down into the Chamber by the thing living in her diary before they could close the net, and Harry Potter went in through Myrtle's bathroom after all — with Ron, with a memorized page of Hermione's handwriting, with a rooster under one arm like the world's worst Quaffle — and what precisely finished the basilisk, fang or phoenix or five-o'clock crow, depends on which of the three of them is telling it, except that all three versions end the same way: *we'd have been dead in the first minute without the page.*

---

She woke in June, mid-sentence — "—the tap with the *snake* on—" — to a hospital wing full of evening light, a stack of get-well cards she would later analyze for political content (four from Slytherin, an anonymous one smelling of Malfoy Manor stationery, and one enormous card signed by what appeared to be the entire Hufflepuff house, whose collective guilt about the Serpent's Bride business had reached devotional intensity), and Harry Potter, asleep in the visitor's chair with his mouth open.

He'd been coming every week, Madam Pomfrey said. Him and the Davis girl, alternating, and once — the entire ward had pretended very hard to be doing something else — Professor Snape, who had stood at the foot of the bed for four minutes in silence, examined the chart, corrected an error on it, and left.

Harry woke when she started reorganizing her get-well cards by strategic value.

"You're back," he said, with a grin she'd never once been on the receiving end of, and then, disgracefully, before she could establish any terms whatsoever: "You were right. About everything. The pipes, the tap, the — Ron wants to apologize, by the way, properly, he's been unbearable about it, he keeps starting letters —"

"Fourteen pages," said Hermione. Her voice came out rusty from six weeks in the drawer. "I gave the staff *fourteen pages* in January."

"Yeah," said Harry, "everyone knows. McGonagall read bits of it out at the leaving feast — you got two hundred points, Slytherin won the cup again, Malfoy's dad got sacked off the Board of Governors, and Dumbledore said — hang on, I wrote it down, Tracey made me write it down —" he dug out a scrap of parchment — "he said, *'It is the particular genius of Slytherin house to be right about the thing everyone else finds unthinkable, and the **particular tragedy of everyone else that we filed the warning \[prose: LMAO\]**.'*"

Hermione lay back on her pillows and looked at the ceiling for a long time.

"Entry forty-one," she said at last, mostly to herself. "*Vindication arrives six weeks after the deadline and is signed by the Headmaster.*"

"You do the numbered thing out loud?"

"I've had six weeks of backlog, Potter. Get the door on your way out, some of us have cards to alphabetize."

But when he'd gone she added, in the surviving notebook, under item forty-one:

*42\. He came every week. Filed under: Unthinkable. Everyone else, take note — this house is right about those.*

# **Chapter Seven: The Homework Cooperative**

Third year began with a mass murderer loose, dementors on the gates, and — far more disruptive to Hermione's actual daily life — a queue.

It formed on the second evening of term, in the common room, beside the table she had occupied nightly since first year: a shuffling, sheepish line of Slytherins clutching essays, extending at its peak past the fireplace and into the shadow of the lake windows. The summer had done something to her stock. She'd left in June as *the odd Muggle-born who's useful*; she returned in September, trailing two hundred feast points and a Headmaster's quotation, as something the house had no prior category for, and Slytherins respond to uncategorizable power the way plants respond to light.

"This has to stop," Hermione told Tracey, on night four, surveying the queue.

"Agreed. You're doing it for free like some kind of *Hufflepuff*. It's embarrassing the whole house."

"That is not what I—"

But Tracey had already flipped over a piece of parchment and was sketching, with surprising draftsmanship, a sign. And thus, on the fifth of September, was founded the institution the school would come to know as the Slytherin Homework Cooperative, and the Ministry would eventually come to know by other names, and the sign said, in full:

**THE HOMEWORK COOPERATIVE** *Checking, tutoring, and structural essay review.* *Payment accepted in: favors (rated), information (verified), or services (negotiable).* *No cash. Cash is for people with nothing interesting to offer.* *All debts recorded. All records private. All privacy conditional on conduct.* *— The Management*

"'The Management,'" read Daphne Greengrass, that first evening, with deep approval. "Anonymous, singular, and mildly threatening. Who wrote the terms?"

"Who do you think," said Tracey.

The genius of the thing — and Hermione would insist, always, that it had been an accident, an insistence precisely no one believed — was the middle line. *Favors, information, or services.* A Galleon spends once. But a second-year who owes the Cooperative *one favor, unspecified, callable at need* is an asset that appreciates; and a fifth-year who settles her Arithmancy debt by mentioning what she overheard in her father's study over Christmas is not paying a fee, she is ***installing plumbing \[prose: sometimes I think the writing is ok and then this nonsense happens\]***, and information, as Hermione had known since she was eleven, flows through plumbing.

By Halloween the ledger — a genuine ledger now, dragonhide, with a Protean-linked duplicate in a location known only to the Management — carried the accounts of forty Slytherins, nine Ravenclaws who'd heard about the quality and come down in disguise, and one Hufflepuff who paid in kitchen access and was the entire reason the Cooperative had biscuits.

The ledger also acquired an enforcement division, hired in a Hogsmeade pet shop on the first October weekend. The shopkeeper said the enormous ginger cat was part Kneazle, could detect untrustworthy persons, and had been there for years because nobody wanted him.

"He's perfect," said Hermione. "He's exactly what the institution needs."

Crookshanks took to the work with the natural talent of a born auditor. He sat on the ledger during business hours. He escorted defaulting clients to the table with a flat, orange, actuarial stare that fourth-years reported feeling in the backs of their knees. And in his first week of service he took an inexplicable, yowling, door-clawing interest in a third-year Gryffindor's pet rat — an elderly, balding specimen that Hermione, apologizing to a furious Ron Weasley across the Entrance Hall while Crookshanks strained in her arms like a hairy crossbow, clocked automatically and filed without thinking:

*Rat, common, male. Age: 12+ years (anomalous; species lifespan 3). Missing one toe. Owner: Weasley.*

It sat in the notebook, quietly waiting, in the section reserved for facts that were not yet **load-bearing \[prose: smh my head\]**.

---

The dementors changed the year's weather, in every sense. They drifted at the boundaries like moored nightmares, and the whole school lived in their draft, and in October the new Defense professor — Lupin, shabby and mild and by an overwhelming margin the best teacher Hermione had encountered in the building — brought his third-years to face a boggart in the staff room wardrobe.

Hermione had researched boggarts, naturally, and arrived with her counter-image pre-planned (McGonagall announcing she'd failed everything; she'd dress the failure notice in Lockhart's robes, riddikulus, done). The wardrobe rattled down the queue, becoming mummies and banshees and, for Malfoy — who'd swanned in expecting to skip the exercise — something in a hooded cloak that spoke in a woman's voice too low for the class to hear, and left him white and silent for the rest of the day, and Hermione put *that* observation not in the Cooperative's ledger but in the other place, the deep file, where she kept the things she'd decided never to monetize.

Then it was her turn, and the boggart pivoted to face her, and became the Sorting Hat.

The class laughed — for a second. Then the Hat opened its brim-mouth and spoke in the small, dry, ancient voice that eleven of the students present had heard on their own heads and every single one recognized:

"*I was wrong about you,*" said the boggart-Hat, gently, in front of everyone. "*I've been watching, and I made a mistake. You aren't Slytherin, and you were never anything else, either. There's no house for what you are. There's no place where—*"

"*Riddikulus,*" said Hermione, on the second attempt, and if her voice cracked in the middle of the word, the crack was gone by the final syllable — and the Hat sprouted the towering chartreuse feathered monstrosity of Neville's grandmother's hat-decor from the previous demonstration, and the laugh caught, and the moment moved on the way moments mercifully do.

Lupin held her back afterward on some pretext about essay lengths.

"For what it's worth, Miss Granger," he said, in the tone of a man who knew everything about being sorted into a category people feared, "I've noticed that the Hat writes to you. It doesn't write to anyone. In eleven hundred years it has never once *revisited*." He handed back her essay, which had no marks on it because there was nothing to correct. "People who worry they don't belong anywhere are almost always the ones holding several places up."

Hermione walked back to the dungeons very fast, and the entry that night read:

*51\. Boggart: noted, contained, nobody's business. Lupin: possibly the first professor to say a true thing to me on purpose. Query: what does HE pay for Wolfsbane, and who brews it?*

*52\. (That was the Cooperative talking. Item 51 stands on its own. Some things are not accounts. Learning the difference may take longer than the N.E.W.T.s.)*

---

Sirius Black broke into the castle twice that year, and the year's whole mystery resolved itself, in the end, not through anything dramatic on Hermione's part but **through the section of the notebook where facts sat waiting to become load-bearing \[prose: this is just not even what “load-bearing” means\]**.

Because in the spring, over a Cooperative transaction — a fifth-year settling an Astronomy debt with gossip — Hermione learned the detail the *Prophet* had published years ago and everyone had forgotten: all they ever found of Peter Pettigrew was a finger. And the notebook, which never forgot anything, quietly connected it to *Rat, common, male. Age anomalous. Missing one toe*, and Hermione sat bolt upright at the table at eleven at night with the specific electric horror of a librarian discovering a misfiled corpse.

She did not go rat-hunting herself. Third-year Hermione had refined the doctrine of first-year Hermione: *establish the facts, then deliver them to the person with both authority and a functioning brain, and this year there is finally one on the premises.*

She went to Lupin. She laid out the chain — the lifespan, the toe, the finger, the way Crookshanks (part Kneazle; *detects untrustworthy persons*; she'd thought it was sales patter) had spent eight months trying to arrest a rat. She watched Remus Lupin's face go through shock, and grief, and then a terrible dawning arithmetic of his own, twelve years deep.

What happened in the Shrieking Shack that June happened, for once, with adult supervision: Lupin and a delegation of staff, a struggling rat, a silver-handed spell reversed, and a gaunt man who walked out of twelve years of hell blinking at his godson, *free*, actually free, because the evidence had been secured before anything could bite anyone under a full moon. Peter Pettigrew went to the Ministry in a warded box. Sirius Black's pardon made the front page in July.

Nobody outside a small circle ever knew where the tip had come from. But at the leaving feast, Harry Potter crossed the entire Great Hall in front of everyone, sat down at the Slytherin table — the scandal sustained the school for a month — and put beside Hermione's plate a wrapped parcel from himself and, of all people, a newly pardoned convict.

It was a book. *Twelfth-Century Wizarding Legal Precedents*, first edition. Inside, in an unfamiliar, half-wild hand:

*Miss Granger — Harry tells me you believe in evidence. So do I, twelve years too late. If you ever want a career ruining the plans of powerful men with paperwork, the House of Black owes you a library. — S.B.*

"Entry sixty," said Tracey, watching her friend's face. "Go on. Out loud."

"*Entry sixty,*" said Hermione, hugging the book, with the composure of a girl who had definitely not just been eyebrowed by fate itself. "*The Cooperative accepts payment in favors, information, or services. It has never once been paid in libraries. Amend the sign.*"

# **Chapter Eight: Genealogy Is a Weapon**

The summer before fourth year, Hermione Granger discovered parish records, and the wizarding world's oldest weapon quietly changed hands.

It started as a defensive project. Three years of *Mudblood* had taught her the architecture of blood prejudice from the inside: the whole edifice rested on the claim of *purity* — unbroken magical descent, pedigrees kept like kennel papers, the Sacred Twenty-Eight. And Hermione, who had been raised by two people whose profession was locating rot beneath polished surfaces, asked the obvious question that no Muggle-born before her had apparently been positioned to ask:

*Who checks?*

Wizarding genealogy, it turned out, was self-reported. The great families kept their own tapestries, burned off their own embarrassments, and cited each other in a closed loop. But wizards had lived *among* Muggles for centuries before the Statute of Secrecy — marrying, christening, burying, and being *recorded*, by the parallel bureaucracy of parish registers, censuses, and civil archives that no wizard had ever bothered to burn because no wizard had ever bothered to read them.

Hermione spent July in county record offices with a notebook and a Muggle rail pass. Her parents thought it was a history project. In a sense it was.

By August she had confirmed what any statistician could have predicted: purity was a fiction of curation. The tapestries had holes precisely where the registers had entries. Squib lines quietly grafted back in under changed names; a "lost at sea" here that was a Muggle marriage in Portsmouth there; second sons who vanished from wizarding records and surfaced in baptismal ones. The Sacred Twenty-Eight, cross-referenced against the archives of rural England, were roughly as pure as the Thames.

She copied everything. She organized it by family. She told absolutely no one, because a weapon announced is a weapon halved, and went back to school with a trunk that was mostly folders.

"You've got your end-of-an-era face on," said Tracey on the train. "Last time you had that face, a rooster got involved."

"I've been doing historical research."

"Historical research on *whom*?"

"Everyone," said Hermione, with simple contentment, and ate a chocolate frog.

---

The Triwizard Tournament was announced at the welcome feast, and the school lost its collective mind on schedule, and Hermione — after noting the Tournament's fatality statistics in the margin of her diary and resolving to stay well clear — found that fourth year had plans for her folders almost immediately.

Because Draco Malfoy came back from summer *worse*. Something had shifted in the Malfoy household after the World Cup — after the Dark Mark over the campsite, after whatever his father had or hadn't worn in the dark that night, a subject on which the Cooperative's plumbing produced only frightened silence — and Draco, fourteen now and rattled and covering it in the traditional family manner, chose aggression. And he chose, at last, the wrong venue: the common room, full house, a Saturday evening in October, when he stood up by the fire and delivered what was clearly a prepared address on the subject of *what this house has become* — a house where purebloods queued, *queued*, to pay tribute to a Mudblood; where the sacred was mortgaged to the polluted; where his father said, where his father said, where his father said —

The room's silence was not agreement. Hermione, watching from the Cooperative table, could read the dungeon's silences fluently by now, and this one was the silence of people checking their account balances. But silence is a vacuum, and vacuums get filled by whoever's standing, and nobody was standing except Draco.

So Hermione stood.

"May I ask a genealogical question?" she said, pleasantly, into the hush. "Since we're on the subject of purity."

"You don't get to say the word," Draco spat.

"Mafalda Hopkirk Malfoy," said Hermione.

The name meant nothing to the room. It meant something to Draco; she watched it land somewhere behind his eyes, in the sealed wing of family memory where children file the names adults spell out over their heads.

"Your great-grandmother," Hermione continued, conversationally, walking closer, folder nowhere in evidence — the folder was upstairs; *she* was the folder. "Born 1897, in Wiltshire, to your great-great-grandparents, and educated — well. Not educated, actually. That's rather the point. There's no Hogwarts record, no Beauxbatons record, no tutor's contract. There *is* a parish record. Saint Botolph's, Fovant. She's in the register four times: baptism, two censuses, and a wedding — a Muggle registry wedding, Malfoy, in 1919, to a Muggle solicitor's clerk, before the family retrieved her, dissolved it, and stitched her back into the tapestry with a story about a Continental education. Because Mafalda, per every record the Muggles kept and your family couldn't burn, never showed a spark of magic in her life. Your pedigree runs through a Squib. Mine runs through two dentists. The difference between us, genealogically speaking, is that my records are *honest*."

The common room did not gasp. Slytherin common rooms don't. What it did was worse: it went *interested*, sixty faces performing rapid private recalculations, and Hermione watched Draco Malfoy watch his entire inheritance — the one asset that had underwritten every word he'd ever said in this room — get marked to market in real time.

"You're lying," he managed.

"I'm citing," said Hermione. "You of all people should know I don't do the other thing. And before you write to your father—" she let that hang exactly one beat "—consider what happens if this becomes a *correspondence*. Right now it's an anecdote between housemates. I've been sitting on it since August, which should tell you something: I don't want your family's secrets in the *Prophet*, Malfoy. I want something much smaller. I want the speeches to stop. All of them. Forever. You can hate me at ordinary volume like everyone else."

She went back to her table. After a moment, Crookshanks resettled himself on the ledger, and the queue re-formed, and the common room resumed at a low murmur that was the dungeon's equivalent of a standing ovation.

Draco Malfoy never gave another speech. It would be years before Hermione learned what the folder never contained: that he'd gone up to the dormitory that night and, for reasons he couldn't have articulated, felt something that took him even longer to identify as *relief* — the relief of a boy shown proof that the wall he'd been ordered to die defending had always had a door in it.

The Daphne negotiation happened three days later and took four minutes.

Daphne Greengrass arrived at the Cooperative table at closing time, sat down, aligned her hands, and said: "My family's in your folders."

"Everyone's family is in my folders."

"I know. That's the pitch." Daphne, at fourteen, had the composure of a career diplomat and precisely one detectable emotion, which was pragmatism. "Sacred Twenty-Eight is a dead asset, Granger. Anyone with eyes can see what's coming — the World Cup was a preview. When it arrives, blood status will be a *liability class*: either you're pure enough to be conscripted or impure enough to be hunted, and there is no third column. Except you're building one. You've been building one since first year — the ledger, the favors, the *folders* — a whole parallel system where what you're owed matters more than what you're born. My sister starts here in two years. I want the Greengrasses in the third column."

Hermione looked at her for a long moment. "The Cooperative charges—"

"Favors, information, or services, I can read the sign. I'm not a client, I'm applying to be *partner*. You have data and leverage. You do not have — no offense — breeding, and I mean that as a technical term: you can't read which fork, which cousin, which silence. Pureblood society is a language. You need a native speaker."

**It was, Hermione had to admit, a gap in the org chart. \[prose: the amount of corporate speak Fable is using is truly absurd. That being said, I do find this one funny\]**

"Terms?"

"Full access to the ledger, veto on actions targeting my family, and when the war comes—" Daphne said it without drama, *when*, and Hermione noted that neither of them flinched "—the Cooperative's protection extends to Astoria."

*Entry 68,* wrote Hermione that night. *The Management is now three (four counting the cat, and audit standards require counting the cat). Tracey handles morale and signage. Daphne handles aristocracy. I handle everything in folders.*

*Entry 69: Malfoy problem: resolved, four years ahead of schedule, at a total cost of one train ticket to Wiltshire. The most powerful spell I have cast at this school remains, statistically, the reading of documents.*

# **Chapter Nine: The Yule Corrections**

The Goblet of Fire produced four names, which was one more than advertised, and the fourth was Harry Potter's, and Hermione spent that entire evening in the registered office watching her best cross-house asset — the term was Tracey's; Hermione had given up correcting it — pace a rut into the flagstones.

"I didn't put my name in."

"I know."

"You *believe* me? Just like that? Half my own house thinks—"

"Potter, I've audited you for three years. You don't want fame; you want a year where nobody tries to kill you. Also, the Age Line was Dumbledore's own casting and you can barely manage a Summoning Charm, which — write that down, actually, we're fixing that regardless. No. Someone entered you under a fourth school, which requires Confunding a millennium-old artifact, which requires an adult, which means an adult inside this castle wants you in a tournament where champions die." She was already making a list. "So we treat the Tournament as what it is: a delivery mechanism. Somebody built a machine to move you somewhere. We can't stop the machine — the contract's binding, I've read the rules four times — so we do the next thing."

"Which is?"

"Survive it so thoroughly that the machine delivers you *in working condition*, to wherever it's pointed, with all of us watching where it goes."

The Cooperative went onto what Tracey called a war footing and Hermione called *elevated operations*. Intelligence flowed in through the plumbing: that Karkaroff was ex-Death Eater (Daphne's father's files); that the first task was dragons (a Cooperative client in the owlery had watched Hagrid lead Madame Maxime toward the roaring); that Moody's flask never left his hand and his office light burned at hours no sane teacher kept, an anomaly Hermione logged, underlined, and — in the single professional lapse she would berate herself over for years — attributed to trauma rather than treachery. Even audits have blind spots. Paranoid old Auror behaves paranoidly: the fact confirmed the cover story instead of piercing it, and she filed it and moved on.

The Summoning Charm got fixed in nine days of drills. The Horntail got outflown. And the Cooperative's fee for services rendered to H. Potter, Gryffindor, was recorded in the ledger as: *nil — strategic alliance*, an entry Daphne reviewed with pursed lips and allowed to stand "as marketing."

---

Then the Tournament revealed its true and most terrible task, worse than dragons: the Yule Ball.

The dungeon convulsed. Slytherin approached the Ball the way its founders approached everything — as a market — and the market said Hermione Granger, fifteen, chief of an institution half the house owed money to, was simultaneously unmarriageable (blood) and unignorable (power), a paradox that resolved itself in her receiving no invitations whatsoever while four separate Slytherins consulted the Cooperative *about* their invitations, including — this was the part Tracey couldn't breathe during — Pansy, who wanted leverage analysis on whether accepting Draco constituted settling.

"You're running a matchmaking desk," Tracey wheezed, "and nobody's asked *you*—"

"The market's rational," said Hermione, without looking up. "Asking me costs blood-status capital that no one down here can spare. I've priced it. I'm not offended by arithmetic."

"That," said a heavily accented voice above them, "is the most interesting sentence I haff heard in this library."

Viktor Krum had been haunting the library for weeks — the entire school had noticed, the giggling shoals of his fan club had noticed, the librarian had noticed — and the school consensus held that he was hiding from the shoals. The school consensus, as usual, had not consulted the evidence, which was that he always sat within four tables of the Slytherin girl with the folders, and had twice been observed pretending to read a book on British plumbing regulation that Hermione recognized because she was the only person to have checked it out in thirty years.

"You are the one who is not entered in the Tournament," Krum said, "and yet the Potter champion does not move without your papers. Karkaroff says you are the most dangerous student in this school. He forbids me to speak to you." He sat down opposite her. "So."

"So," said Hermione, and to her lasting fury, item one of her own internal minutes for that meeting read: *he has very kind eyes and I have lost the thread of the plumbing report.*

He asked her to the Ball in the second week of December, in Bulgarian-accented English of great formality, holding his hat. She ran no analysis whatsoever. Tracey later called this "the single greatest day of my administrative career."

The Yule Ball, as an event, is preserved in three records.

# **Chapter Ten: In Triplicate**

Dolores Umbridge arrived at Hogwarts with a Ministry mandate, a wardrobe in seventeen shades of institutional pink, and one structural misconception that would, over the course of the year, dismantle her career with the slow inevitability of subsidence: she believed that the clever Slytherin girl was on her side.

It was, Hermione had to concede, a defensible reading of the surface data. Slytherin was the establishment house. The girl ran what appeared to be a tidy little tutoring concern, quoted regulations from memory, kept records, filed things — Dolores Umbridge had built a career on the belief that the fileable were the loyal. At the second staff-supervised study session of September she paused at the Cooperative's table, surveyed the ledger, the queue, the enormous cat, and the sign, and produced the smile that made first-years check their hands were still attached.

"*Such* an orderly operation," she cooed. "So rare to find a student with proper respect for *procedure*."

"Procedure is the only thing standing between civilization and Gryffindor, Professor," said Hermione gravely.

Tracey had to leave the table.

The courtship proceeded through the autumn as Umbridge's power metastasized — High Inquisitor by October, Educational Decrees papering the entrance hall like scale armor — and Hermione let herself be courted, visibly, deliberately, at the price of eating lunch within earshot of that laugh and enduring the sincere revulsion of three-quarters of Gryffindor house, who watched the Serpent's Bride go collaborator with the grim satisfaction of people whose worldview was finally paying out. Harry cornered her in October, incandescent: Umbridge's detentions had left the back of his hand a scabbed mess reading *I must not tell lies*, and his ally was drinking *tea* with the woman.

She looked at his hand for a long moment.

"Registered office. Tonight," was all she said.

What she told him there, with Daphne posted at the door: "A blood quill is a Class B controlled artifact. Its use on a minor carries a mandatory Wizengamot referral — *mandatory*, Potter, no ministerial discretion — under the Children's Protective Statutes of 1836, which not even Fudge's Ministry has dared amend because the optics are radioactive. She's not just cruel. She's *criminally exposed*. And she doesn't know I know, because she thinks I'm her little procedure girl, and the day she finds out otherwise has to be worth *everything*. So I need you to do the hardest thing anyone's ever asked of a Gryffindor." She'd taken a breath. "Nothing. Document every detention — dates, wording, the wound, photographs, Dean can borrow Colin's camera — and let her keep committing the crime onto my evidence pile. I know it's monstrous. If I move now she loses her job; if I move at the right moment she loses the *building*. Can you hold?"

Harry, who at fifteen had learned more strategy from three years of Slytherin partnership than from any Gryffindor curriculum, looked at the scars on his hand.

"Third column?" he said.

"Third column," said Hermione.

He held.

The Inquisitorial Squad was announced in the spring, and Hermione was appointed to it before she could apply, which even she found insulting. She wore the little silver *I* with public gravity and spent her patrols perfecting the art of arriving at corridors thirty seconds after whatever had happened in them. Under cover of the badge, three operations ran simultaneously, coordinated from the Cooperative's table like an air-traffic control desk with biscuits.

Operation one: the study group. Harry's people — it had become Dumbledore's Army over the autumn, drilling in a hidden room the Cooperative's plumbing had located for them in exchange for *nil, strategic alliance* — were illegal under Educational Decree Twenty-Four, which banned all unapproved student organizations. Hermione's parallel group, the Charms Consolidation Circle (motto: *Review Is Its Own Reward*), was in contrast painstakingly, provocatively *approved*: she had filed for a permit under Decree Twenty-Four's own subsection F, listed Umbridge herself as faculty patron without asking, and requested inspection so often that the High Inquisitor eventually stopped coming. The Circle's curriculum was ministry-approved wand-free theory. Its supply cupboard shared a wall, and after Cooperative renovations a *door*, with the Room of Requirement. The DA came and went through a study group with a permit. Filch inspected the Circle nine times and found nineteen students reviewing flashcards.

Operation two: the paper. Every Educational Decree was a rushed document — Umbridge drafted them the way she did everything, viciously and carelessly — and Hermione filed every single one in a binder and read them the way her rooster had once waited in its crate. Decree Twenty-Six banned teachers from discussing non-curricular matters with students; Hermione lodged a formal query as to whether Quidditch coaching was curricular, forcing a humiliating exemption. Decree Twenty-Nine granted the Inquisitorial Squad authority to dock points; Hermione, docking points with showy rigor from Gryffindors for offenses like "boisterous stair use," was careful to log each docking on the official forms, in triplicate, until the hourglass audit she quietly requested from Professor Flitwick — as was her *documented right under Decree Twenty-Nine, subsection three* — revealed that Squad members other than herself had docked four hundred points with no paperwork whatsoever, forcing the High Inquisitor to void the lot and restore Gryffindor's hourglass personally, in the Great Hall, at dinner, while Hermione watched with the grave face of procedure's last honest friend.

"You're using her own decrees to strangle her," said Daphne, doing the accounts, "and she keeps *thanking you* for your rigor. I've seen you do things to Malfoy that were kinder."

"Malfoy never used a blood quill on a fifteen-year-old," said Hermione, and there was nothing in her voice at all, and Daphne, who was learning to read her partner's silences the way Hermione read the dungeon's, quietly re-dated the war's start in her own diary to October.

Operation three was the evidence pile. It grew all year: the photographs, the dated wound logs, Harry's testimony and — once word spread through the plumbing that the Management was collecting — depositions from eleven other students across three houses, because Harry had never been the only one, plus the intercepted memo (a Cooperative client in the Owlery, an unsealed envelope, a copying charm) in which Umbridge mused to a Ministry colleague about the permissibility of Veritaserum on students, an illegal act she then attempted in the spring on Harry directly, with Snape supplying fake serum and Hermione supplying, invisibly, the suggestion that he be asked.

By June the binder was four inches thick and indexed.

The end, when it came, came fast, the way subsidence does: gradually, then the floor's gone.

Umbridge sacked Trelawney, exiled Dumbledore, ambushed Harry's fireplace call, and finally — vibrating with triumph in her own office, Inquisitorial Squad assembled, DA members under guard — reached for the Cruciatus Curse in front of witnesses, at which point Hermione Granger, procedure's last honest friend, stepped smoothly forward and asked if she might first fetch the High Inquisitor's *own authorization forms*, since an unforgivable curse deployed for interrogation required Ministerial sign-off under the Emergency Provisions, and surely the Minister's office would want everything *in order* —

# **Chapter Eleven: Extraction**

Sixth year opened with a war on, a Ministry pretending to fight it, and Slytherin house quietly coming apart along a seam that only its own residents could see.

From outside, the dungeon looked like enemy territory, and the school treated it accordingly: Death Eaters' children, the lot of them, whisper and let them pass. From inside, Hermione watched something more specific and much worse. The house was sorting itself — not by belief, mostly, but by *exposure*. There were the committed, a genuinely frightening handful. There were the safe, old money with old escape routes, already wintering assets in Switzerland. And then there was the column nobody outside ever counted: the trapped. Half-bloods whose fathers' names were on the wrong lists. Purebloods whose parents had taken the Mark in terror or greed and now couldn't give it back. Fourteen-year-olds who came back from Christmas silent, with new dress robes and hunted eyes, because a recruiter had sat at their family table over the goose and everyone had smiled and smiled.

The Cooperative's ledger had always tracked debts. That autumn, without any formal decision, its master copy acquired a new section, in Hermione's smallest handwriting, headed simply: *Exposed.*

"We're doing this, then," said Daphne. It wasn't a question. Her own sister was on the second page.

"We're doing this."

"It'll need money, documents, safe addresses, and a route out of the country that doesn't pass a Ministry checkpoint." Daphne ticked them off with the calm of a woman who had spent two years watching the Management build capacity for something and was mildly relieved to learn what. "So: Black money, your forging, my addresses, and Davis's cousin with the fishing boat."

"Tracey has a cousin with a fishing boat?"

"Granger. *Everyone* has a cousin with a fishing boat. You'd know that if you were **properly working-class instead of dentistry \[cont: Daphne has been established as their entrance into elite pure blood circles, but this phrasing makes it sound like she thinks Hermione not being properly working class puts her in the out group. Also, how would she know where dentistry sits?\]**."

The forging deserves a word, because it was the summer's work and the war's most quietly heretical weapon. Hermione had spent two years learning that wizarding genealogy was unaudited fiction; it followed, with the simple ruthlessness of a syllogism, that fiction could be *written*. Parish registers could gain entries as easily as tapestries lost them — a baptism here, a marriage there, aged iron-gall ink and period-correct hands, planted in archives no wizard could check because no wizard knew how the system worked. She did not invent pureblood ancestors for anyone; that lie was checkable against living memory. She built *half-blood* documentation: a magical grandparent, plausibly obscure, verifiably archived. Enough, under the blood laws everyone could feel coming, to move a child from the killing category to the merely despised one.

She built her own file first, as proof of concept: a complete documentary skeleton for one Hermione Jean Granger, maternal granddaughter of a conveniently itinerant, conveniently dead wizard named Hector Dagworth-Granger — a real historical family, a real historical possibility, and a chain of records now supporting it in three county archives and one church crypt. Slughorn, encountering her name at his first Potions class, had asked the question unprompted: "Any relation to Hector Dagworth-Granger, founder of the Most Extraordinary Society of Potioneers?"

"So my grandmother always claimed, sir," said Hermione, with the modesty of a woman planting a flag on prepared ground, and watched the fiction enter circulation through the most efficient gossip organ in wizarding Britain.

"You've made yourself a half-blood," said Tracey that evening, torn between horror and professional admiration. "You. The — sorry, but — the *symbol*. You've retconned the symbol."

"The symbol doesn't stop the Killing Curse, and the symbol can't run a network from Azkaban." Hermione shut the folder. "I'll be Muggle-born again when it's *useful*, Tracey, and I'll be it in public, loudly, at maximum cost to the people who made it a category. Until then it's an operational liability, and the Management doesn't carry those for sentiment."

Tracey looked at her for a moment. "The Hat," she said, "has a great deal to answer for."

"The Hat," said Hermione, "sends its regards every September, and I file them."

Draco Malfoy took the Dark Mark that summer, and Hermione knew it by the second week of term — not through the plumbing, but by audit. He'd stopped bullying, stopped eating, stopped sleeping by the look of him; he'd resigned from the Quidditch team and returned to the ledger, unprompted, a favor owed since fourth year, *balancing accounts*, which in dungeon dialect was a will. The boy was closing his books.

She got the whole picture assembled by Halloween — the Vanishing Cabinet, the reason Katie Bell had touched a cursed necklace meant for nobody sane, the impossible task designed to kill him slowly as punishment for his father's failures — and then she did the thing the Cooperative existed to do. She called a debt.

Empty classroom, November, Daphne on the door. Draco arrived expecting anything but what was waiting: the folder. *His* folder. Six years thick. She put it on the desk between them and then, while he watched, she opened the deep file section and turned it around: the boggart entry. The terrace conversation. The testimony against Umbridge. The dossier heading, *D.M. — recoverable?*, with the question mark crossed out in fresh ink.

"Here's what I know," she said, and told him — cabinet, necklace, mission, deadline, the thing wintering in his manor, all of it, in the flat complete voice of the fourteen-page memo, and watched six years of mask slide off his face like snow off a roof, leaving a seventeen-year-old boy so far past his depth that the surface wasn't visible anymore.

"You can't help me," he said finally, barely audible. "He has my mother."

"I know he does. Narcissa Black Malfoy, currently confined to a house he's using as a headquarters, which — sit down, Malfoy, we're not at the hard part." She took out the second folder. "I don't extract people from him by *dueling* him. I extract them the way I do everything: on paper, through doors nobody watches. Your mother has a sister she hasn't spoken to in twenty-five years, who has a house under Fidelius that the Order will extend on my word, and the route between the two goes through exactly one Cooperative asset with a fishing boat, and the *reason* it can work at all is that nobody — nobody, in his entire organization — believes that Slytherin house contains an underground railroad, because they believe what everyone believes about us. Their prejudice is my infrastructure. It always has been."

Silence. The lake, somewhere. Then: "Why," said Draco Malfoy, the audit question, the only question the dungeon ever really asks. "Six years. I called you — I did the *voice*. Why would you—"

"Because the terms were: your family was off the table," said Hermione. "I set them in first year. I don't break my own terms. And because," she added, gathering the folders, and for once the flat voice cracked half a degree, "I have spent six years learning exactly what it costs to sit at a table where everyone has decided what you are. Congratulations, Malfoy. You're finally at it too. The Cooperative's rates for people at that table are on the sign: *negotiable*."

He didn't take the Dumbledore option; events were already too fast for it. What he took — the night the cabinet finally worked, the night the Death Eaters came through and Dumbledore fell off the tower and the Mark burned green over the Astronomy Tower and everything ended — was the other door. In the chaos of the Death Eaters' retreat, Draco Malfoy failed to be among them. He was, instead, on a fishing boat out of Grimsby with his mother, who had received, that same evening, by untraceable channels, a letter in her estranged sister Andromeda's handwriting and a parish-perfect set of French identity documents for two.

Severus Snape killed Albus Dumbledore on the Astronomy Tower and vanished into legend as the war's blackest traitor, and Hermione Granger — who had audited the man for six years, who had been eyebrowed twice, who had watched him correct her hospital chart and choose the version of events that cost nothing so many times she could feel the shape of the strategy — sat in the wreckage of the following morning, and opened the notebook, and wrote:

*Entry 118: Dumbledore is dead and Snape killed him, per forty witnesses.*

*Entry 119: Snape has spent sixteen years as the only man alive who could make both sides believe they held his ledger. I have seen him choose the costless version of the truth a hundred times. Query, filed, never to be spoken aloud, on my life, on the whole network's life: whose account was the tower on?*

*Entry 120: Extractions to date: eleven students, four mothers, one symbol's own paperwork. The Gryffindors are going to fight this war. Someone has to make sure there are people left to win it for. That's the third column. That's always been the third column.*

# **Chapter Twelve: The Courtship**

The Ministry fell on the first of August, and on the first of September, Hermione Granger went back to Hogwarts.

The Order argued against it for two days. Harry — hunting Horcruxes from a tent by then, on a mission she'd spent July helping to plan, provision, and (over his objections) index — argued against it for four, by owl, in increasingly underlined letters. Her file answered every objection: the Muggle-born Registration Commission would come for Hermione Granger, dentists' daughter, but the archives of three English counties and the fond memory of Horace Slughorn stood ready to testify for Hermione Granger, granddaughter of Hector Dagworth-Granger, half-blood, Slytherin, top of her class, exactly the sort of talent the new regime told itself it existed to cultivate. Her documentation wasn't just survivable. It was *attractive*.

"You want them to recruit you," Harry had written, the parchment nearly torn with quill pressure.

"I want them to think they thought of it," she'd written back. "Somebody has to be inside that castle this year. The Carrows will run it like a camp. The trapped column of my house is still trapped. The network needs its switchboard where the switches are, and Potter — for seven years, every single plan has ended with information moving through me. Don't break the architecture now. Hunt. I'll hold the building."

The courtship began in October, in Slughorn's office, over crystallized pineapple.

They sent, of all people, a Ministry liaison she recognized from the Umbridge inquiry — smooth, careful, one of the survivors who served any regime that kept the trains running — and he spoke for twenty minutes about *rebuilding*, about *talent identified irrespective of the old politics*, about how a young witch of her documented abilities and, he was delighted to note, *newly clarified ancestry* might find the restructured Ministry offered opportunities the old boys' network never would have. The new order, he said without saying, was hiring.

Hermione asked intelligent questions, expressed measured ambition, accepted a second slice of pineapple, and committed the entire conversation to memory for transmission to the Order through the week's laundered channels, coded into the marking scheme of a batch of second-year Charms essays.

"They're serious," she reported to the registered office — the Management, plus, via enchanted galleon relay, what remained of the DA. "Recruitment's real. They've lost half their competent administrators to blood rules they wrote themselves and the other half to Azkaban staffing. The regime is *understaffed*. That's the vulnerability. They need people who can run things, and everyone who can run things is either dead, fled, or in this room."

"So the plan is what," said Neville Longbottom — commanding Gryffindor's resistance by then, and after six years, the alliance's strangest and most solid axis: the boy the war had hardened and the girl it had sharpened, who between them ran the castle's overt defiance and covert plumbing like a two-chamber government. "You *join* them?"

"The plan is I keep almost joining them all year," said Hermione. "Perpetually courtable. Flattered, ambitious, cautious — a girl who wants to see which way the wind settles. Every meeting they take with me is intelligence. Every favor they extend is infrastructure. And every week they spend believing Slytherin's best student is nearly theirs is a week they don't look hard at why the dungeon leaks."

The dungeon leaked magnificently. The Carrows ran the castle on torture and could not understand why the machine kept slipping — why targeted students vanished the night before arrests (fishing boat; its cousin fleet had grown to four), why the Muggle-born third-years they'd registered in September proved, on documentary review, to be half-bloods to a child (three county archives, working overtime), why their own disciplinary records kept developing errors that made prosecutions collapse (the records officer owed the Cooperative from 1994; the Cooperative had never once called the debt; the Cooperative called it). Amycus Carrow raged through the corridors looking for an armed resistance and found one — Neville gave him one, visible, loud, drawing every eye — and never once thought to look for a *filing* resistance, because men like Carrow do not believe in paper until it testifies against them.

Snape, from the Headmaster's office, watched the whole architecture with black unreadable eyes, punished the visible resistance with sentences that reliably proved milder than advertised, and said nothing. In March, when the network's luck failed and a courier fourth-year was caught with a galleon that shouldn't exist, the child was sentenced by the Headmaster personally to detention in the Forbidden Forest with Hagrid — the regime's most feared punishment and, as Hermione noted in the deep file, the only one in the castle that consisted of an evening of tea and dangerous animals with the Order's oldest member.

*Entry 129: Query 119 upgraded to working assumption. Never spoken. But I've started brewing the thing I'll need if I'm right, because if I'm right, on the night everything ends, **somebody's going to have to be standing in the Shrieking Shack with anti-venin and blood-replenisher \[canon-cont: how on earth would she have figured that out?\]**, and it is going to have to be somebody who reads the paperwork all the way to the end.*

The year's dark center, the part she never put in any notebook, was the Easter holidays.

The network's forged-document line finally tripped a wire — not magic, in the end, but bureaucracy: a Registration Commission clerk cross-matched two archives one county apart and found the same obscure dead wizard grandfathering two unrelated children. The trace led upward through the false chain toward its author, and for nine days in April, Hermione Granger was the most wanted document in Britain, and the courtship turned, overnight, into the other thing it had always been wearing as a coat.

They took her in for what the summons called *clarifying conversation*, at the Ministry, in a room with no window, and the interviewer was Dolores Umbridge — sprung from Azkaban by the regime that found her skills relevant, pink again, running the Muggle-born Registration Commission, and staring across the table at the procedure girl with two years of concentrated understanding of exactly who had put her away.

What happened in that room over six hours became, in later testimony, a founding text of the Granger legend, though Hermione herself only ever confirmed the outline: that she'd walked in with nothing but her own file; that every thread Umbridge pulled unraveled into *more documentation*, archives Umbridge's people hadn't found, witnesses already on record, a paper fortress built two years deep against precisely this table in precisely this room; that when the Veritaserum came out, illegally, Hermione had recited the relevant statute, the relevant precedent, and then — the detail every version keeps — drunk it voluntarily, and answered thirty questions in perfect documentary truth, because she had spent two years making sure the falsehoods were all in the *archives* and none of them were in *her claims*: her grandmother had indeed *always claimed* the Dagworth connection, since Hermione had written the claim into the family record herself, and truth serum tests the speaker, not the filing system.

They released her at midnight with an apology drafted by a clerk who owed the Cooperative money.

She made it to the safe flat, and stood in the dark kitchen, and shook — properly, at last, the full nine days of it — while Tracey held her and Daphne made the tea, and none of the three of them said anything at all for an hour, because some things are not accounts.

Then she opened the notebook and finally filled in the blank.

*Entry 121 (completing): Reasons this is actually fine. It isn't. It hasn't been fine since the tower, or honestly since the graveyard, and the list was never really for fine — I was eleven, I didn't have the vocabulary. *Entry 130: Word from the tent (via V.K., via the seventh boat — he says the plumbing book was the best cover of his career, and other things not for the ledger): three down. It's coming to the castle. All of it. Soon.*

*Entry 131: Called in the last favors tonight. All of them. Every account, seven years, the whole ledger, called for the same date and place. Balance of the Slytherin Homework Cooperative as of this entry: zero. It was never money. It was never even homework. It was always this.*

# **Chapter Thirteen: The Third of the Table**

Harry Potter came back to Hogwarts on the second of May, through a portrait hole from the Hog's Head, into a Room of Requirement packed with the visible resistance — and found, waiting beside Neville with a folder under her arm, the invisible one.

"You're here," he said, and hugged her, folder and all, in front of everyone, which did more for cross-house relations in four seconds than the Ministry had managed in four centuries.

"I'm here, you're behind schedule, and if you're looking for the diadem," said Hermione, "it's a lost thing, and lost things in this castle go where everything goes that people want hidden — the room where you lot have been sleeping is one wish away from the room where the castle keeps its secrets. Ravenclaw's diadem is under a stuffed troll and a Vanishing Cabinet's twin brother; the Cooperative inventoried the Room of Hidden Things in fifth year, because of *course* we did, it's the largest unaudited asset repository in Britain. Here's the floor plan." She handed him the folder. "Also, before the castle seals: fangs. Chamber of Secrets, the carcass will have decades of yield left, entrance is still the tap, you still have to say *open* in Parseltongue, and I've had a standing plan for basilisk-fang Horcrux disposal since I was thirteen. It's filed under *obviously*."

Around them the Room was erupting — the DA summoning reinforcements, the teachers sealing corridors, McGonagall's voice in the walls sending the school to the Great Hall — and the last hour of the world before the war arrived was all lists and running feet.

What happened in the Great Hall is in every history of the battle, usually as its hinge.

Voldemort's voice had come out of the air demanding Harry Potter. In the ringing silence afterward, Pansy Parkinson — terrified, seventeen, raised her whole life inside exactly this fear — pointed at Harry and shrieked the obvious, human, unforgivable thing: *grab him*. And four houses turned as one to look at the Slytherin table, wands rising, seven years of verdict arriving in a single motion, and McGonagall, grim, already giving the order that history nearly remembered her for: Slytherin house would evacuate first.

Which is when Hermione Granger stood up on her house's bench where everyone could see her, and said, in the voice she'd been training on that hall since the Sorting:

"The Cooperative is calling its accounts."

History records the sentence. What it can't record is the sound the Slytherin table made: no sound at all, and inside the no-sound, the noise of two hundred people checking a ledger they all knew by heart.

"Every debt, every favor, every entry since 1991, called now, on this spot, one time only," said Hermione. "Here are the terms. Anyone who walks out of that door with Filch is settled — paid in full, no judgment, and the boats are running tonight for your families, same as always; the network doesn't end because the war showed up. But anyone who *stays* —" and her voice cracked exactly once, on the word, and she let it, because Daphne had taught her that the aristocracy shows one crack on purpose — "anyone who stays fights with me, for the school that never once wanted us, because it's *ours anyway*. The Hat put every one of us in the house of ambition. Well. I have never wanted anything in my life the way I want us to be the house that they were all wrong about. Balance your accounts."

And the third of the table stood up.

It was not, in strict fact, a third; the Ministry's official history counted it at thirty-eight percent, because the Ministry's official history was eventually written by people who checked. Gemma Farley's year came back through the doors from the Entrance Hall, where they'd already been evacuating, and stood. Millicent Bulstrode stood the way a wardrobe declares war. Theodore Nott, whose father was at that moment outside the walls in a silver mask, stood with his chess player's face on and his wand already out. The entire membership of the Charms Consolidation Circle stood — *Review Is Its Own Reward* — along with every extraction family's every cousin, the records clerk's little brother, both Beaters, and, at the very end, alone, white as the day of the troll, Pansy Parkinson, who looked at Hermione across the hall of the school where they'd hated and traded and grown up with each other, and said, audibly, furiously, "*Oh, I have owed you since the bathroom, you absolute nightmare,*" and stood.

Slughorn came back an hour later with reinforcements from Hogsmeade and wept openly at the sight of the house colors on the barricades, and the battle historians, cross-referencing, would note that the defense's entire eastern flank — the one that held — was green.

---

The night itself, Hermione ever afterward experienced as a filing system on fire.

The fangs, retrieved with Tracey through the Chamber's tap — the cup destroyed on the Chamber floor, thirteen-year-old plan executed to specification. The diadem, cornered in the Room of Hidden Things through Crabbe's Fiendfyre by the strangest task force of the war: Harry, Ron, and — arrived that evening through channels unexplained, on a French passport, with his mother's wand — Draco Malfoy, who dragged an unconscious Goyle onto Harry's broom out of the firestorm and thereby settled, in the Cooperative's final audit, every balance his family ever carried.

The Shrieking Shack, where working assumption 119 came due at last: Voldemort setting his snake on his most loyal servant to steal a wand's allegiance, and the doorway afterward, and the man bleeding out on the boards with sixteen years of double ledgers finally torn open — except that the person who came through the door was not only Harry Potter with an empty flask for memories, but a Slytherin head girl with a bag she'd been packing since entry 129: anti-venin brewed from the Cooperative's own basilisk stock (the paradox pleased her even then: the school's monster, milked postmortem, canceling the Dark Lord's), blood-replenisher, dittany, and a stasis charm executed at the precise standard of a woman who'd been eyebrowed twice and did not intend to lose the source.

Severus Snape lived. He was furious about it for years, which everyone agreed was the surest sign of full recovery.

And the cost, because the honest books record the cost: Gemma Farley died on the eastern barricade, holding the flank that held. Colin Creevey died with his camera in his hand. Fred Weasley died laughing at his own joke, and Remus Lupin — who had once told a third-year that the people who fear they belong nowhere are the ones holding the places up — died holding one up, beside his wife, and Hermione carried entry 51 to both funerals and read it to no one.

The end in the Great Hall she watched from twenty feet away, wand up, in the ring of defenders — Harry and the creature that had made her whole world circling each other over the question of a wand's allegiance, a question of *chain of custody*, and she almost laughed through the terror at the purity of it: the Dark Lord of the age, undone in the final accounting by improper documentation of an asset transfer. The Elder Wand had changed hands on a tower, and then in a manor, and the man had never once checked the paperwork.

*He never checked the paperwork.* She would build an entire Department someday on the lesson.

The green light met the red, and the wand went home to its true master's hand, and Tom Riddle — Special Award for Services to the School, 1943, the file that started her whole war — hit the floor of the Great Hall with the mundane finality of a closed account.

In the roaring, weeping, sunrise chaos afterward, somebody hoisted Hermione Granger onto the Slytherin table, and she stood there — soot-blackened, one sleeve gone, clutching the master ledger she'd carried through the whole battle like other people carried photographs — looking out at the hall where she'd once walked two hundred feet of silence to a table that didn't clap.

The table was clapping now. All four of them were.

"SPEECH," bellowed Tracey, from below, incorrigible, alive.

Hermione opened the ledger to the last page, and the hall quieted, and she read out, in the crisp Received Pronunciation of a girl who had once done this with a feather:

"Balance of all accounts: *settled*."

Then she burst into tears in front of a thousand people, on a technicality she never bothered to invoke, because the war was over, and some things are not accounts.

# **Chapter Fourteen: Yes**

*Eleven years later.*

The Ministry of Magic's Department of Records, Verification and Documentary Integrity did not exist before the war, and there were people on Level Two who would tell you, in lowered voices, that they preferred it that way.

It had been founded in the war's aftermath by the youngest Department Head in Ministry history, over the objections of very nearly everyone, on a premise she had stated once, to the Wizengamot, in a confirmation hearing that lasted nine minutes because nobody could think of a question she hadn't pre-filed the answer to: *"Voldemort did not rise because we lacked heroes. He rose because we lacked audit. Every step — the false imprisonments, the unexamined pedigrees, the unread reports, the fourteen-page memoranda in drawers — every single step survived on the assumption that no one would check. I am proposing a Department whose entire function is that someone checks."*

The Department checked. In eleven years it had voided ninety years of blood-status records as documentarily unsound (the *Prophet* called it the Granger Purge; Hermione called it "corrections"), **retried every Muggle-born Registration case \[canon-char: what? why?\]**, unwound the pedigree-fraud economy so thoroughly that the term *Sacred Twenty-Eight* now appeared in Ministry documents only with the prefix "so-called," and developed a certain reputation, such that when a Records owl arrived at a great house requesting *clarifying documentation*, the great house's solicitors cleared their week.

Purebloods of the old persuasion did not, whatever the legend said, flinch when Hermione Granger entered a room. They flinched when she opened a folder. The distinction mattered to her.

The morning of the anniversary — the tenth of the battle, a soft May Saturday — her office received its traditional annual post.

From the Sorting Hat, via the Headmistress's office, the yearly note, this one reading: *Seventeenth annual review of the objection lodged 1 September 1991\. Objection remains on file. Verdict remains correct. You may notice these are not in conflict, which was, if you recall, my entire point. — S.H.* She filed it with the other sixteen, in the drawer marked *Correspondence, Smug*, which had its own warding now, because Rita Skeeter had once tried to burgle it.

From Hogwarts, a letter in Slughorn's successor's hand: the Slytherin first-years had asked *again* whether the Head of Records had really once levitated a troll's own club over its head, and whether it was true about the rooster, and whether the ledger in the common room case was *the* ledger, and could she possibly visit, as the legend was developing unsourced embellishments, "which we know you'll agree is the real emergency."

From the Minister's office — Kingsley's, still — the quiet annual query as to whether she'd reconsidered the Deputy job. She sent back her annual reply: *Why would I take a demotion?*

And from the *Daily Prophet*, a courtesy proof of tomorrow's anniversary edition, whose front page carried, beneath a photograph of the memorial and above a story quoting an unnamed Wizengamot source calling her "the most quietly powerful person in Britain, and we do all quietly hope she stays benevolent," the headline the subeditors had fought over for a week:

**GRANGER: BRILLIANT OR TERRIFYING?**

The *Prophet* had owled for comment. Her reply, transmitted through the Department's press office, ran in full beneath the headline, in accordance with her stated terms, and read:

*"Yes."*

---

The anniversary dinner was held, by eleven-year tradition, at the Davis house, because Tracey had a garden and a policy: no speeches, no press, and no more than one work anecdote per attendee, enforced by fine.

They were all there. Tracey, who ran the boats still — a legitimate shipping concern these days, mostly, and the *mostly* was nobody's business. Daphne, who had taken the third column public after the war as the wizarding world's first actual consulting firm and now advised three ministries, one goblin syndicate, and, to her eternal delight, the reconstituted Board of Governors. Neville, who taught. Harry, who ran the Aurors and still, after everything, filed his paperwork so badly that the Department of Records maintained a dedicated clerk it privately called the Potter Provision. Ron, who had apologized in fourth year — properly, at last, eleven drafts, the twelfth delivered in person and kept, though she never told him, in the deep file — and who now ran the shop with George and **told the troll story better than she did \[cont: I can’t say for sure, but I’m pretty sure this is here because Ron is involved in the canon troll incident, though he is not in the fanfic’s\]**, with sound effects. Viktor, retired from Seeking, current occupation: married to the Head of Records, an arrangement the international press had called improbable and the notebook had called, years before any of them, *Subfile: Mine*. Pansy, who did the flowers, magnificently, under protest, every year. Draco, who had come back from France in year two of the peace, testified in eleven trials, and now brewed — of all the endings nobody wrote down in advance — with Severus Snape, the apothecary partnership of Prince & Malfoy being the most bad-tempered establishment in Diagon Alley and the only one where the sign in the window read, by direct plagiarism with the original's written blessing: *All debts recorded. All records private. All privacy conditional on conduct.*

Snape himself came at Christmas instead, annually, for precisely ninety minutes, and had been observed — once, by Tracey, who was believed by no one — to laugh.

After dinner, in the garden, under the same kind of May stars the battle had ended beneath, Hermione sat with the last glass of wine and the old notebook, because the anniversary was the one night a year she allowed herself to read it straight through: toothbrush to troll, memo to mirror, ledger to boats to the blank entry and what finally filled it. The marginalia of a war nobody had known was coming except, apparently, a hat.

Crookshanks — ancient now, mostly asleep, still technically on the Department's books as *Enforcement, Emeritus* — arranged himself on her feet.

She turned to the first page, where an eleven-year-old had written **REASONS THIS IS ACTUALLY FINE** in handwriting that still believed the list would be short. Then she turned to the last page, and beneath a decade of entries, added the year's:

*212\. The first-years want to know if the stories are true. Visit scheduled. I'm bringing the primary sources — they should learn early that the legend is nothing. Any fool can be legendary. The RECORDS, children. The records are the power.*

*213\. Skeeter's headline asks: brilliant or terrifying. The house that never wanted me taught me the answer to every either/or that matters, and it's the same answer that got me sorted, eleven minutes in, **when the Hat asked whether I truly wanted to be the greatest witch of my age and I refused to say no \[cont: this simply didn’t happen\]**.*

*214\. (See what I did there. Page 214\. The Hat's authority in Sorting disputes is absolute; there is no appeals process; I checked twice, while arguing.*

*I hereby withdraw the objection.*

*Filed, at last, under: Reasons This Is Actually Fine.)*

She closed the notebook. In the house behind her, someone — Ron, from the sound effects — had reached the part of the story where the club stops in midair, and the garden was full of the laughter of people who had walked out of a dungeon and a war and into a world with better paperwork, and above the dark lawn the stars came out in their old positions, unaudited, magnificent, and hers.

**THE END**
