## **Chapter Two: Founding Principles**

Master Fyrian Cade taught Founding Principles in a lecture hall built to hold two hundred students in tiered rows of hard benches, and he taught it, Tris decided within the first quarter hour, the way a man teaches a subject he has stopped finding interesting and started finding sacred, which was a considerably worse combination than either alone.

"Magic," he said, pacing the low stage at the front of the hall with his hands clasped behind his back, "is not a feeling. I want that understood before we go one page further into your primers. Magic is not a mood, not a sensation, not something that happens *to* a person the way weather happens to a person." He said *weather* the way some men said the names of diseases. "It is a discipline. It is Founding Principles, and Applied Geometries, and the patient memorization of ward-forms that were tested and refined over four hundred years by mages who understood that power without structure is merely noise. Anyone" — his gaze swept the rows, and Tris had the distinct, uncomfortable sense that it snagged, very briefly, on her — "can *feel* magic. It takes an education to *use* it."

Beside her, Fenn was already scribbling notes with the single-minded devotion of a boy who intended to be the best student Cade had ever had out of pure spite. On her other side, a girl with a merchant's daughter's excellent posture and a mender's-guild pin on her collar sat with her jaw set hard enough that Tris could see the muscle working.

"He does this every year," the girl murmured, barely moving her lips, once Cade had turned to write a diagram on the slate. "The bit about feeling versus discipline. My sister sat through it four years ago and told me nearly word for word."

"Sounds like a man who's given the same speech so many times he's forgotten anyone's listening," Tris murmured back.

"Oh, he knows people are listening. That's rather the point of it." The girl held out a hand below the level of the desk, where Cade wouldn't see the gesture as insubordinate. "Perrine Sohl."

"Trisana Chandler. Tris."

"You're the placement student." It wasn't quite a question, and Tris felt the small, cold drop in her stomach that came whenever anyone said the word *placement* in a tone that meant they'd already filed her under a category. "Ferrisey's talking about it. We don't get many, this far into term."

"I tested well," Tris said, which was, again, entirely true, and let the sentence sit there without elaboration.

Perrine studied her for a moment with an expression that suggested she had opinions about aptitude-testing that she was choosing not to air in the middle of a lecture, and then nodded, apparently satisfied, or at least willing to set the question aside. "Well. Welcome to four hundred years of patient memorization."

Cade's diagram, when Tris actually looked at it, was a basic containment ward — the kind of thing she'd learned to draw with her eyes shut before she was fourteen, back when Niko had made her copy ward-forms every night for a month until her hand stopped shaking with the cold that came, back then, whenever her temper spiked. She copied it into her own notebook with the careful, slightly-too-neat lines of someone pretending not to already know it, and was congratulating herself on the performance when Cade's voice cut across the hall directly at her.

"You. Placement student. Chandler, is it?"

Every head in the hall turned. Tris kept her spine straight and her face arranged into the mildly startled expression of a student called on unexpectedly, which required less acting than she would have liked, because being singled out on the first real lecture of term was not, in fact, part of her plan. "Yes, Master Cade."

"You come to us aptitude-tested rather than prep-schooled, I understand. Untutored talent." He said *untutored* the way he'd said *weather*. "Tell the hall, then, in your own words, since you arrive to us without the benefit of a proper foundation — what is a containment ward *for*?"

It was, transparently, a trap dressed as an easy question: answer correctly and look suspiciously well-prepared for an untutored girl, answer badly and confirm whatever Cade had already decided about aptitude-tested students. Tris chose the third option, which was to answer correctly but slowly, with the halting cadence of someone reasoning it out rather than reciting it.

"It's — meant to keep a working from spreading past the boundary you've set for it," she said. "So the containment ward itself needs to be stronger than whatever's inside pushing against it, or it fails at the weakest point in the boundary rather than holding evenly."

A small silence. Cade's expression did something complicated — not quite approval, closer to recalculation. "That is," he said, "a more complete answer than I expected. Have you studied ward theory before, Miss Chandler?"

*I have used ward theory to keep a hurricane off a fishing fleet,* Tris did not say. "I've read what I could get my hands on," she said instead, which was, again, technically true, and let her voice go faintly apologetic, as though she suspected she'd overstepped. "I like to be prepared."

"Commendable," Cade said, in the tone of a man filing away a suspicion for later use rather than setting it aside, and turned back to his slate. Tris let her shoulders drop half an inch, and did not look at Perrine, who she could feel watching her with new and unwelcome interest.

She found the third of them at dinner three days later, in the noisy, tallow-smelling refectory hall, where a heavyset boy with docker's shoulders and ink permanently ground into the calluses of his right hand was arguing, with the particular fury of someone repeating an old argument rather than starting a new one, with a table of boys wearing the good wool and better manners of the merchant classes.

"—because *I* had to test into a scholarship on my knees in front of three examiners while the lot of you walked in on your fathers' names," he was saying, low and furious, "so forgive me if I don't find it funny when Master Ellard makes a joke about dockworkers' sons not knowing their left hand from their right on a diagram."

"It was a joke, Vasse," one of the merchant boys said, in the bored tone of someone who had already decided the conversation was beneath continuing.

"It's always a joke," the boy — Vasse — said, and then seemed to notice he'd drawn an audience, including Tris, who had stopped at the end of the table with her tray rather than pretend she hadn't heard. He straightened, defensive. "Something you want?"

"A seat, if there's one going," Tris said. "I don't have an opinion about the joke because I wasn't there for it, but I've heard three separate people at this university explain, unprompted, that ambient mages don't know their left hand from their right on a diagram either, so if you want company that's already annoyed on your behalf, I'm available."

That got a short, surprised laugh out of him, more relief than humor. "Corin Vasse," he said, sliding over to make room. "You're the placement girl. Chandler."

"Word travels."

"It's a small university that thinks it's a large one," Corin said. "Everybody knows everybody's business inside a week. You get used to it or you leave."

Perrine arrived a few minutes later with her own tray, spotted the two of them, and sat down with the air of someone joining a conspiracy she hadn't quite decided the shape of yet. By the end of the meal Tris had learned that Corin's mother mended nets on the Lightsbridge docks and had spent nine years' savings on his examination fee alone; that Perrine's family had once been considered one of the great academic-mage lines of the city, before a scandal two generations back stripped her grandmother's accreditation and left the Sohls scraping for relevance ever since, a fact Perrine delivered flatly, like a debt she was tired of owing; and that both of them had, independently and for entirely different reasons, decided Master Cade was the single most insufferable man either had encountered in their lives, which put them both several lengths ahead in Tris's estimation before dessert was even cleared.

"He asked you a trap question in lecture," Perrine said, over the last of a rather good bread pudding. "First week. Everyone's still talking about it."

"He does that to placement students," Corin said. "Especially the aptitude-tested ones. Thinks we've been let in on charity and need reminding of it."

Tris thought of the scroll case at the bottom of her trunk and said nothing, and reached, instead, for more pudding, and let the two of them talk around her, grateful, for the length of one dinner, simply to be a girl at a table rather than a problem someone was trying to solve.

## **Chapter Three: The Weight of Not Saying**

By the fourth week, Tris had discovered that hiding what she knew was, if anything, harder work than learning what she didn't.

Applied Geometries came easily to her in the parts that overlapped with ward theory, and she had to deliberately mangle her diagrams — leaving a containment circle a half-degree off true, mislabeling a resonance point she could have located blindfolded — to keep her marks in the respectable-but-unremarkable range she'd decided on as safe. It was, she found, an oddly specific kind of exhausting, like walking with a stone in her shoe on purpose so as not to walk too fast. Fenn, who marked at the top of every exercise and made no secret of enjoying it, kept glancing at her ruined diagrams with a small, puzzled frown, as though the mistakes didn't quite match the girl making them, and Tris made a private note to be sloppier in ways that looked more natural and less like sabotage.

The wind, meanwhile, would not cooperate with her plan to be unremarkable.

She felt weather the way other people felt an oncoming headache — a pressure behind the eyes, a texture in the air her skin registered before her mind caught up — and Lightsbridge sat on a strait known, the local students all agreed with the resigned pride of people describing an ugly but beloved relative, for the worst autumn gales on this coast. Three weeks into term, a squall line came through in the night sharp enough to rattle every shutter in Ferrisey Court, and Tris lay awake tracking its pressure drop with the automatic, half-conscious attention she'd trained into herself since she was ten years old, unable to stop doing it any more than she could stop breathing, while Fenn slept undisturbed three feet away.

"You're doing it again," Perrine said the next morning, catching her at breakfast staring at nothing with her head tilted, listening to something no one else in the room could hear. "That thing where you go somewhere else for a minute."

"Bad night's sleep," Tris said, which had at least the virtue of being true.

"You do it whenever the weather turns," Perrine said, not unkindly, but with the flat, assessing attention Tris was beginning to recognize as her particular gift — Perrine noticed things, filed them, and waited to see what they added up to. "My grandmother used to say ambient mages could feel a storm coming the way old wounds feel rain. Is that true?"

"Something like that," Tris said, and changed the subject to the essay due for Founding Principles, and told herself the deflection didn't count against Niko's rule, because she hadn't lied, only declined to finish the thought.

It was Cade who made the deflection harder. He had settled, over the following weeks, into a habit of calling on her more often than any other student in the hall, always with the same faint, needling emphasis on the word *placement*, always framing his questions as tests of whether her untutored talent could keep pace with proper schooling. Tris answered exactly as well as she judged safe and no better, which required a running mental calculation she found more taxing than any of the actual coursework — how much true knowledge could she show without showing too much, how convincingly could she stumble over a concept she'd understood at twelve.

"You are better prepared than most placement students I've taught," Cade told her once, after she'd successfully talked her way through a question on ward decay rates while making it sound like she was reasoning it out fresh, "which I find, frankly, a little suspicious. Aptitude tests measure raw capacity. They do not typically produce this level of applied comprehension without formal instruction. Where, precisely, did you say you studied before Lightsbridge?"

"I didn't say," Tris said, and met his eyes, and let just enough of her old, flat, don't-push-me expression show through that Cade, to his credit, recalculated whatever line of questioning he'd been building and let it drop for the day.

It did not feel like a victory. It felt like buying one more week.

The one place she allowed herself no restraint at all was the small, unofficial study circle the three of them had fallen into — Fenn included, once he'd worked out that Perrine and Corin were serious people rather than distractions from his studies — meeting most evenings in the corner of Ferrisey's common room with a pot of bad tea and their notes spread across two pushed-together tables. There, Tris let herself actually think out loud, testing theories, arguing ward-geometry with Fenn until Perrine threatened to confiscate both their notebooks, watching Corin work through a problem with the dogged, methodical patience of someone who'd learned early that talent unsupported by hard work got you nowhere and hard work unsupported by talent got you further than people expected. She liked them. She liked them enough that the secret sitting at the bottom of her trunk had started to feel, some nights, less like a strategy and more like a debt she hadn't agreed to owe them.

"You're good at this," Fenn told her one night, out of nowhere, staring at a ward-diagram she'd sketched half-absently while explaining something else. "Better than good. I don't know why you keep pretending you're only just getting the hang of it."

Tris felt something cold move through her chest that had nothing to do with the weather. "I'm not pretending," she said, which was, by then, close enough to a lie that she felt it land wrong in her own mouth, and was grateful when Perrine, without seeming to notice anything amiss, redirected the conversation toward the essay prompt due Friday, and the moment passed.

But it didn't pass cleanly. That night, lying awake with the wind picking up again outside — a real front this time, she could feel it, still three days out but building weight over the water the way a fist builds before it swings — Tris found herself doing the arithmetic Niko had warned her she'd eventually have to do: how much longer could this actually last, and what, precisely, would it cost her friends to learn the truth from someone other than her.

She didn't have an answer. She turned over, pulled the blanket higher, and told herself there was still time to decide before the storm she could feel building actually arrived.

She was wrong about that, though she wouldn't know it for three more days.

## **Chapter Four: What the Wards Weren't Built For**

The storm announced itself the way all the worst ones did, with two days of deceptively mild weather that made everyone but Tris relax.

"It'll blow through north of us," said a fourth-year at breakfast, with the confidence of someone repeating received wisdom rather than reading a sky. "It always does this time of year. Strait current pulls them north past the point."

Tris said nothing, because saying *this one won't* required an explanation she wasn't prepared to give, and instead spent her free hour that afternoon walking the seawall below the university, reading the water the way another student might read a book, cataloguing the wrongness of it: the swell arriving from a angle that didn't match the wind, the gulls gone entirely from the harbor, the particular flat, waiting quality to the light that she'd learned, the hard way, off the coast near Winding Circle, to take seriously.

She found Perrine in the library that evening, hunched over a genealogical record with the single-minded focus of someone chasing a thread she didn't want witnesses to see her chasing, and it was there, in the low lamplight with no one else at their table, that Perrine finally said the thing she'd clearly been building toward for weeks.

"My grandmother wasn't stripped of her accreditation for incompetence," she said, not looking up from the page. "Everyone assumes that. It's easier than the truth, so I let them." She turned the book slightly so Tris could see the entry — a name, Sohl, and beside it a formal notation in University Latin that Tris's four weeks of half-remembered lessons let her parse only roughly, something about a ward failure and a loss of life. "She built a flood-ward for the lower docks that failed in a storm surge. Forty people drowned. The university took her name off every record they could reach, and my family's spent two generations trying to be allowed back into a discipline that still blames us for a wall of water none of us built."

"That's not fair," Tris said, low, and meant it entirely.

"No," Perrine agreed. "But it's why I'm here. Not to prove she didn't fail — she did fail, the ward broke, people died, that's simply true — but to prove the Sohls can build a ward that holds. That's the whole of my ambition, Tris. One ward. One storm. Enough that nobody says the name like a curse anymore." She closed the book. "I'm telling you because you're the only person here who doesn't look at me like I'm asking for something I don't deserve."

Tris thought, with a lurch of guilt so sharp it nearly showed on her face, of the scroll case at the bottom of her own trunk, and of how unevenly the two of them were sharing secrets, and said only, "You'll get your storm. There's one coming that the fourth-years all insist will blow north past the point. It won't."

Perrine looked at her properly then, for the first time all evening. "How do you know that?"

"I read weather," Tris said, which was true, and left it exactly as vague as it needed to be, and hated, for the length of one held breath, every single word of the plan she'd built with Niko over a winter that felt, suddenly, very far away and very poorly thought through.

---

Master Cade announced the storm-preparation drill himself, two mornings later, with the brisk satisfaction of a man about to demonstrate the superiority of proper training. "Lightsbridge's harbor wards are among the finest academic constructions on this coast," he told the assembled Founding Principles lecture, gesturing at a diagram of the seawall's warding array pinned across his slate — and Tris, sitting in her usual seat with her stomach already sinking, recognized the architecture of it immediately as his own work, cited proudly in the corner in his own hand. "A storm surge of the kind we occasionally see this time of year is precisely what these wards were designed to handle. You will observe, over the coming days should the current system actually reach us, an excellent practical demonstration of applied ward theory holding against genuine environmental pressure. I mention this," and here his eyes found Tris, deliberately, the way they'd started doing whenever he wanted to make a point about untutored talent versus proper schooling, "because I understand some among us prefer to trust their instincts over four hundred years of tested design. This storm, should it arrive, will settle that question rather thoroughly."

Tris looked at the diagram on his slate — really looked, the way she looked at things when she'd stopped performing for an audience — and felt the cold, familiar drop of recognizing a mistake before anyone else in the room had noticed there was one to find. The array was elegant. It was also built, unmistakably, to withstand a storm surge arriving from the northwest, the direction every storm on this stretch of coast had arrived from for the three centuries the wards had been refined against, because no one at Lightsbridge had ever built one to withstand a storm arriving from due north, driven by a pressure system that didn't match the historical pattern at all.

Which was, as it happened, exactly the angle the swell had been coming from all week.

She did not say this out loud. She told herself, sitting very still while Cade moved on to the next slide, that it was not yet her business to correct a senior master's four-hundred-year-old ward design in the middle of a lecture on the strength of a feeling in her chest and a week of watching gulls leave the harbor — that there was still time, that the storm might yet swing north the way the fourth-years insisted it would, that she had, after all, been wrong before.

She was not wrong this time. She would spend a great many hours afterward wishing, bitterly, that she had been.

## **Chapter Five: The Storm**

The wards began to fail a little after midnight, two days later, in the middle of the worst gale Tris had felt cross open water in her life.

She knew before the first alarm bell rang from the Hall of Reckoning, because she'd been awake for an hour already, sitting bolt upright in bed with her hands pressed flat against her thighs, feeling the pressure of it through the stone of the building the way another girl might feel a fever coming on — a wrongness in the air that built and built past anything the harbor wards should have been asked to bear, arriving from due north exactly as she'd feared, hitting the seawall's warding array at precisely the angle it hadn't been built to hold.

"Tris." Fenn's voice, thick with sleep and then abruptly not. "Tris, what's—"

"Get dressed," she said, already moving. "Something's wrong at the seawall."

By the time they reached Ferrisey Court's stairwell, the alarm bell was ringing in earnest, a frantic, uneven clang that told her whoever was pulling the rope had never had to do it in genuine crisis before, and the courtyard beyond was full of half-dressed students and shouting proctors and rain coming in sideways hard enough to sting. Tris caught Corin's arm as he came pelting past, wild-eyed, and Perrine arrived a moment later at a dead run with her coat only half-buttoned, and the four of them fell in together without discussing it, moving toward the harbor the way everyone else was streaming away from it.

They found the seawall lit by storm-lanterns and swarming with senior mages, Cade among them, his formal robes soaked through and his composed lecture-hall manner entirely gone, replaced by the raw, focused terror of a man watching his own life's work come apart exactly where he'd sworn it couldn't.

"The angle's wrong," she heard him shouting to another master, over the wind, "it's coming from the north face, the array wasn't built for—" and then his voice was lost under a crack of failing ward-structure that Tris felt in her teeth, a section of the warding array along the north face buckling visibly, a wall of black water surging through the gap toward the lower docks, toward, Tris registered with sick clarity, the exact stretch of harborside where the dockworkers' families lived, where Corin's mother mended nets, where forty people had drowned two generations ago under a ward built by Perrine's grandmother that also hadn't held against water it wasn't designed for.

She had perhaps three seconds to make the decision she'd spent a term trying to avoid making.

She made it in considerably less than three seconds, because there wasn't, in the end, actually a decision to make at all.

Tris shoved past two proctors who tried to hold the crowd back, planted her feet on the seawall's stone lip with the wind trying to take her off it, and reached for the storm the way she'd reached for storms since she was ten years old and furious and untrained and full of more power than anyone around her knew what to do with — except that now she was neither untrained nor unknowing, and she gathered the gale's own force in both hands, the ambient way, the way no academic ward-form in any of Cade's four hundred years of theory had ever been built to do, and she pushed it.

Not against the water. Against the wind driving the water, the pressure system itself, twisting it the way she'd twisted a hurricane off a fishing fleet three years before anyone at this university had thought to question her right to be here. The gale screamed as it turned, a physical wrongness in the air that made every mage on that seawall stagger, and the surge that had been building to overtop the failing wards lost, in the space of a few violent heartbeats, the wind at its back, and dropped.

It wasn't clean. It wasn't elegant. Water still poured through the broken section of wall, and Tris was distantly aware of Cade and two other masters throwing every scrap of academic ward-craft they had into shoring up the gap the moment the surge eased enough to let them, patching with trained precision what she'd bought them time to patch. But the wall of water that would have taken the lower docks apart did not arrive, and by the time the worst of it had passed, Tris was on her knees on the wet stone, drained hollow in the specific, bone-deep way that came from spending more power in five minutes than she usually spent in a month, with Perrine's hands gripping her shoulders and Corin shouting something at the proctors that she was too tired to fully hear, and Fenn standing over her with an expression of dawning, thunderstruck understanding that told her, with perfect clarity, that the plan she'd built with Niko over a long winter had just ended, all at once, on a seawall in the rain.

## **Chapter Six: What the Records Show**

She woke the next morning in the university infirmary with a headache like a spike behind each eye, Perrine asleep upright in a chair beside the bed, and, on the little table between them, her scroll case — the one from the bottom of her trunk, seal unbroken — sitting where someone had clearly gone looking for exactly this kind of explanation and found it.

"Niko sent word this morning, after the harbor bell rang loud enough to reach half the province," Perrine said, without opening her eyes, when Tris shifted. "I didn't open it. I didn't have to. Fenn told the proctors what he saw, and one of them remembered your name from somewhere, and someone finally thought to ask the Mage Council registry instead of assuming." She did open her eyes then, and there was something complicated in them — hurt, and something that might have been the specific fury of a person who has just learned a friend kept something enormous from her, held very carefully alongside something that might, eventually, become forgiveness. "You could have told me. Of everyone at this university, Tris, you could have told *me.*"

"I know," Tris said, and did not try to defend the choice, because she'd stopped believing, somewhere on that seawall, that it had ever been the right one. "I'm sorry. I was so busy deciding what would protect me from Cade that I didn't think enough about what it cost the rest of you."

Perrine studied her for a long moment, and then, apparently deciding the apology met some private standard, reached over and took the scroll case, turning it over once in her hands before setting it back down. "Well," she said. "You'll have to explain it to the Dean before you explain it to me properly, and I understand he's already spoken to Cade, and Cade has apparently spent the whole morning alternating between demanding an inquiry and rewriting his north-face ward diagrams, so I'd brace for a complicated day."

The hearing, when it came that afternoon, was not the triumphant unmasking Tris might have imagined, in weaker moments, over the winter of planning it never to happen. It was a long, tired, thoroughly bureaucratic afternoon in Dean Osric Vell's cramped office, with the scroll case open on the desk between them, the Dean reading her Council certification twice through with the flat, careful attention of a man deciding how much trouble he was in for not having caught it sooner, and Cade sitting rigid in the corner chair with the particular stiffness of someone who has been proven both right and wrong in the same night and hasn't decided which fact to be angrier about.

"You allowed yourself to be enrolled as an untested placement student," the Dean said, "while holding a Council accreditation that would ordinarily place you well above our own senior lecturers in raw certified capacity."

"I allowed people to assume what my paperwork suggested," Tris said, steady, because she'd had all morning to rehearse this and Niko's rule had, in the end, held up exactly as well as he'd promised it would. "I never swore to anything false. I wanted to learn academic theory the way any beginning student learns it, without my accreditation deciding in advance how people would teach me or fail to. I recognize that put the university in an awkward position once it mattered, and I take responsibility for that part of it."

"You could have caused a diplomatic incident with the Council," the Dean said, "had last night ended differently."

"Had last night ended differently," Tris said, with an edge she didn't entirely manage to smooth out of her voice, "the diplomatic incident would have been considerably smaller than the forty families who'd have been living in it."

It was Cade, unexpectedly, who broke the silence that followed. "The north face was miscalculated," he said, stiffly, to the Dean rather than to Tris, as though it were easier to confess a professional failing to one authority than to the girl who'd exposed it. "I designed that array eleven years ago against every recorded storm pattern on this coast, and none of them arrived from due north. I would like it noted, for whatever it is worth, that Miss Chandler's intervention was not merely unauthorized. It was necessary. I would also like it noted," and here he did look at Tris, and something in his face was almost, though not quite, an apology, "that I spent a term treating her competence as suspicious rather than simply asking where it came from, and that the university's four hundred years of tested design did not, in the event, hold up nearly as well as untutored instinct."

"That's not nothing, from you," Tris said, and meant it, though she didn't entirely forgive him the term of needling questions either, and suspected she wouldn't for some time.

The Dean, in the end, did not expel her, which had been Tris's genuine fear through the whole of the hearing, nor did he simply wave the matter away, which would have insulted the very real chaos the concealment had caused. He assigned her, instead, to a revised course of study — advanced ward theory alongside the first-years, since pretending she needed Founding Principles was no longer sustainable for anyone involved — and to a term of formal consultation on the harbor wards' redesign, a piece of practical work that made Cade visibly wince and Perrine, watching from the doorway once the hearing broke up, visibly delighted.

"You'll be rebuilding the north face," Perrine said afterward, catching up with Tris in the corridor, "with Cade. For a full term."

"I noticed."

"He's going to be unbearable about it."

"He's going to be unbearable regardless," Tris said. "At least this way I get to correct his diagrams to his face instead of in secret." She hesitated, then added, because it needed saying and she'd learned, if nothing else this term, that it did not get easier by waiting: "I really am sorry, Perrine. About all of it."

"I know," Perrine said. "I'm still angry. I expect I'll stay angry for a while yet, and then, probably, I'll stop, because you turned a hurricane around with your bare hands to save my family's docks two generations after my grandmother drowned people trying to do the same thing with proper ward-craft, and it turns out that's a difficult thing to stay angry at forever." She managed something that was nearly a smile. "Corin wants to know if you'll teach him what you did. Properly, this time. Not secretly."

"Properly," Tris agreed, "I can do."

Fenn found them by the stairwell a few minutes later, notebook already in hand, and did not bother with any preamble at all. "I have several hundred questions about ambient ward-theory that four hundred years of academic literature has apparently never bothered to ask," he announced, "and I would like to start immediately, if that's permitted now, since you're not pretending to be a beginner anymore."

Tris looked at the three of them — furious, forgiving, insatiably curious, none of them looking at her the way she'd spent a whole winter dreading, as a prodigy or a threat or a problem to be managed — and found, for the first time since she'd boarded the ferry, that she didn't mind Lightsbridge knowing exactly what she was.

"Get your notebook," she said. "We might as well start with the north face. It's wrong in more places than the one that broke."

## **Epilogue: The North Face**

Word of the harbor, inevitably, reached Winding Circle faster than Tris's own letter did, which meant that by the time her account of events arrived, Niko's reply was already waiting for her, delivered by a Council courier with a speed that suggested he'd paid extra for it.

*I told you it would come apart eventually,* he'd written, in the cramped, impatient hand she knew better than her own. *I did not expect it to come apart quite so publicly, nor quite so usefully. Sandry sends her love and a great deal of unhelpful commentary about how she always thought secrets were more trouble than they were worth, which I remind her she is saying from inside a household that currently contains three people who have each, at various points, hidden something enormous from the others for what they insisted were excellent reasons. Daja wants to know if the seawall needs a smith's hand as well as a mage's, and volunteers herself with a bluntness I suspect is meant as support rather than an actual offer of travel. Briar says nothing useful, as usual, except to observe that you always did prefer to solve a problem the loud way once the quiet way stopped working, which I believe he intends as a compliment.*

*For my part: I am relieved you are unhurt, unsurprised that the wards were wrong, and moderately amused that Master Cade has apparently requested your continued involvement rather than your expulsion. Four hundred years of tested design taught him a great deal, I think, about how much he still had to learn. See that you teach him well, and see that you let your friends teach you something too, since I gather that is a subject you have some catching up to do on. — N.*

Tris read the letter twice, folded it into her notebook where the ward-diagrams for the north face were multiplying by the week, and went to find the others, because Niko, infuriatingly and as usual, was right.

The work on the harbor wards took the better part of the winter term, and it was, against every expectation Tris had walked into Lightsbridge with, the best education she received there. Cade turned out to be a far more careful mage away from the lecture hall than inside it, stripped of the need to perform authority for two hundred students, and though he never entirely stopped correcting her grammar of spellwork with more precision than warmth, he also never again asked her, even obliquely, where an untutored girl had learned to think the way she did. They rebuilt the north face together, academic ward-form braided through with the ambient sensitivity to actual weather that no amount of historical pattern-study could replace, and if the finished array was not quite like anything in Cade's four hundred years of literature, it held, that spring, against a storm that arrived from due north exactly as Tris had known it would, and this time nothing broke.

Perrine spent that same winter compiling, with a thoroughness that would have done Tris proud, a formal petition to have her grandmother's name restored to the university's ward-craft records — not exonerated, since the old ward genuinely had failed, but reframed, with the north-face precedent freshly on file, as a mage who had built to the best knowledge available to her against a storm pattern nobody yet understood, rather than as a name to be scrubbed from history out of institutional embarrassment. The Dean, weighing a term that had already cost him one uncomfortable hearing, signed it without much argument. Perrine did not become the legend she'd once said she wanted to be. She became, instead, something she found she wanted more: a Sohl whose name people said, once again, without flinching.

Corin got his ambient theory lessons, properly this time, three evenings a week in the corner of Ferrisey's common room, and proved to have more natural feel for reading pressure and current than his formal marks in Applied Geometries had ever suggested, a discovery that did more for his standing among the merchant boys who'd mocked him than any argument ever could have. Fenn filled two entire notebooks with questions academic literature had never bothered to ask and began, by spring, drafting the first pages of what he cheerfully announced would be his life's work: a proper cross-disciplinary text on ambient and academic ward theory, dedicated, in advance and without her permission, "to T.C., who ruined a perfectly good secret and improved the university considerably by doing it."

As for Tris herself: she stayed at Lightsbridge two years past that storm, long enough to finish the education she'd actually come for, and she never again pretended to be less than she was. It turned out, in the end, to cost her rather less than she'd feared, and to cost the people around her rather more than she'd allowed herself to imagine, and she carried that lesson forward more carefully than any ward-diagram she copied that whole first, complicated year. The scroll case stayed at the top of her trunk from then on, seal broken, contents known to anyone who asked, gathering considerably less dust than it had at the bottom of it.

## **Plot Summary**

Trisana Chandler is seventeen and already a credentialed ambient mage — vetted, tested, and certified by the Mage Council for her mastery of weather and lightning magic, a rank most ambient mages twice her age never reach. It should be enough. Instead she boards a ship for Lightsbridge, the great university of academic magic, because Niko has taught her everything he can of instinct and will, and she wants the other half: the grammar of spellcraft, the geometry of wards, the centuries of written theory that schooled mages take for granted and ambient mages are rarely offered the chance to learn. She goes not to prove herself but to fill a gap, and she expects to be treated, for once, as simply a student.

She doesn't expect what actually happens: that a credentialed ambient mage enrolling as a first-year is a walking scandal. Academic mages have spent generations building a hierarchy with themselves at the top and "hedge witches" at the bottom, and an ambient mage who outranks half the faculty in raw power, and who has the paperwork to prove it, threatens that hierarchy just by existing in a lecture hall. So Tris does the practical thing: she says nothing. She lets herself be taken for an unusually promising green girl who tested into the program on aptitude alone, endures the condescension that comes with it, and buries her accreditation papers at the bottom of her trunk.

The lie is not entirely a lie — she genuinely is a beginner at academic magic, fumbling through Founding Principles and copying diagrams she could improve blindfolded — and for a while it works. She collects a small, prickly circle of friends: a scholarship boy from the docks who is brilliant and furious about being condescended to; a merchant's daughter hiding her own secret, a broken academic-mage lineage she's desperate to restore; and a boy who talks too much and turns out to be exactly the kind of ally Tris didn't know she needed. She also collects enemies, chief among them Master Fyrian Cade, a lecturer who has built his reputation on the doctrine that ambient magic is a lesser, undisciplined cousin of the real thing, and who takes a particular, needling interest in the "gifted farm girl" who won't stop asking questions above her station.

The trouble Tris cannot outrun is weather. Her magic doesn't wait for her to graduate before it notices a storm building over the city, and Lightsbridge sits low and unwarded against exactly the kind of flood-driving gale that's rolling in off the strait. When the university's own storm-wards — Cade's wards, as it happens — begin to fail under a magnitude of weather the schooled mages weren't trained to read, Tris has a choice between watching her professors and her new friends drown in their own arrogance, or reaching for power she was never supposed to have in that room. She chooses the storm. What follows is not a triumphant unmasking so much as a mess: exposure, fury, grudging credit, a hearing before university authorities, and the uncomfortable, necessary work of making Lightsbridge reckon with what it means that its best storm-mage this century was the girl they'd all been correcting for a term. By the end, Tris hasn't burned the place down — though she's tempted — she's forced it to make room.
