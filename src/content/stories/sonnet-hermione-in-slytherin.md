# **The Serpent's Bookmark**

## **Chapter One: A Difference of Twelve Feet and One Hat**

Hermione Granger had read *Hogwarts: A History* four times, which she felt was a reasonable number of times to read a book about the school one was about to attend for the next seven years, and which Ron Weasley — whom she had met perhaps ninety minutes earlier — **seemed to feel was a deeply alarming number of times to read anything at all \[+canon-char: yup, that’s Ron\]**.

"You've really read it four times," he said, in the tone of a boy assessing whether the strange girl in his compartment might be contagious.

"It's not that long," Hermione said, which was a lie, it was extremely long, but **she had a policy of never admitting a book had defeated her, even by page count** **\[canon-cont: Hogwarts a History is definitely not long for Hermione\]**.

She had also memorized the twelve standard uses of dragon's blood, could recite the wand-lengths of every documented Ollivander customer from 1920 onward if pressed (she had not yet been pressed, but she remained ready), and had privately ranked the four Hogwarts houses by a rubric of her own devising, weighting academic rigor, historical achievement, and — this last category she was slightly embarrassed about — vibes **\[+prose: this was fun, I laughed\]**. Gryffindor had come out on top. Gryffindor was brave and noble and full of people who did the right thing, and Hermione intended to be sorted there the way other children intended to grow up and become Aurors: as a simple, settled fact about the future.

She did not rank Slytherin last, exactly. She ranked it accurately, which she felt was different. Cunning. Ambition. A distinguished history of producing dark wizards, which the book she was currently not-reading-for-a-fifth-time mentioned with the weary diplomacy of a family friend declining to bring up a relative's criminal record at Christmas dinner.

So when the Sorting Hat came down over her ears in the flickering candlelight of the Great Hall, and four hundred students looked on with the **specific hunger \[prose: weird description\]** of an audience that has already sat through several sortings and would like something to happen, Hermione was not worried. She had prepared for many outcomes. **This was not one of them. \[cont: no outcome has happened yet; if this is meant to be foreshadowing, it needs to be more clear\]**

*Well,* said a small dry voice directly inside her skull, in the manner of someone settling into an armchair they intend to occupy for some time. *Aren't you a thicket.*

*I beg your pardon?*

*Ambition,* the Hat said, sounding delighted, the way **Professor Flitwick sounded when a student produced an unusually elegant Charm \[cont: Hermione wouldn’t know this yet\]**. *Layers of it. Not the showy kind — you've buried it under all this studying, bless you, like hiding a sword under a very large pile of homework. You don't want to belong to Gryffindor. You want to earn it. You want to earn everything. **You have already decided you deserve nothing you haven't proven you deserve six times over, and do you know, that is possibly the most Slytherin thing I have encountered in a decade. \[canon-cont: I have a hard time reconciling this with any interpretation of Slytherin’s house traits\]***

*I want Gryffindor,* Hermione informed the Hat, **with the crisp finality of a girl who had, until roughly four seconds ago, been extremely confident about something. \[prose: this is the third time ‘with the ... of’ has shown up, and in this case the extended description adds no info\]**

*People rarely get what they want from me,* the Hat said, not unkindly. *They get what's true. You've a **spine of ambition six feet of solid oak \[prose: what?\]**, a mind that will not rest until it has won, and a **self-preservation instinct sharper than anyone in this hall currently suspects \[prose: is a self-preservation instinct really so notable?\]**, including yourself. You'll survive things in that house that would flatten a lesser sort of bravery. **Braver, in its way, than charging in with your chest out. \[canon-cont: why is the Hat using her bravery to justify putting her in Slytherin?\]** **Slytherin's had worse in it. Slytherin's about to have much, much better. \[prose: so dramatic for so little meaning\]***

*Please —*

*SLYTHERIN\!*

The word landed in the Great Hall like a dropped tray of goblets.

There is a particular silence that occurs when several hundred people all decide, independently and in the same half-second, that they must have misheard something, and then all simultaneously realize they did not. It lasted, Hermione would later estimate with her customary precision, one full second — an eternity, in Great Hall time — before the silence cracked open into noise. The Slytherin table did not cheer. The Slytherin table looked at her the way one looks at a delivery of the wrong furniture: **politely bewildered, faintly insulted, unsure who to complain to \[+prose: lol\]**.

Somewhere near the middle of the Gryffindor table, a red-haired boy who had, ninety minutes earlier, eaten most of her Chocolate Frogs while explaining the rules of Quidditch to her at great and unrequested length, went the colour of his own hair.

## **Chapter Two: The Politics of Pencil Cases**

By the end of the first week, Hermione had developed a working taxonomy of the ways a person could be unkind to her, which she found, in a grim sort of way, intellectually satisfying. There was the Gryffindor variety — **hurt, wounded, personal, the sort that came from people who had liked the idea of her before they had liked her, and now felt cheated \[canon-cont: wounded? The Gryffindors are more likely to be distrustful\]**. And there was the Slytherin variety, which was cooler, more procedural, and in its own way easier to bear: a sort of institutional skepticism, applied evenly, that would apparently lift the instant she gave them a reason to lift it. Slytherins, she decided, disliked being embarrassed by association a great deal more than they disliked her specifically. This was, at least, a problem with a solution.

The solution was schoolwork, deployed the way other people deployed charm.

In Charms, she levitated her feather on the first attempt, with the crisp *Wingardium Leviosa* she'd practiced forty times in front of a bathroom mirror the night before, and did not notice — because she was watching her feather with fierce, private pride — that half of Slytherin had turned to look at her rather than at Professor Flitwick, who was doing his own small delighted hop of professional joy. She noticed only when Daphne Greengrass, in the seat beside her, muttered, without looking up from her own uncooperative feather, "Do that again and slower, would you. Some of us don't want to look like fools next to the little **lion who got lost \[canon-cont: why would they identify her as a Gryffindor rather than a Ravenclaw?\]**."

"I'm not a lion," Hermione said. "I checked. Extensively."

"No," Daphne agreed, watching the feather rise a second time with the flat consideration of an accountant reassessing an asset. "You're really not."

In Transfiguration, Professor McGonagall — stern, precise, visibly still recalibrating her expectations of the girl whose name she'd read off a Gryffindor-leaning intake list months before term began — set the class the task of turning a match into a needle, and looked, when Hermione's match went silver and sharp on the second try, at the small green-and-silver-tied girl in the third row with an expression Hermione couldn't quite parse. It wasn't warmth. McGonagall's face rarely offered warmth as an opening bid. But it was something adjacent to respect, filed for later use, and Hermione — **who read faces the way she read footnotes, thoroughly and for what they implied rather than what they said outright \[canon-char: she does not\]** — filed it right back.

Potions was where the real trouble lived, because Potions was taught by Professor Snape, who was also the Head of Slytherin House, and who regarded Hermione's presence in his classroom with an expression she could not, in her first week, decide was contempt, curiosity, or some private arithmetic he wasn't yet ready to share the answer to.

"Miss Granger," he said, on the third day, after she had — quietly, and only because he'd asked the room and nobody else had answered — correctly identified the properties of asphodel and wormwood in a Draught of Living Death, in more detail than the question had strictly required. "Five points to Slytherin, for showing off."

It took her a full three seconds to realize this was, in Snape's private currency, praise. Around her, several Slytherins were looking at her with the beginning of something less hostile than curiosity now had room to be.

Malfoy did not appreciate the arithmetic. Malfoy had spent the last five years of his life understanding, with total and comfortable certainty, exactly where he stood in any room containing other wizarding children — near the top, by right of blood, and unchallenged. A Muggle-born girl outperforming him in the one class taught by his own Head of House, in front of his own housemates, was not a data point he had budgeting for.

He escalated, as boys with more pride than tactics generally do, by being louder about it.

"I don't see why we should have to share a common room with her," he announced, at breakfast, to an audience of Crabbe, Goyle, and the long-suffering middle distance. "My father says Muggle-borns steal magic. Absorb it off a proper witch or wizard nearby, like — like damp." He seemed pleased with the metaphor. "Explains why she's so *keen*, doesn't it. Desperate to prove she deserves what she took."

Hermione set down her toast with the unhurried precision of someone setting down a chess piece that would, several moves from now, prove decisive.

"That's not how magic works," she said. "It's genetic in the sense that magical ability appears to run partly in families, yes, but Muggle-born wizards and witches are not statistical anomalies stealing anything — there's a well-documented phenomenon of magical children born to non-magical parents throughout recorded history, going back at least as far as the founding of this school, given that Rowena Ravenclaw's own — "

"Nobody asked for a lecture," said Malfoy.

"No," Hermione agreed, "you asked for an insult to be correct, which is a much higher bar, and I'm afraid I can't in good conscience let it stand uncorrected at the breakfast table. Also — and I only mention this because you clearly haven't checked — your father's theory would mean I ought to be getting steadily better at magic the longer I stand near powerful wizards. Which, since you insist on sitting near me at every meal, means you're personally responsible for accelerating my education. I'll be sure to thank you in my Newt results."

Goyle laughed before he could stop himself, a short surprised bark, and then looked terrified about it. Malfoy went the shade of a Quaffle and did not speak again until the eggs were cold.

It was a small victory. Hermione, cataloguing it in her notebook later with the same rigor she applied to Transfiguration theory, recorded it as: *Malfoy — public engagement, round one: won on points. Sustainability of strategy: uncertain. Continue monitoring.*

She was, by the second week, spending a great deal of time in the library, which surprised no one who had met her and worried several people who hadn't quite decided what to make of her yet. It was in the library, on a Wednesday, elbow-deep in a stack of books on wandlore she had no immediate assignment requiring, that Daphne Greengrass sat down across from her without invitation, arranged her own books with rather more elegance than the task required, and said, without looking up, "You're doing this wrong, you know."

"Doing what wrong?"

"Slytherin." Daphne turned a page. "**You're trying to win them over by being unbearably right about things in front of an audience. That works on professors. It does not work on eleven-year-olds \[canon-char: this absolutely rings true for how Hermione’s personality would be received\]** who've been raised since birth to believe that people who talk like you talk are — the word my mother uses is *strident* — showing off doesn't make people like you here. It makes people wary of you. Which, actually," she added, with the air of someone conceding a point she found mildly annoying to concede, "might be more useful to you than being liked. But you should know which one you're getting."

Hermione considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "What would you suggest instead?"

"Fewer lectures at breakfast. More information delivered privately, to people who can use it, in exchange for something." Daphne finally looked up. "You corrected Pansy's Charms essay last night. I watched you do it. You didn't have to — she didn't ask, and she certainly didn't thank you."

"It was wrong. The wand movement for the Locking Charm was inverted."

"Yes. And now she knows it, and doesn't have to embarrass herself in front of Flitwick, and she still doesn't like you, because you told her in front of three other people in a tone that suggested you found her stupidity personally inconvenient." Daphne closed her book with a small, decisive snap. "Try telling her quietly. Try implying, somehow, that you're doing her a favor rather than doing her a service to your own ego. People here don't want to be *corrected*, Granger. They want to be *helped*, discreetly, in ways they can later pretend they didn't need."

It was, Hermione reflected, walking back to the dungeons that evening with this new and slightly uncomfortable data folded into her worldview, essentially a lesson in diplomacy delivered with the clinical detachment of a Potions demonstration, and she rather thought she preferred it that way. Warmth, as she'd already decided, she could survive without for a while. Precision she could use immediately.

She started, cautiously, to apply it. She stopped announcing corrections and started delivering them as private asides — *you might want to check the third ingredient, I think there's a misprint in the older textbooks* — and watched, with real scientific interest, as the same information that had earned her resentment when broadcast earned her something closer to cautious gratitude when whispered. She noticed, too, which of her housemates began, very slowly, to sit slightly nearer to her at meals: not Malfoy's set, who remained implacable, but a loose scattering of quieter Slytherins — Theodore Nott, who said almost nothing but appeared, twice, at her elbow in the library with a book he thought she might want; Millicent Bulstrode, who defended her once in a corridor from a pair of second-year Ravenclaws with a bluntness that required no thanks and received none, because Millicent looked as though thanks would embarrass her badly.

It was not friendship. Hermione was too precise a person to call it that yet. But it was, she noted in the notebook, ***the beginning of a data set \[prose: what?\]**,* and for the first time since the Hat had upended her carefully organized future, she went to sleep without rehearsing arguments for why it had been wrong.

## **Chapter Three: A Troll, a Toilet, and the Beginnings of a Reputation**

The trouble with being universally regarded as strange, Hermione discovered on the thirty-first of October, was that it left her almost entirely alone at the one moment a school full of children might reasonably have used some help.

She had not, in fact, gone to the Halloween feast at all. She had spent the afternoon in an unused second-floor bathroom that smelled faintly of damp stone and lavender bleach, crying with the furious, humiliated intensity of someone who had, for the first time since the sorting, allowed herself to feel the full weight of eight weeks of careful strategy amounting to *cautious tolerance* rather than anything softer — because Pansy Parkinson had, that lunchtime, in front of the whole Slytherin table, done an extremely accurate impression of Hermione's speech patterns that had made even Theodore Nott laugh, and something about being mocked accurately, rather than unfairly, had cut in a way she hadn't braced for.

She was still there, composing herself with the ferocious discipline of a girl **who did not believe in being seen crying twice in one lifetime \[prose: what?\]**, when the smell reached her — something enormous and unwashed, drifting down the corridor like a rumor — and then the bathroom door came off its hinges.

The troll was, Hermione would reflect afterward with the detached precision she brought to reviewing any experience for lessons learned, considerably larger in person than the illustrations in *Fantastic Beasts* had led her to expect, and considerably less interested in conversation. It had a club. It smelled the way she imagined a swamp might smell if the swamp had also died. And it was between her and the door.

She did not scream, which she would later consider one of her few decisions that evening she was unreservedly proud of. She did, however, dive under the row of sinks with a speed that startled her own body, wand out, mind moving through every piece of first-year theory she had at a pace that felt less like thinking and more like flipping through an index at terrifying velocity — *Wingardium Leviosa isn't a combat spell, there's no offensive curse in the curriculum yet, but the sinks are heavy, and heavy things can be moved —*

The troll's club came down on the row of sinks she'd been crouched beside a half-second after she'd rolled clear, and cold porcelain shrapnel sprayed the tiles, and somewhere very distantly Hermione registered that this was the single most frightened she had ever been and that fear, it turned out, sharpened her mind rather than scattering it, which she suspected was either a very good sign about her future or a very bad one.

It was Harry Potter and Ron Weasley who came through the ruined doorway — Ron white as parchment, Harry with a wand already half-raised, both of them apparently having realized over the pumpkin soup that she was the one first-year unaccounted for and having done the thing Gryffindors reliably did, which was run directly toward the danger rather than away from it, a tendency Hermione found reckless, inefficient, and — quietly, in the part of her mind she wasn't examining closely — moving.

"Swish and flick\!" she shouted at Ron, over the crash of the troll's club finding the second sink, because if nobody else was going to say the useful thing, she would, terror or no terror. "The wand movement — it's swish and flick, not what you're doing —"

Ron, to his eternal credit and considerable later embarrassment, actually adjusted his grip mid-panic and got the incantation right on the second attempt: the troll's own club rose from its hand, hovered with magnificent unconcern for several seconds, and dropped, with a crack Hermione felt in her back teeth, directly onto its owner's head.

The troll swayed. The troll sat down, with the profound, deliberate slowness of a felled tree deciding on its own angle of repose, and did not get up again.

For a moment none of the three of them moved. Then Ron said, in a voice several registers higher than his usual, "Is it — is it dead?"

"Unconscious, I should think," Hermione said, climbing out from under the ruined sinks with more dignity than the situation strictly allowed, brushing porcelain dust from her robes with hands that had only just begun to shake now that there was time to notice. "Trolls have exceptionally thick skulls. It would likely take rather more than that to actually kill it, which I imagine is fortunate, since none of us intended to."

Professor McGonagall arrived moments later with Snape and a flustered Professor Quirrell at her heels, took in the scene — the wrecked bathroom, the unconscious troll, the two Gryffindor boys and the one very disheveled Slytherin girl standing amid the wreckage — with an expression that suggested she was doing quick and unhappy arithmetic of her own.

"Explain," she said, in the voice of a woman who suspected she would not enjoy the explanation.

And here Hermione made a decision that would, in its own small way, do more for her standing in Slytherin house than three months of correctly answered Potions questions.

"**It's my fault, Professor \[exec: I don’t understand why this will be good for her standing with Slytherin\]**," she said, before either boy could speak. "I went looking for the troll myself — I'd read about them and I wanted to see one, I didn't think anyone had mentioned there was actually one loose in the castle until it was rather too late to reconsider. Harry and Ron came to help me. If they hadn't, I don't imagine I'd be explaining anything to you right now."

It was not, strictly, a lie by omission Slytherin's Head of House would ever have expected from her — Snape's dark eyes flicked to her with an expression she couldn't read, something that might, in a better-lit room, have resolved into surprise — and it was certainly not a story that made her look clever, brave, or even particularly sensible. It cost her five points and a stern lecture about reckless curiosity that she absorbed with the placid patience of someone who had calculated the exchange rate in advance and found it acceptable.

"That was completely mental," Ron said afterward, in the corridor, once the professors had gone and the three of them stood alone amid the settling dust, "why would you tell McGonagall it was your fault? You'd have got off with a warning otherwise, we're the ones who — "

"Because," Hermione said, "if I'd told her the truth, she'd have had to ask why the two of you were looking for a Slytherin at all, given that we've barely spoken since the sorting, and that would have meant explaining that you'd noticed me missing and come to check, which is a kindness neither of you would probably want widely known you extended to someone in this tie. I've done the maths. It was cheaper for me to lie."

Harry looked at her for a long moment, in a way that made her feel unexpectedly examined, and said, "Thanks."

"Don't mention it," Hermione said, meaning it rather more literally than the phrase usually implied, and turned to go — and found Ron still staring at her with the baffled, dawning expression of a boy revising a first impression in real time.

"You're still completely mental," he said, but he said it the way one says it about a friend, and Hermione filed that too, carefully, in the growing section of her notebook she had begun, privately, to label *exceptions.*

The story that circulated through Slytherin house over the following days — as such stories inevitably mutate in the retelling — arrived considerably embellished, with Hermione variously reported to have hexed the troll unconscious single-handed, negotiated with it, or (Pansy's contribution, delivered with more grudging admiration than she likely intended) *bitten it.* Hermione corrected none of these versions, on the grounds that Daphne's diplomatic principles applied here too: better to let a flattering rumor do its own quiet work than spoil it with something as inconvenient as the truth.

"You let a troll story make you popular," Daphne observed, over breakfast three days later, "by doing absolutely nothing except failing to correct people. I'm almost impressed."

"I'm learning," Hermione said, and reached for the marmalade, and did not mention, because it was not yet relevant data, that she had also quietly begun brewing a Confusing Concoction in a disused second-floor bathroom that smelled, she'd decided, considerably better since the troll had stopped using it as a personal residence.

## **Chapter Four: The Confusing Concoction**

The theft of Hermione's Potions notes had happened in the second week of term — a folder of meticulously cross-referenced parchment, color-coded by ingredient and annotated with her own predictions about which of Snape's questions were designed to catch out the unprepared, gone from her bag between Charms and lunch, and reappearing, three days later, in slightly rearranged form in Draco Malfoy's satchel, from which he had been quietly copying answers he could not otherwise have produced given his demonstrated grasp of asphodel.

Hermione had said nothing at the time. This was not, she would like it clearly understood, forgiveness. It was patience, which she considered an entirely different and more useful virtue, deployed while she identified the correct instrument of consequence.

The Confusing Concoction — a mild, mostly harmless potion whose principal effect was several hours of profound and specific difficulty telling left from right, up from down, and, most usefully, the letters *b*, *d*, *p*, and *q* apart from one another — was not, strictly, part of the first-year curriculum. Hermione had found the recipe in a book two years above her level, on the grounds that if she was going to break several school rules regarding unsupervised brewing, she intended to do it competently.

She brewed it, over three evenings, in the second-floor bathroom, using a cauldron borrowed (she preferred *requisitioned*) from the back of a supply cupboard she had noticed was rarely checked, and administered it — this was the part requiring genuine cunning, the part the Hat, she suspected, would have approved of most — not to Malfoy's pumpkin juice, which he watched with the vigilance of a boy who had made enemies and knew it, but to the ink in his personal inkwell, purchased that same week from Diagon Alley with an ostentatious flourish he'd made sure the whole common room witnessed, which he therefore trusted absolutely and refilled from a large bottle he kept, unguarded, in his open trunk.

The Potions essay Draco Malfoy submitted the following Tuesday — on the correct order of ingredients for a Wit-Sharpening Potion, an assignment whose thematic irony was not lost on Hermione even as she maintained an expression of perfect, studious innocence three rows away — was, Professor Snape informed the class in a voice of glacial, delighted disbelief, "written, Mr. Malfoy, in a hand I can only assume belongs to someone experiencing a temporary and profound disagreement with the alphabet," and awarded a Troll, the lowest possible grade, with the specific relish of a man who did not often get to enjoy his job this much.

Malfoy, whose ink had apparently begun reversing his letters somewhere around the third paragraph without his noticing until it was much too late to start over, went the color of curdled milk and spent the rest of the lesson attempting, with mounting hysteria, to determine whether the problem was his handwriting, his eyesight, or a curse, while Hermione took notes on the correct ordering of Wit-Sharpening ingredients with the serene, unhurried penmanship of someone whose own ink had never once betrayed her.

He never proved it was her. This, Hermione felt, was the elegant part. Confusing Concoction left no trace after eight hours, the ink itself tested perfectly normal to any diagnostic charm Malfoy's father's solicitor-adjacent connections could produce, and — crucially — Hermione had spoken to precisely no one about it, which meant that when the story nonetheless filtered through Slytherin house within the week, in the specific hushed, delighted tones reserved for excellent gossip, it arrived entirely without her fingerprints on the telling.

"You didn't," Daphne said, cornering her in the library with the flat, assessing stare of someone conducting an audit.

"Didn't what?"

"There is currently a rumor circulating that Draco Malfoy's own ink attacked his intelligence, three weeks after he was caught with your stolen notes in his bag, and you have said nothing about it whatsoever, in either direction, which is, for you, deeply suspicious."

"I have no idea what you're referring to," Hermione said, turning a page of *Advanced Rune Theory* she did not, strictly, need to be reading yet.

Daphne studied her for a long moment, and then — slowly, reluctantly, as though the expression cost her something — smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of a chess player watching an opponent make a move she hadn't anticipated and rather admired despite herself.

"That," she said, "was extremely Slytherin of you."

"Thank you," said Hermione, who had, by November, stopped flinching at the word.

The consequence of the whole affair — beyond the private, deeply satisfying knowledge that Draco Malfoy would check his inkwell for curses before every essay for the remainder of the term — was a subtle and permanent recalibration of where Hermione Granger sat in Slytherin's internal ledger of who was worth crossing. Malfoy did not stop disliking her. But he stopped, notably, stealing her notes, and began instead addressing her with the wary, formal hostility one reserves for an opponent one has decided, grudgingly, not to underestimate twice.

**Marcus Nott, passing her in the common room a few days later, paused just long enough to say, without any particular warmth but also without any of the earlier skepticism, "Nicely done, Granger," \[+exec: I like this, simple way to show how her getting untraceable revenge was effective\]** and did not elaborate on what, exactly, he was congratulating her for, which Hermione understood to be the entire point.

## **Chapter Five: Christmas, and an Unsolicited Alliance**

Hermione stayed at Hogwarts over the Christmas holidays — **her parents had, with the particular generous bewilderment of Muggle parents whose daughter had been sorted into the one house even they had heard bad things about, offered to bring her home the moment she said the word, and she had, after some consideration, declined \[prose: aside from the ridiculous sentence structure, I dislike this because I would have loved to see this interaction with her parents\]**, on the grounds that the castle over the holidays was quiet, the library was hers nearly alone, and there was a particular corridor on the third floor she had become extremely curious about since September, when a large three-headed dog had, very briefly and very memorably, been visible through a door that was absolutely, according to every professor she'd delicately interrogated on the subject, none of her business.

She was not, it must be said, alone in her curiosity. Harry Potter and Ron Weasley had also stayed, for reasons of their own — Ron's family off to Romania, Harry simply having nowhere else that felt like anywhere — and the three of them had drifted, over the quiet week between terms, into the sort of wary, unofficial alliance that forms between people who have all independently decided the same mystery is worth solving and would rather not be caught solving it alone.

"It's obviously about the dog," Hermione said, on the twenty-seventh, in a mostly empty library, having cornered the two boys at a table with the brisk efficiency of someone calling a meeting to order. "Fluffy — Hagrid told you its name is *Fluffy*, which is, incidentally, the least secure piece of information security I have ever encountered in a person entrusted with guarding anything — is standing on a trapdoor. Whatever's under that trapdoor is what Hagrid collected from Gringotts in July, which was also broken into in July, on the same day, which cannot be a coincidence, because I don't believe in those."

Ron blinked at her. "How d'you even know about Gringotts?"

"I read the Daily Prophet. Both of you, I've noticed, only read it when someone else has already told you what's in it, which is an appalling way to gather information, but I'll let that go for now." She pulled a book toward her — *Nicolas Flamel and the Alchemical Renaissance*, borrowed, with some difficulty, from the Restricted Section shelf she was not technically permitted near, using a signed note she had, in her own defense, only mildly forged from Professor Snape's very distinctive and therefore very copyable handwriting. "The Chocolate Frog card. The one you told me about, Harry — Dumbledore's card, mentioning his work in alchemy with a partner named Nicolas Flamel. It took me four days to find him properly indexed, because whoever catalogued this section has an organizational system that appears to be *vibes*, but I found him. He's the only known maker of the Philosopher's Stone. Six hundred and sixty-five years old. Married to a woman named Perenelle."

Ron looked, briefly, like his brain had been hit with its own version of a troll's club. "The Philosopher's Stone. That's — that turns things to gold, doesn't it. And the — the immortality thing."

"The Elixir of Life, yes. Which means," Hermione said, closing the book with the crisp finality she reserved for delivering conclusions she considered self-evident, "that whatever is under that trapdoor is very possibly the single most valuable magical object in Britain, that someone already attempted to steal it from the one place it should have been unimpeachably safe, and that it has subsequently been moved to a school full of children, guarded by a three-headed dog whose name suggests the security planning was conducted by someone who does not take threats seriously. I would like to know who is trying to steal it, and I would like to know before they succeed, because an unsolved mystery of this magnitude sitting directly beneath my dormitory is, frankly, an insult to my intelligence that I refuse to tolerate."

There was a short silence.

"You're doing this because it's annoying you," Harry said slowly, "not because you're worried someone might get hurt."

"I'm doing it," Hermione said, "for several overlapping reasons, of which that is honestly one of the smaller ones, but yes, it is on the list, and I don't see why that should surprise you." She looked between the two of them, weighing something. "I am aware that an alliance between a Slytherin and two Gryffindors is, socially, a fairly significant liability for all three of us if it becomes widely known. I'd propose we keep it quiet and functional. I have research capacity you don't. You have — " she considered Ron for a moment " — Ron has a working knowledge of the layout of this castle from six older brothers' worth of secondhand mischief, and you," to Harry, "apparently have a habit of walking directly into danger that seems to reliably produce useful information, so between the three of us I believe we cover the necessary ground."

"You've really thought this through," Ron said, sounding faintly awed and faintly alarmed in equal measure.

"I generally do," said Hermione, and opened the next book.

It was, over the following weeks, a strange sort of friendship — conducted mostly in the library, occasionally in whispered exchanges in corridors where the wrong Slytherin or the wrong Gryffindor might see and draw conclusions neither house would receive kindly, built less on affection, at first, than on the shared, slightly thrilling sensation of three people independently deciding the rules didn't quite apply to them if the cause was good enough. Ron brought a rumor, once, from his brothers' old notes, about a passage behind a tapestry. Harry brought an unsettling account of Professor Snape's limp after a Quidditch match, and a bite mark on his leg that matched, disturbingly, a dog's. Hermione brought footnotes, cross-references, and — increasingly, as the weeks went on — the particular steadying presence of someone who had decided, once and for all, that she was not going to be caught unprepared by anything ever again, and intended to extend that policy to her two entirely unauthorized new collaborators whether they'd asked for the protection or not.

She did not, in this period, tell Daphne Greengrass any of it. This was, she recognized even as she did it, a small and specific betrayal of the diplomatic principles Daphne herself had taught her — *information delivered privately, to people who can use it* — but some information, she had also learned, was safer split cleanly down house lines, and she was not yet certain enough of anyone's loyalties, her own included, to risk otherwise.

## **Chapter Six: Detention, Doubles, and the Insufferable Draco Malfoy**

Their eventual pairing was, in the great tradition of most of the important events of Hermione's first year, not her idea.

It happened in February, in Potions, when Draco Malfoy — who had spent the intervening months developing a habit of pointed, needling commentary he'd apparently decided was safer than outright confrontation, having learned his lesson about direct engagement rather thoroughly the previous November — made the mistake of loudly speculating, mid-lesson, about how a Mudblood had presumably bribed, blackmailed, or magically coerced her way to the top of every class, and did so at a volume Professor Snape's notoriously excellent hearing had no difficulty catching.

Snape did not, as Hermione had half-expected, ignore it in the manner he ignored most Slytherin-on-Slytherin unpleasantness that didn't involve visible property damage. He set down the chalk with the particular slow deliberation of a man deciding, after some months of quiet observation, that a correction was overdue, and said, in a voice that dropped the temperature of the dungeon by several degrees, "Detention, Mr. Malfoy. Tonight. You will be assisting Miss Granger in the preparation of ingredients for the second-year classes, and you will do so — " a pause, aimed with surgical precision " — competently, which I understand may require some effort on your part given how rarely you've demonstrated the skill in this classroom. Miss Granger will join you, since I am led to believe she alone in this room is capable of ensuring the task is completed correctly, and since I have found, in twelve years of teaching, that the fastest way to cure a student of an unfounded assumption about someone's abilities is prolonged, supervised proximity to the evidence."

It was, Hermione reflected afterward, possibly the single most Slytherin punishment she had ever witnessed — technically a discipline, functionally an education, and delivered with just enough plausible deniability that Snape could claim, if pressed, that he was simply optimizing for efficient ingredient preparation.

The detention itself began in near-total silence, Malfoy slicing shrivelfig with the aggrieved precision of a boy determined not to give her the satisfaction of getting anything wrong in front of her, and Hermione measuring powdered dragon claw with the placid patience of someone who had long since stopped expecting conversation from this particular direction. It was Malfoy, unexpectedly, who broke first.

"You actually know what you're doing," he said, not quite a question, watching her measure with a scale he clearly hadn't realized required calibrating before use. "With this. Properly. Not just — swotting from a book."

"I read the book," Hermione said. "And then I practiced what the book said, repeatedly, until my hands did it without my having to think about it, which is generally how competence works, in case nobody's explained that to you before."

"Everyone's always explained it to me," Malfoy said, with a flash of something that might, on a more self-aware boy, have been resentment turned inward. "Doesn't mean I've ever had to actually — " He stopped himself, visibly annoyed at having said as much as he had, and went back to the shrivelfig with unnecessary violence.

"Had to actually do it yourself," Hermione finished for him, not unkindly. "Because someone's always been willing to do it for you, or excuse you when you didn't. That must be — " she considered her words with the same care she'd have given a difficult translation " — inconvenient, now that you're somewhere that grades you on whether the potion actually works rather than on who your father is."

"Watch it," Malfoy said, but with less heat than the words were built for.

They worked in silence for a while after that, and it was, strangely, a more companionable silence than Hermione had expected to ever share a room with Draco Malfoy in, and when he finally did speak again it was to ask, grudgingly, whether she'd found the correct technique for peeling a shrivelfig without bruising it in some book he could borrow, which was, in its own small and thoroughly Slytherin way, as close to a white flag as either of them was likely to raise that year.

Snape, checking on them near the end of the two hours, found them arguing — with genuine, engaged irritation rather than hostility — over whether the second-years' porcupine quills had been sorted correctly by size, and said nothing, but Hermione caught, just briefly, an expression on his face that looked almost like satisfaction, filed away behind the usual sourness the way a man files away a bet he's quietly pleased to have won.

It did not make them friends, not yet, not in any sense either of them would have admitted aloud to anyone. But it changed something structural in how Malfoy regarded her — from that point on his commentary lost its edge of genuine contempt and settled into something more like the sparring two people do when they've each privately conceded the other is worth sparring with, and by March he had taken, with visible reluctance, to asking her actual questions about actual coursework, in the tone of someone requesting a favor he intended to resent being grateful for.

"I still don't like you," he informed her, in the common room, in early April, apropos of nothing, after she'd pointed out — quietly, in the manner Daphne had once taught her — an error in his Transfiguration theory that would have cost him significant marks.

"I know," Hermione said. "I find that oddly reassuring. Consistency is one of the few virtues you have in real abundance."

He almost smiled. He caught himself doing it, and stopped, and went back to his essay with the specific fury of a boy discovering an unwelcome new feeling and declining, on principle, to examine it.

## **Chapter Seven: In Which the Puzzle Gets Personal**

By May, the unofficial and increasingly well-informed alliance of Hermione, Harry, and Ron had assembled, between library research, overheard staffroom fragments, and one memorably ill-advised evening spent listening at Hagrid's door while he got progressively and confessionally drunker over a growing dragon egg he should not have owned, a working theory that satisfied Hermione's rigorous standards for evidence rather better than she found comfortable.

Someone was after the Stone. Snape had been bitten by a three-headed dog and had, by Harry's account, been arguing with Quirrell in a manner that suggested threats rather than collegial disagreement. Hagrid had been persuaded, by a stranger in a pub whose face he could not quite recall under his cloak, to trade a dragon egg for information about exactly the kind of music that would put Fluffy to sleep — a fact Hagrid delivered with the crestfallen honesty of a man who has only just realized, mid-sentence, what he's done.

"It's Snape," Ron said, with the flat certainty of a boy who had disliked the man on sight and required no further evidence. "It's obviously Snape. He's been awful to all of us since September, he hates Harry for no reason anyone's ever explained, and he's the one who got hurt by the dog, which means he's the one who's already tried to get past it."

"That is precisely the kind of conclusion I'd expect someone to jump to," Hermione said, not looking up from the parchment where she'd been mapping, with color-coded ink, every scrap of evidence they'd gathered against every suspect, "which is exactly why I don't trust it. Snape has had multiple unsupervised opportunities to do something considerably worse to Harry than glare at him if that were genuinely his intention, and a man clever enough to brew what he brews doesn't get caught by his own dog on a first attempt. It's too obvious. I don't believe the universe organizes its villains for our narrative convenience."

"Then who?" Harry asked.

Hermione did not, in fact, have an answer yet, and it bothered her considerably more than she let either of them see — there is a particular flavor of frustration reserved for a puzzle that refuses to resolve on schedule, and Hermione, who had spent eight months building a reputation on always, eventually, having the answer, found this specific unsolved variable close to unbearable. She stayed up later. She reread Hagrid's account four times, cross-referenced against every book on cloaked strangers and stuttering professors the library would yield, and arrived, three days before the end of term, at a conclusion so uncomfortable she nearly didn't say it aloud.

"Quirrell," she said, finally, at breakfast, low enough that only Harry and Ron — who had, by unspoken and increasingly conspicuous arrangement, taken to finding excuses to sit near the end of the Slytherin table where she could talk to them without either house noticing too pointedly — could hear her. "It's Quirrell."

"Quirrell can barely look at a candle without flinching," Ron said. "He stutters through his own name."

"Which is either an extraordinary coincidence of temperament for a man guarding nothing of interest," Hermione said, "or an extraordinarily convenient performance for a man who does not want anyone looking at him twice. Snape's been suspicious of him for months, by your own account — not because Snape's protecting the Stone, Ron, because Snape's been trying to work out the same thing we have, probably rather better resourced and considerably more annoyed about the three of us blundering around in the middle of it."

It was, in the end, a theory that would be confirmed considerably sooner and considerably more dramatically than any of them wanted, because two days before the end of term, Hagrid let slip — devastated, the moment he realized it — that he'd told the cloaked stranger exactly how to get past Fluffy, and Harry, Ron, and Hermione realized, with the particular cold-water clarity of a deadline suddenly made real, that whoever was after the Stone would not wait for the safety of a new school year to make their attempt.

"Tonight," Hermione said, and did not enjoy, this time, being right.

## **Chapter Eight: Through the Trapdoor**

They went that night, the three of them, in a state of preparation that Hermione considered wildly insufficient and Harry and Ron, being Gryffindors, considered roughly standard operating procedure. She had, at least, insisted on bringing what she could: a satchel of ingredients she thought might prove useful, every relevant page of research she'd copied out in her own hurried shorthand, and — over Ron's initial objections that it would slow them down — a plan, of sorts, which was rather more than either boy had brought on his own.

Fluffy, disastrously, was already asleep when they arrived — someone had gotten there first, and left a harp playing itself beside the trapdoor, which told Hermione, with a lurch of real fear she did not have time to examine, that Quirrell had a head start they could not afford to waste calculating.

The trapdoor dropped them into Devil's Snare, and it was Hermione — flinging her frightened housemates' training instincts a lifetime, or at least several very stressful minutes, out the window — who kept her head while Ron, tangled and thrashing, made the plant's grip worse with every panicked movement.

"Stop moving, both of you, it reacts to struggle — " she said, already reaching for the memory of the passage she'd read on Devil's Snare not because she'd anticipated needing it here specifically but because she made a policy, these days, of reading everything, on the grounds that one never knew, precisely, when a plant might try to kill her — and produced, with hands only slightly unsteady, a small blue flame that filled the chamber with sudden, blessed light and sent the vines recoiling, releasing all three of them onto the stone floor below in an ungainly heap.

"You could've said 'it hates light' a bit sooner," Ron said, extracting a leaf from his collar with wounded dignity.

"I was getting there," Hermione said. "Some of us process information under pressure at slightly different speeds. I'll thank you not to critique my methodology while we're both still alive because of it."

The key chase, in the next chamber, was Harry's to solve — Quidditch instincts translating neatly onto broomstick pursuit of a single, deliberately battered silver key among a swarm of enchanted decoys — and Hermione, watching from the ground with her heart in her throat and a running mental commentary on flight trajectories she was too frightened to voice usefully, found herself, for possibly the first time all year, entirely without a contribution to make, and disliked the sensation intensely enough to resolve, privately, to remedy her complete ignorance of practical flying at the earliest available opportunity.

The chess set was Ron's, and it cost him more than any of them were prepared for — the calm, terrible decision to sacrifice himself as a piece, to be struck down by a stone knight twice his size so that Harry could finish the game, made with a steadiness that startled Hermione into a new and reluctant respect for a boy she had, for most of the year, filed loosely under *well-meaning but disorganized.* She knelt by him afterward, checking with shaking, businesslike hands that he was only unconscious and not worse, and found, when she looked up at Harry, that neither of them needed to say aloud how badly this had already gone.

It was the final chamber — the seven bottles, the riddle in verse, the logic puzzle standing between them and the door ahead — that was, of course, hers.

She read it twice. She felt, reading it, something settle in her chest that was almost like relief: not because the danger had lessened, but because here, finally, in a dungeon two children her own age had already been carried out of, was a problem shaped exactly like the ones she had spent eight months and a lifetime before that training herself to solve.

"It's not magic," she said, half to herself, working through the riddle's logic with her finger tracing lines only she could see. "It's logic. Whoever set this wanted to keep out someone with power and no brains, which — " a flicker of grim humor, even here " — is rather flattering to those of us who go the other way." She worked it through, out loud, for Harry's benefit and her own, eliminating bottles by process of elimination with the crisp, unhurried confidence of a girl doing something she was, at last, unambiguously built for. "This one takes you back. This one lets you forward. And this one — " she held up the smallest bottle, considering it with a scientist's detachment even as her hand trembled slightly " — is poison, which means there's exactly enough of the one that lets you through for one person."

Harry looked at the bottle, and at her, and understood the shape of the problem a half-second before she said it aloud.

"You have to go back for Ron," Hermione said. "He needs help, and I can't leave him unconscious down here alone with the fire still up behind us. You have to go on. Alone."

"Hermione — "

"I'm not being brave," she said, briskly, because she had found, over the year, that briskness was the fastest route through a feeling she didn't have time to sit with. "I'm doing the maths. You're the one he's after. You're the one who needs to be there. I am considerably more useful getting Ron to the hospital wing than I am facing whatever's through that door with no offensive spells and a bottle of research. Go. I'll see you when it's over."

Harry drank the potion that would take him forward. Hermione drank the one that would take her back, sat with Ron's head in her lap in the flickering blue firelight, and spent what felt like the longest twenty minutes of her life doing the two things she was, by now, genuinely excellent at: waiting, and thinking through every possible way the next hour might go wrong, so that whichever one actually happened, she would not be caught unprepared by it.

## **Chapter Nine: The Reputation**

Harry survived. Quirrell did not — the truth of it, when it finally circulated in careful, half-official fragments over the following days, turned out to involve rather more Voldemort than anyone at the breakfast table was emotionally prepared to hear before their toast, and Hermione, who had correctly identified the wrong professor for entirely the right reasons, found herself in the odd position of having solved most of a mystery and still been startled by its ending, which she recorded in her notebook with a rare and grudging note of humility: *Prediction accuracy: 90%. Remaining 10%: catastrophic. Revise model to account for the possibility that the actual worst-case scenario is somehow still worse than the worst case I imagined.*

The story of what had happened beneath the school spread in the usual Hogwarts fashion, which was badly, incompletely, and with wild embellishment depending on which house was doing the telling. Gryffindor's version cast Harry as the singular hero, with Ron a valiant second and Hermione — when she featured at all — as a helpful, vaguely mysterious associate whose house tie nobody quite wanted to dwell on in a triumphant narrative. Slytherin's version, once it filtered down through channels Hermione suspected began with an offhand comment from Professor Snape and gathered detail the way a snowball gathers everything in its path, cast it rather differently: a Slytherin girl, calm under pressure that had unmanned two Gryffindor boys, who had solved a logic puzzle guarding the most valuable magical object in Britain and then made the harder, colder calculation to walk away from the glory in favor of the practical necessity of saving her friend's life.

"You let him go on alone," Daphne said, cornering her — as she so often did — in the library, the week after, with an expression Hermione hadn't seen on her before: something that had graduated, finally, all the way from *recalculating* to something that might, in Daphne Greengrass's carefully rationed emotional vocabulary, be called *impressed.* "You could have gone through yourself. You'd solved the puzzle. It was your bottle to drink."

"Ron needed help immediately, and I couldn't provide any facing whatever was ahead that I couldn't provide from the past side of that fire," Hermione said. "It wasn't a sacrifice, particularly. It was the correct decision, and I made it, and if that's heroic then I suppose heroism is simply arithmetic that happens to feel dramatic in retrospect."

"That," Daphne said, "is the single most Slytherin sentence I have ever heard a person say about an act of selflessness, and I want you to know I mean that as the highest compliment this house is capable of giving." She paused. "Also, Pansy wants to know if you'll help her with her Charms essay. Properly this time. Not — the other way you used to do it."

"Tell her I'll do it after dinner," Hermione said, "and tell her if she does another impression of my speech patterns while I'm helping her, I will simply stop, and let her fail Flitwick's assessment on her own considerable merits."

"I'll tell her," Daphne said, and there was, in her voice, something that had not been there in September: not warmth, not quite, but the beginnings of something adjacent, a loyalty accumulated one correctly-solved problem at a time, the only kind of currency Hermione had ever really trusted enough to spend.

Professor Snape, awarding house points at the end-of-year feast in a speech that gave away nothing of the events beneath the school beyond a tight, unreadable acknowledgment that "certain first-years demonstrated judgment considerably in advance of their years," found reason, in the final tally, to award Slytherin a startling number of points for what the official record described only as *exceptional initiative in Potions and related coursework* — a description so carefully bloodless that only Hermione, Malfoy, Daphne, and the ghost of a bathroom troll fully understood what it was covering for. **Slytherin won the House Cup that year, for the first time in some seasons \[canon-cont: not true\]**, and the applause from the green-and-silver table, when the final points were tallied, included — grudging, astonished, and entirely genuine — Draco Malfoy's, who caught Hermione's eye across the hall and gave her the smallest possible nod, the closest thing to an apology he was constitutionally capable of producing, and which she accepted, in the same spirit, with an equally small nod back.

Ron found her on the train home, squeezing into the compartment she shared, somewhat scandalously by both houses' standards, with Harry, and said, with the particular bluntness of a boy who had spent a year revising an opinion and had finally landed somewhere solid, "You're the best witch in our year. Maybe the best in the whole school. I don't care what tie you wear."

"I know," Hermione said, and then, because total honesty had become something of a habit this year, "thank you. That means more than I probably ought to let on."

"You'll probably end up running the Ministry or terrorizing it, one or the other," Harry said, "and I genuinely can't tell which."

"Neither can I, yet," Hermione admitted, watching the countryside blur past the window, green giving way to grey giving way to the first grimy outskirts of London. "I suppose we'll all find out together."

**Epilogue: A Difference of Opinion**

In a small office lined with portraits who slept through most conversations out of professional courtesy, the Sorting Hat sat on its shelf, mid-argument, with a headmaster who had asked, not for the first time, whether it stood by its decision back in September.

*I have had eleven hundred years of practice,* the Hat said, with the particular patience of an old thing that had answered this question, in one form or another, every year since its creation. *I do not sort children into what would make a tidy story, Albus. I sort them into what is true, and let the story catch up.*

"And if the story is a difficult one?"

*Then it was always going to be difficult, wherever I put her,* said the Hat. *The difference is only whether she spends her difficulty proving she belongs, or spending it becoming someone nobody can afford to say doesn't. I know which one produces a better witch. Ask me again in six years. I suspect you'll already know the answer, and I suspect you won't much like how loudly the rest of the wizarding world agrees with me.*

Dumbledore, who had learned, over a very long life, not to argue with hats that had been right rather more often than he had, poured them both — himself and the empty air where a second cup of tea might have gone, out of old habit — a fresh pot, and said nothing further on the matter that evening.

He would, however, keep a rather closer eye than usual on the Slytherin end of the Great Hall from that September onward, and would find, more often than not, that his eye landed on the same small, fiercely composed, endlessly footnoted girl — and that he was, on the whole, glad of it.

**— END —**

## **Plot Summary**

Hermione Granger boards the Hogwarts Express having memorized every set text twice and is sorted, to the visible horror of the Great Hall, into Slytherin. The Hat's reasoning (which it shares with irritating smugness): ambition sharp enough to cut glass, a near-pathological need to win, and a self-preservation instinct any snake would envy. Gryffindor sulks. Slytherin does not cheer either — a Muggle-born in the ancient and noble house of blood purists goes over like a Weasley at a wine tasting.

The first term is rough. Her own housemates freeze her out or needle her (Draco Malfoy elects himself her personal tormentor, which backfires steadily and hilariously as she out-argues, out-grades, and out-manoeuvres him at every turn), while Gryffindors — including a betrayed, confused Ron — treat her like a defector. Only Harry, who knows what it's like to be sorted into a house you didn't expect and mocked for reasons outside your control, keeps quiet lines of contact open. Hermione responds to universal suspicion the way she responds to everything: with research. If nobody will vouch for her, she'll simply become undeniable. She buries herself in the library, strikes up a wary, transactional friendship with a bemused Daphne Greengrass and a Slytherin fifth-year prefect who admires efficiency over ideology, and starts quietly correcting professors in class, which the Slytherins find far more entertaining than they expected.

The canonical plot beats still happen, refracted through a Slytherin lens: she solves the troll incident from the dungeons side (and gets no thanks for it), needles her way into the Philosopher's Stone mystery not out of loyalty to Gryffindor's trio but because an unsolved puzzle offends her personally, and ends up begrudgingly, chaotically allied with Harry and Ron for the climax — to everyone's discomfort, herself included. Malfoy, forced to team up with her at one point (Snape's idea, delivered as punishment to them both), discovers she's insufferable to duel against and, worse, kind of likes it.

By the end of the year she hasn't won anyone's uncomplicated affection — Slytherin still finds her tactless and terrifyingly earnest, Gryffindor still finds her unnerving — but she has won something better: a reputation. People start choosing their words around her carefully. Professors stop calling on her not because she's wrong but because she's never once wrong and it's exhausting. The book ends with the Sorting Hat, glimpsed in Dumbledore's office, muttering that it stands by its decision, thanks very much.

Across the rest of the (implied, unwritten) series arc, this sets Hermione on a trajectory distinct from canon: outsider status forges her into someone who trusts competence over belonging, who collects debts and favors instead of friends at first, and who — by fifth or sixth year — is the witch other students quietly warn first-years not to cross, while the staff exchange the specific, weary glances reserved for a student they suspect could dismantle the wards if she got sufficiently annoyed. Fiercely loyal once earned, ruthless when crossed, and never, ever short of a citation to back up why she was right. The humor stays load-bearing throughout: petty magical sabotage, spectacularly overprepared homework as a power move, Slytherin house meetings that are 40% politics and 60% Hermione correcting people's Latin.
