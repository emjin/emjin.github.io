# **Chapter One — A Girl With Too Many Books**

The road to Lightsbridge smelled of chalk dust and cabbages, and Trisana Chandler disliked both.

She had walked the last mile from the carriage-stop by choice, because after four days boxed up with a snoring wool-factor and his wife she wanted the wind on her face more than she wanted to arrive with dignity. The wind obliged. It came down off the northern hills tasting of late apples and of **rain that would not fall until evening \[prose: this detail is not worth the number of words that were spent on it\]**, and it wound itself around her braids the way a cat winds round an ankle, hoping to be noticed.

“Not here,” Tris told it under her breath. “Not now. Behave.”

The wind sulked and let go. In the braids over her left ear, where she kept a **coil of it against emergencies, something small settled and went still \[canon-cont: Tris’s braid winds aren’t small\]**.

Ahead, Lightsbridge rose out of the river-plain like a scholar's untidy desk: towers of grey stone leaning companionably together, roofs pitched at forty stubborn angles, and over all of it the great glass dome of the University library, catching the afternoon light and throwing it back in a hundred pieces. Tris stopped in the road to look, and for a moment forgot to be prickly. **She had crossed half the world and read her way through a good part of it, and still the sight of that much knowledge under one roof made something in her chest go tight and hungry. \[+canon-char: that’s very Tris\]**

**Against her ribs, under her cloak, a cool weight stirred \[+prose: nice transition\]**. **A narrow glass head slid out at her collar \[+canon-char: I like this description of how Chime moves\]**, delicate as spun sugar, and two glass eyes fixed on the dome with what Tris could only call professional interest.

“No,” said Tris.

“I mean it. **You are a lapdog. \[exec: the plan is for Chime to pretend to be a lapdog? I cannot imagine that working\]** A very ordinary, very silent lapdog, and you are staying under my cloak until I say otherwise. We agreed.”

They had not agreed. One did not so much agree with Chime as negotiate a ceasefire, but the little dragon condescended to pour herself back into the warm dark, and Tris pulled her plain brown cloak straight over the lump of her. Plain brown was the whole of the plan. **Plain brown cloak, plain grey dress \[canon-cont: and spelled by Sandry, surely?\]**, spectacles mended at one hinge with a twist of wire. A merchant's girl of no particular family, come to study — like the thousand other hopeful children who washed up at Lightsbridge every autumn, each one privately certain of being the cleverest person their village had ever produced.

# **Chapter Two — The Slow Way**

By the end of her first week, Tris had learned something she had not learned in years, and it was this: she was stupid.

Not at everything. She could still out-read anyone in the room, hold six facts in her head where others held two, and do sums in the dark. But academic magic — the actual doing of it — was a wall she kept walking into nose-first, and the reason was maddeningly simple. She had too much magic, and it wanted to help.

“The sign is drawn once, cleanly, from the heart outward,” said Master Coary, a mild round man who taught Elementary Signs and had the patience of a man who has watched a great many children set fire to a great many things. “The syllable is spoken on the outbreath. The working follows the sign and the word. Not your feeling about the working. Not your wish for it. The sign and the word. Try again, Chandler.”

In front of Tris sat a plain white candle. The task was to light it — the very first thing any Lightsbridge child learned, easier than tying a shoe. Draw the fire-sign, speak the syllable, and the borrowed structure of the spell would reach out and wake the wick.

Tris drew the sign. She spoke the syllable. And her own power, delighted to be asked to do anything at all after a week of being sat on, surged up her arm without being invited and lit the candle, the candle beside it, and a faint blue crown of flame along the rim of the desk that she had to smother with both sleeves.

“Interesting,” said Master Coary, in the tone of a man who has decided not to ask. “Rather more than a candle. You are — forcing it, I think. Let the spell do the work. You need only permit it.”

Permit it. That was the whole trick, and it was the one thing four years of discipline had trained out of her. Ambient magic did not ask permission; it lived in her bones and her blood and the coil of wind in her braids, and everything she knew was bent toward controlling a power that would otherwise flatten a village. To do academic magic she had to stand her own magic down entirely, get out of the spell's way, and trust a structure someone else had built. It felt like learning to walk by forgetting she had legs.

She loved it. That was the humiliating part. She hated being bad at it and she loved it anyway, the cleanness of it, the way a spell did exactly and only what its maker decided it would do, no more, no temper, no weather. She could have wept with wanting it.

♦  ♦  ♦

The boy at the next desk had the opposite problem, which was that he had, as far as Tris could tell, no magic that could find its own feet in the dark.

His name was Osk. He was built like a friendly door, cheerful past all reason, the son of a Sarrin cartwright, and he could recite the theory of the fire-sign backward, forward, and in verse, and could not for the life of him make a candle so much as smoke. “It's in there,” he said gloomily, poking his own broad chest, the third time his candle sat cold. “Doctor Nairn tested me, there's magic in me, it's just — shy. Yours isn't shy.” He looked at the scorch-mark on her desk with open envy. “Yours is the opposite of shy.”

“Mine is a menace,” Tris said, and Osk laughed his enormous laugh, and that was that; they were friends. It was not a thing Tris had ever known how to do on purpose. It kept happening to her at Lightsbridge anyway, the way rain happens, and she was too honest with herself not to notice that she liked it.

Elu adopted Osk on sight, on the grounds that anyone that large and that sad about candles needed feeding, and by the end of the second week the three of them had a table in the corner of the refectory that was understood to be theirs. Tris kept Chime in a covered basket under it and fed her the chips from the glaziers' yard, and Chime, who could have exposed the entire arrangement with a single note, developed an unexpected talent for discretion whenever there was warm bread on offer.

♦  ♦  ♦

The library lived under the great glass dome, and the first time Tris walked in properly she stopped dead in the doorway for the second time in a week.

It went up and up, gallery over gallery, tier over tier, more books than she had known were in the world, and the dome poured afternoon down through them all in long coloured bars. She stood there with her mouth slightly open until a voice said, dryly, from somewhere near her elbow, “It does that to everyone. You'll want to move, though, or they shelve you.”

The boy was about her own age, narrow and ink-stained, with a stack of rebound volumes balanced against one hip and the particular unhurried quiet of a person who has spent more of his life with pages than people. “Vared,” he said, when she went on staring, this time at him. “I mind the eastern galleries. Bookbinder's son — they let me work off my fees mending the ones people love to death.” He nodded at the satchel dragging her shoulder down. “You're the one with the library on her back. Half the second-years have a wager on what's in it.”

“Let them lose money,” said Tris. But she opened the satchel, because he had said the one word — books — in the one tone that got past her guard, and within ten minutes they were arguing happily about a marginal note in a treatise on ward-theory that Vared insisted was a scribe's error and Tris insisted was the cleverest thing on the page.

“It's not an error,” she said. “Look — he's saying the old anchor-wards don't fail all at once, they fail politely, one stone at a time, and the records never catch it because the whole structure still tests sound right up until the hour it doesn't. That's not a mistake. That's a warning nobody read.”

Vared looked at the page, and then at her, with the first real attention she had seen from him. “You read ward-theory for pleasure.”

“I read everything for pleasure,” said Tris. “It's the only reliable pleasure there is.”

He smiled at that — a slow, surprised, private sort of smile — and pulled a stool over without being asked, and they read until the coloured bars of light climbed the far wall and went out. It was, Tris thought later, lying awake under the eaves with Chime chiming softly in her sleep, one of the better afternoons of her life. She did not examine the thought too closely. She was not, whatever else she was, a fool about herself.

♦  ♦  ♦

Master Girt taught Fundamental Theory on the days the sun most deserved to be walked in, and he was, infuriatingly, superb at it. Tris took more notes in his lectures than in any other, and hated him steadily throughout, because he could not go a full hour without stepping on her whole life.

“The ambient mage,” he said that week, tapping a diagram of the University's own famous wards, “feels the world. We measure it. When the wards beneath this very dome were laid three hundred years ago, they were laid by academic mages, by calculation, by record — and they have held for three centuries precisely because no one has been permitted to feel his way around them.” He said feel the way another man might say soil his hands. “Sentiment is not structure. If you take nothing else from this hall, take that.”

Tris wrote down every word about the wards and none of the sneer. Under the floor, faint and steady, the thread of hot metal and burnt air was still there. It had been there all week. It was, she was fairly sure, a very little stronger than it had been the day she arrived. She looked at Girt's beautiful diagram of the three-hundred-year-old wards that no one had been permitted to feel, and she thought of the marginal note — they fail politely, one stone at a time — and something cold turned over in her stomach.

She said nothing. She was here to be nobody. She had promised.

But that night she opened the shutters over her bed, and let the wind in properly for the first time since the road, and asked it, very quietly, what it had smelled under the dome.

# **Chapter Three — Something in the Air**

The trouble with letting the wind in was that it never brought only one thing.

It brought her the kitchen fires and the river-fog and a cart overturned two streets away and a pair of second-years courting badly in a doorway, all at once, the whole gossiping breath of the town. Tris had spent years learning to sort that flood, to lift one strand out of it the way you pull one thread from a skein, and she lay awake half the night doing it: sorting, discarding, reaching down under the noise of the living town toward the deep quiet stone of the University and the thing that did not belong there.

Hot metal. Burnt air. And under it, so faint she nearly lost it a dozen times, a sound that was not a sound — the particular strained groan of a spell being made to carry more than it was built for. It came from below the library. From the undercroft, where the ward-stones were.

By morning she was gritty-eyed and certain, and certainty, in Tris, was a thing with edges.

♦  ♦  ♦

She took it, because she had promised not to make trouble and this seemed the least troublesome door, to Doctor Nairn.

Doctor Nairn was old in the way of good knives — worn thin and gone very sharp. She taught nothing and examined everyone; it was she who tested each new student's magic in their first week, and she had tested Tris behind a closed door and said only, afterward, “Well,” in a tone Tris had been turning over ever since. Her study was up a crooked stair and full of instruments that measured things Tris did not have names for.

“You think the anchor-wards are failing,” Nairn said, when Tris had laid it out — carefully, in the language of a clever first-year who had merely read a great deal, keeping the wind entirely out of it. “Because you read a marginal note. And because you have a feeling.”

“Because the note describes exactly the kind of failure that wouldn't show in the records,” said Tris, “and the records are the only thing anyone here trusts.”

“That is a good argument,” Nairn allowed. “It is also, I am obliged to tell you, not evidence. The wards test sound. They tested sound last month; Master Girt himself signed the survey. In this University, child, a feeling that a thing is wrong is worth precisely nothing until you can put a number on it.” She held Tris's eyes a moment longer than was comfortable. “I would find the number, if I were you. Before I troubled anyone with the feeling.” It was not quite a dismissal. It was, Tris realized on the stair going down, something rarer: an old woman telling her exactly where the door was, and declining to notice her walk through it.

♦  ♦  ♦

She did not go alone, in the end, because her friends declined to allow it.

She had meant to. **She had spent sixteen years being the one who went alone. \[canon-cont: excuse me???\]** But she had made the mistake of thinking aloud over the corner table in the refectory, and Elu had said “The undercroft?” far too loudly, and Osk had said “I'm coming, I'm enormous, I'm useful,” and Vared had simply set down his tea and said “I have the key to the eastern stair. I mend the books they keep down there,” in the tone of a man reporting the weather, and that had rather settled it.

“It isn't a game,” Tris warned them. “If I'm right, there's a working going on down there that shouldn't be, and whoever's running it won't want to be found.”

“Then it's a very good thing,” said Elu serenely, tucking her healer's satchel over her shoulder, “that you'll have a healer, a door, and a boy with a key. What have you got, Tris?”

Books, thought Tris. A glass dragon. A drought in my hands and a hall full of people who don't know it. “I've got a bad temper,” she said aloud, “and good hearing,” and Chime, in her basket, chimed agreement in a note pitched exactly like a lock turning over.

♦  ♦  ♦

The near-disaster came before they ever reached the undercroft, and it came in Master Coary's mild classroom, over a candle.

A boy three rows up — nervous, clumsy, showing off for a girl — drew a fire-sign far too large and spoke the syllable far too hard, reaching for a flame that would impress. The borrowed structure of the spell, given that much sloppy hunger to feed on, buckled. Tris felt it go the way you feel a stair that isn't there: the working folded inward on itself and began, in the space of a breath, to pull heat out of everything around it — the desks, the air, the boy's own startled hands — gathering to a point that would, in another breath, let go all at once and take the front of the room with it.

She did not decide to move. Her power decided for her. It always had, in the half-second before disaster, which was the whole reason she had spent her life chaining it — and now it slipped the chain, reached across the room without a sign or a syllable, and simply took the runaway heat, drank it down, and folded the whole failing working shut as neatly as closing a book.

The candle went out. The boy sat down hard. And Tris sat very still with a stranger's stolen heat pooling under her ribs and her heart going like a hammer, because half the room had turned to look at the front of it — and not one of them had seen where the working had gone, except one.

Vared, two seats over, was looking not at the boy, and not at the doused candle, but at her. At her hands, flat and white on the desk. At the frost of gooseflesh that a great pull of heat leaves on the skin of the one who took it.

He did not say anything. Master Coary was already scolding the clumsy boy, the moment sliding shut over itself, the room exhaling. But after, in the corridor, Vared fell into step beside her and said, low, “That spell was going to go. I've seen one go before — it took a man's hand. This one didn't go.” He was quiet a moment. “I don't know what you are, Tris Chandler. But you're not a first-year who reads a lot.”

Tris's whole body went to the readiness it knew best, the flat cold place from which she lied to strangers and lightning alike. And then she looked at him — ink-stained, unhurried, entirely unfrightened, watching her with nothing in his face but a kind of careful, patient wanting-to-know — and the cold place, unaccountably, would not close.

“Not here,” she said, which was the truth. “I'll tell you. Not here.”

“All right,” said Vared, as if she had promised him nothing more alarming than a book, and did not ask again. It was, Tris thought, the single most disarming thing anyone had done to her in years — to want to know, and then simply to wait. She walked the rest of the corridor beside him not saying anything at all, and found, to her deep suspicion, that she did not want to walk faster.

# **Chapter Four — The Reading of the Winds**

That night Tris did the thing she did not do lightly, the thing that had frightened even Niko a little the first time he saw it: she read the winds.

It was a working with no sign and no syllable, nothing academic in it at all, and it would have made Master Girt's skin crawl. She sat on the roof of the east dormitory with her braids unbound and her eyes half shut, and she let the wind pour through her open hands and read the pictures folded into it — not the future exactly, but the shape of things moving toward it, the way you can read from a river's swirl where the drowned rock lies.

Chime sat on the ridgetile beside her, gone still and grave, wings furled. Below them the town breathed and the great dome caught the last grey light and, deep beneath it, the wrong thing groaned.

The wind gave it to her all at once, and it was worse than she had feared.

She saw the undercroft: the ring of ancient ward-stones that held the dome and everything under it. She saw that they were failing exactly as the dead scribe had warned — politely, one stone at a time, three of them already gone dark and dead, the rest carrying a weight they were never built to carry alone. And she saw why. In a chamber hard against the ward-ring, someone had set up a working of their own: a slow, greedy, brilliant heat-working, an alchemist's forge-spell drawing power up out of the deep stone to feed itself, night after night, month after month. It was not the wards' age that was killing them. It was that someone had been quietly drinking from the same well.

And she saw when it would end. She saw the undercroft full of light and heat, and above it the great glass dome hanging over a floor packed shoulder to shoulder with the whole University — gowns, banners, the long tables of a feast. Convocation. The autumn convocation, three days off, when every soul in Lightsbridge crowded in under the glass to watch the year's new mages take their oaths.

Tris came back into her own body with a wrench, cold to the bone, Chime keening softly against her neck. Three days. She had a number now. She had the whole shape of it. And she had exactly one place a first-year was allowed to take such a thing.

♦  ♦  ♦

She took it to Master Girt, because he had signed the survey, because the wards were his, because there was no time left to be clever about doors.

She was careful. She had rehearsed it half the night — no wind in it, no feeling, only the marginal note, the theory of polite failure, the observation (she did not say how observed) of a heat-working drawing on the deep stone, the arithmetic of three dark stones and a crowded convocation. She laid it before him in his beautiful cold study like a clerk laying down accounts, and she watched his handsome face move from irritation to amusement to something worse.

“Let me be sure I understand you,” said Master Girt. “A first-year. Six weeks enrolled. Wishes to inform me that the wards I surveyed with my own hand last month — the wards that have held three hundred years — are three days from failing. On the strength of a marginal note.” He set his fingertips together. “Tell me, Chandler. How exactly did you observe a heat-working in a sealed undercroft you have never entered?”

And there it was: the one question she could not answer without the wind, and the wind was the one thing she could not give him.

“I can feel it,” she said, because it was true, and because she was out of clever.

Something shifted in Girt's face — a flash of pure, delighted contempt, the look of a man handed exactly the proof he wanted. “You feel it.” He sat back. “Do you know, I might have guessed. You have the whole cast of it — the certainty, the sentiment, the conviction that a feeling outranks three centuries of record.” He said the word again, savoring it: “Ambient. You've been reading their nonsense, and now you feel the wards failing, the way a peasant feels rain in his knee. I will say this slowly, so you carry it out of here: a feeling is not evidence. It is superstition with good posture. You will not go near the undercroft, you will not repeat this to another soul, and you will not, under any circumstance, interfere with the convocation of this University. Is that understood?”

Tris stood very still. The coil of wind in her braids had gone tight enough to sing, and under her ribs the drought and the lightning and every accredited scrap of her strained toward telling him precisely, in a voice he would feel in his teeth, what she was — to watch that glossy certainty come apart in his hands.

She thought of a hall full of gowns under a hanging dome of glass. She thought: if I tell him now, in temper, he'll spend two days proving I lied about who I am instead of one hour checking his wards, and people will die of his pride and my temper both.

“Understood,” she said, and turned, and walked out, and did not slam the door, which was the hardest working she had done since she arrived.

♦  ♦  ♦

They went down the eastern stair that same night, all four of them and a dragon, because there were three days left and Tris had run out of permitted doors.

Vared's key turned in the old lock. The undercroft opened cold and vast beneath the library, forested with pillars, and at the heart of it stood the ring of ward-stones — and Tris did not need the wind now, because anyone could feel it here: three of the great stones stood dark and dead as cinders, and the rest were fever-hot to the hand, humming with a strain that set Chime shivering out a thin continuous note of distress.

And hard against the ring, in a chamber that should have been empty, they found the working. It was beautiful, in its way — a scholar's forge-spell, precise and patient and utterly heedless, sunk into the deep stone like a tick into a vein, drinking. Beside it, on a slate, in a neat academic hand, were months of notes: temperatures, yields, refinements. A signature at the bottom of the first page. A graduate student. One of Girt's own, running a brilliant unlicensed experiment in the one place in the University no one was permitted to feel their way around — and never once imagining that the well he drank from was the same well that held the roof up.

“Oh,” said Elu softly, in the voice of a healer looking at a wound the patient doesn't know he has. “He doesn't even know. Does he. He thinks it's free.”

“It's never free,” said Tris. She looked up at the low stone ceiling, and past it, in her mind's eye, at the great glass dome and the empty hall that in three days would not be empty. Behind her glasses her grey eyes had gone the flat pale color of a sky before hail. “We have two days to make someone with authority feel this. And I've just spent my one chance at authority being told I have superstition with good posture.”

Chime, on her shoulder, lifted her glass head and sang — low and sure and entirely unafraid — a single sustained note that was, note for note, the deep straining groan of the failing wards themselves. She had been listening. She had it perfectly. And Tris, hearing her own worst fear given back to her in a voice made of glass, felt the last of her caution go quiet and cold and certain.

“All right,” she said. “Then we do it the loud way. And gods help Master Girt's posture.”

# **Chapter Five — The Storm She Kept**

They did try, first, the quiet ways. They had two days and they spent every hour of them.

They found the graduate student — Perrin, his name was, thin and brilliant and exhausted — and Tris told him plainly what his forge-spell was drinking from, and watched him laugh, and then stop laughing, and then refuse to believe it, because to believe it was to have been, for eight careful months, catastrophically wrong. They went back to Doctor Nairn, who listened to the number and the notes and the slate and said, grimly, “Now that is evidence,” and carried it to the faculty herself — who agreed, with academic thoroughness, to convene a review of the ward-survey the week after convocation. The week after. Tris had stood in that paneled room and said the wards will fail during convocation, not after it, and watched two dozen learned faces decline, politely, to feel it.

So when the day came, they did the only thing left. They stationed themselves. Osk by the great west doors, to hold them open against the crush. Elu near the front with her healer's satchel and a plan for the wounded she prayed she would not need. Vared in the undercroft stair with a lamp, watching the stones. And Tris in the gallery above the packed hall, with her braids unbound, and a glass dragon on her shoulder, and the shutters of a high window open at her back to let the sky in.

Below her, the whole of Lightsbridge sat down to feast under three hundred years of glass.

♦  ♦  ♦

It began, as the scribe had promised, politely.

Tris felt the fourth stone die — a small dark lurch, far down, like a missed heartbeat. Then the fifth, faster. The weight the dead stones had carried came slamming onto the living ones, and she felt those begin to go too, all at once now, the polite failure turning headlong, and deep beneath the feasting hall Perrin's beautiful greedy forge-spell, its well pulled suddenly dry, convulsed and let go every scrap of hoarded heat at once, straight up, into the roots of the dome.

Fire bloomed in the undercroft. The great glass dome, three centuries old, took the heat into itself and began, with a sound like the sky clearing its throat, to crack.

Someone screamed. A pane fell — a long blade of glass, turning end over end, bright as a dropped star, down toward the crowded tables.

And Trisana Chandler stopped hiding.

♦  ♦  ♦

She caught the falling glass on a fist of wind.

It was the smallest thing she did and it was the thing that gave her away, because a blade of glass the length of a man does not simply stop in the air and hang there and drift, gently, sideways, to rest against a pillar — not unless someone with a great deal of power and no time left for secrets has reached up out of a gallery and caught it. Two thousand faces turned. Up in the high window at her back the open sky felt her call it and came.

She pulled the storm off the northern hills the way she had pulled it once over Tharios, the way she had been aching to since the road — the whole grey weight of the evening's rain, dragged bodily across the miles and down through the breaking dome, because glass and fire and a packed hall wanted, more than anything on earth, water. It came in a roaring cold cataract through the cracking panes, drowning the flames climbing the dome's ribs, and where the water was not fast enough the wind was, snatching the falling shards sideways, herding them away from the upturned faces, sweeping the aisles clear toward the doors that Osk, gods bless his enormous shoulders, was holding open with his whole body.

“OUT,” Tris shouted, and her voice came out of the storm the way weather-mages' voices do, everywhere at once, impossible to disobey. “EVERYONE OUT. THE DOORS. NOW.”

And into the roar and the terror Chime flung herself off Tris's shoulder and flew the length of the burning dome, blazing, and gave the panicking crowd the one thing a panicking crowd cannot make for itself — direction. She sang the west doors. She sang them in Osk's booming voice, bellowing Here\! This way\! Here\!, and in Elu's steady healer's call, and in the deep sure groan of the dying wards turned somehow, in her glass throat, into a sound that said run and not a sound that said despair. She flew ahead of the crush singing the way out, a small bright unafraid thing under a sky of falling fire, and the crowd followed her voice because it was the only voice in the hall that was not screaming.

Tris held the storm.

She stood in the gallery with her arms up and her braids streaming lightning — she had let it out at last, all of it, the coil she had carried since the road cracking down her hands — and she held the rain over the fire and the wind under the falling glass and the whole roaring shape of a disaster back, by main force, by every scrap of the power she had spent six weeks pretending she did not have. It was the hardest working of her life and she was, distantly, aware that she was weeping, and that it was not from fear. It was that she had been so careful for so long, and it turned out that this — this, hands full of storm, a hall emptying to safety under her held breath — was the truest thing she knew how to do, and she had come six hundred miles to a school for the quiet magic precisely so that she would never have to do it again.

Down in the undercroft Vared and Perrin together shut the forge-spell's throat and choked the fire at its root. Up in the hall the last of the crowd stumbled out through the west doors into the rain-lashed dark. And when the hall below her was empty — empty, every soul of it out and living — Tris let the storm go.

It went up and out through the ruined dome and back to the hills where it belonged, grumbling, and the lightning drained out of her braids, and the great glass dome of Lightsbridge, cracked and steaming and standing, dripped rain onto two thousand abandoned chairs.

In the sudden enormous quiet, Tris Chandler stood alone in the gallery, soaked to the skin, shaking, entirely and forever no longer nobody in particular — and looked down into the upturned, streaming, astonished face of Master Rowan Girt, who had felt, at last, exactly what an ambient mage was for.

# **Chapter Six — Accredited**

They gave her a week to recover and then they did not know what to do with her, which Tris found, on the whole, more tiring than the storm.

The trouble was procedural, and Lightsbridge was a place that felt about procedure the way other people felt about their children. A first-year student had reached into the University's oldest working and saved the better part of two thousand lives; that was one fact. That the first-year was an accredited master mage, older in credential than half the faculty, who had enrolled under a sealed file reading none declared — that was another fact, and it sat in the middle of every meeting like a boulder in a doorway. Mistress Anwyll produced the file. The faculty read it. And a great many learned people discovered at once that they had, six weeks ago, been marking the homework of someone qualified to examine them.

Doctor Nairn found it all extremely funny, in her dry knife of a way. “I knew the week I tested you,” she told Tris, over tea in the crooked study. “One does not mistake that much sea for a rain-barrel. I said Well, and I meant, well, here is a great deal of trouble arriving on small feet, and I was right, and I have never enjoyed being right so much in my life.” She refilled Tris's cup. “You'll have noticed they want to give you things now. A chair. A title. An honorary this, a special that. To make the boulder fit the doorway.”

“I've noticed,” said Tris.

“And?”

“And I came here to learn to light a candle without setting fire to the desk,” said Tris, “and I still can't do it. So I'll thank them to keep their chair, and let me keep my seat. In the back. Where the candles are.” Nairn laughed until she had to put her cup down.

♦  ♦  ♦

Master Girt came to find her himself, which she had not expected, and which cost him something she could see.

He found her in the library under the cracked dome, where the glaziers were up on their scaffolds and the coloured light came down now through a great healing seam of new glass. She was mending a book with Vared — badly, being all thumbs at it, which he found endlessly and quietly amusing — and she looked up, and there was Rowan Girt, elegant as ever and, for the first time since she had met him, unsure of his own beautiful voice.

“Mistress Chandler,” he said. Not Chandler. Mistress. She let him have the silence to feel it. “I have signed that ward-survey for nine years,” he said at last. “I signed it a month ago. I stood in this hall and told a room full of children that a feeling was superstition with good posture.” His jaw worked. “You had the feeling. You were correct. I had the record, and the record was a corpse that had not yet noticed it was dead. I find I do not much care for what that says about my three hundred years.”

Tris could have had him then. He had laid his neck right out on the block, and six weeks of weather-vanes with opinions were stacked up behind her teeth wanting to fall on it. She thought about it. She genuinely did.

“You were half right,” she said instead. “A feeling isn't evidence. You were dead right about that, and if I'd had two more days I'd have got you a number instead of a feeling, and you'd have believed me, and no one would have had to watch me pull a storm through your roof.” She pushed her mended, lumpy, disgraceful book across the table. “But a record isn't the world either. It's just the last time somebody looked. Feeling's for knowing where to look. Numbers are for proving it. You need both, or you get —” she nodded up at the great seam of new glass “— that. I'd have thought a man who loves precision as much as you do would want the half of it he's missing.”

Girt looked at her for a long moment. Then, stiffly, as though the words had to be got out one at a time past three centuries of posture, he said: “Would you consider — when you are recovered — addressing my Fundamental Theory hall. On the reading of a structure that tests sound. From the ambient side.” He paused. “They should hear it from someone who can do both. I find, to my considerable annoyance, that I cannot.”

“I'll think about it,” said Tris, which from her was practically a yes, and Girt, who had somewhere in the last week learned to hear her, inclined his head and left them to their books. Vared watched him go. “you let him off,” he observed.

“I let him improve,” said Tris. “It's worse for him. He'll have to feel things now.”

♦  ♦  ♦

She stayed, in the end. That was the part no one at Lightsbridge quite understood — that a master mage who could haul storms across the sky would sit through Elementary Signs a second term, red-faced over a candle, permitting a borrowed spell to do the small clean work her own great power kept trying to snatch out of its hands. But Tris understood it, and that was enough.

Because it turned out she had been right, on the road, cold and hungry and staring at a dome full of light. She had wanted to be a beginner. Not a marvel, not a curiosity, not the youngest and strangest thing in the room — just a girl who was bad at something and getting better, slowly, the ordinary way, with friends at the corner table and a dragon under it stealing the bread. The storm had not changed that. If anything the storm had proved it: she already had the loud magic, all of it, and it had never once made her happy. This might.

Osk still couldn't light a candle; he had decided, cheerfully, to become a theorist instead, on the grounds that the world needed people who understood magic almost as much as people who could do it, and Tris privately thought he'd be better at it than half the faculty. Elu had been offered a place training under the University's chief healer, on the strength of a night's work in a burning hall, and had accepted so loudly that Chime sang it back to her for a week. And Chime herself had become, to Tris's disgust and secret pride, the most famous creature in Lightsbridge — the glass dragon who sang two thousand people out of the fire — and gave up entirely on the pretense of being a lapdog, which had never once fooled anyone.

And Vared. Vared mended the books people loved to death, and read ward-theory with her under the new glass on the long grey afternoons, and had not once, in all the noise of what she turned out to be, looked at her as though she were a marvel instead of a girl. On the evening the last pane of the dome went in, they sat in the eastern gallery watching the glaziers pack up, not talking, which was a thing they were both good at, and he put his ink-stained hand flat on the table not quite touching hers, and left it there, and Tris — who had faced down lightning and drought and Master Rowan Girt — looked at that hand for a long, cowardly, delighted moment, and then moved hers the last half-inch to rest against it.

She did not examine the thought too closely. She was not, whatever else she was, a fool about herself. But she let her hand stay where it was, under the mended dome, in the school she had chosen, and thought that being nobody in particular had been, on balance, the best idea she'd ever had — right up until the moment it wasn't, and even then.

Above them the new glass held the last of the light and let it down gently, in a hundred coloured pieces, over the quietest mage at Lightsbridge, who was, at long last, exactly as ordinary as she had always wanted to be, and not one scrap more.

THE END
