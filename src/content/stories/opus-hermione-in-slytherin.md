# **A Granger Among Serpents**

*A Harry Potter fan fiction*

---

On September 1, 1991, the Sorting Hat spends four and a half minutes on Hermione Granger's head — the longest sorting in living memory — before shouting **SLYTHERIN**. The Hat had wanted Gryffindor. Hermione, who had read *Hogwarts: A History* twice, informed it that Gryffindor was statistically the house most likely to get her killed by age fourteen, and that she intended to *survive* magical Britain, thank you. The Hat, impressed and slightly frightened, obliged.

This one change bends everything.

## **Chapter One: The Longest Sorting in Living Memory**

Hermione Granger had prepared for Hogwarts the way other children prepared for the apocalypse.

She had read *Hogwarts: A History*. Then she had read it again, this time taking notes. She had read *Modern Magical History*, *The Rise and Fall of the Dark Arts*, and — because it seemed prudent — a small volume called *Great Wizarding Disasters and the Fools Who Caused Them*, which had turned out to be less a history and more a warning. By the time she boarded the Hogwarts Express she had assembled, in her mind, **something close to a survival strategy \[canon-char: this is quite far from the optimistic attitude Hermione canonically started with towards Hogwarts, I’d prefer a few lines of explanation to justify the deviance\]**, and the strategy had one central pillar.

Do not, under any circumstances, be interesting to the wrong people.

This had already gone poorly. She had spent the train ride helping a round-faced boy look for his toad, which was fine, and then correcting a redheaded boy's pronunciation of a spell, which she suspected was not, and by the time the carriages rattled up to the castle she had a nagging sense that she had introduced herself to approximately thirty strangers as *that girl*. She resolved to be quieter. **The resolution lasted until the Great Hall \[+char: I like that Hermione knows she’s a lot but can’t repress it\]**, where she looked up at a ceiling enchanted to mimic the night sky and said, out loud, to no one, "It's charmed. It's in the book, it's charmed, it doesn't actually — oh, it's *lovely*."

So much for quiet.

They were herded to the front by a witch in emerald robes who introduced herself as Professor McGonagall in a voice suggesting she had introduced herself to a great many children and did not expect to remember any of them. A stool was produced. Upon the stool was set a hat so old and so patched it looked less like an object than like a decision several generations of moths had come to regret. Hermione had read about this too. She knew what it did. She had, in fact, spent a not-insignificant amount of the summer wondering where it would put her, and had concluded — with the quiet confidence of a girl who had never once been wrong about anything she could look up — that it would put her in Gryffindor.

Gryffindor was where the brave went. Gryffindor was where the *heroes* went. ## **Chapter Two: The Rules of the House**

The Slytherin common room was underground, underwater, and — Hermione decided within her first hour of living there — underhanded, **which she felt made three for three and boded well for consistency if nothing else \[prose: what?\]**.

It was a long, low room beneath the lake, lit green by the water pressing at the windows, and it was beautiful in the **specific way of things designed to remind you that you were beneath the surface and could not easily leave \[prose: ...is this a specific way things are designed?\]**. Silver lamps hung on chains. Carved serpents watched from the mantelpiece. The furniture was black leather and older than several countries. On her first morning Hermione woke to find that someone had taken her school bag, emptied it, and refilled it with lake water, and that her four roommates were pretending to be asleep with the theatrical stillness of girls waiting to hear a Muggle-born cry.

Hermione looked at the ruined bag. She looked at the four pretending sleepers — Pansy Parkinson, whose pug-nosed sneer was already becoming familiar; Millicent, **who was genuinely asleep \[cont: so...only three are pretending?\]** and snoring like distant weather; and two others, Tracey and Daphne, who were better at stillness than Pansy but not, she noted, better than *her*.

Then she said, to the room, in a bright and carrying voice: "**Whoever did this used *Aguamenti*. It's a fourth-year charm. \[canon-cont: Aguamenti produces drinkable water\]** That means one of you is either very advanced" — a pause, calibrated — "or you got an older student to do it for you, which means you couldn't manage it yourselves and had to ask a favor you now owe. Either way, thank you. It's genuinely useful to know which of you can't do her own dirty work."

The snoring stopped being the only sound in the room. Pansy Parkinson opened one eye.

"Nobody's going to owe *you* anything, Mudblood," she said.

It was the first time Hermione had been called it to her face. She had read the word. She had known it was coming. She had promised herself, on the train, that when it came she would not give it the satisfaction of a reaction, and she discovered now that the promise held — that the word landed on her like a stone thrown at a wall and simply *stopped* there, a fact about Pansy rather than a fact about herself.

"No," Hermione agreed pleasantly. "But you already do, and you don't even know to whom. That's the difference between us." She picked up her waterlogged bag, upended it into Pansy's open trunk with a splash, and went to breakfast.

She did not, it should be said, feel as calm as she sounded. She spent the walk to the Great Hall with her jaw clenched so hard it ached and a hot, wet pressure behind her eyes that she refused, absolutely refused, to let become anything. But she had learned something on the train and confirmed it in the dormitory, and it was this: *the performance and the feeling are two different rooms, and you can live in one while you renovate the other.* She would cry later, privately, on a schedule, having budgeted for it. Right now she had a house to survive.

So she did what she always did when the world became frightening. She went to the library, and she read.

---

What she read, over her first three weeks, was everything the library had on the founding and governance of the school — and buried in a dull, brass-clasped volume called *A Consideration of the Four Houses and Their Proper Government*, published 1743 and evidently opened by no student since, she found the thing that changed her life.

Slytherin House, the book explained, had never been founded on blood.

That was a later fashion. Salazar Slytherin had valued *ambition, resourcefulness, cunning, and a certain fraternal loyalty among those who possessed them.* The obsession with pure blood had crept in over centuries, the way rot creeps into a beam — slowly, from the outside, following the grain of whoever happened to be in charge. It was, the 1743 author sniffed, "a vulgar simplification, mistaking the accident of birth for the substance of merit, and much beloved of those who possess the former and lack the latter."

Hermione read that sentence four times. Then she copied it out in her best handwriting and pinned it above her bed, where Pansy Parkinson would see it every single night and slowly, over the course of a year, come to understand that it was about her.

Because here was the thing Hermione understood, sitting in the green underwater light with a book older than the concept of prejudice she was standing inside of: *blood purity was not the rule of the house. It was merely the current price of admission, set by the people currently at the top.* And prices could be changed. Not by arguing that they were unfair — she had already grasped, with the swift cold clarity that would define her, that fairness was not a currency anyone here accepted — but by making yourself so valuable that the old price simply stopped making sense.

Slytherin respected power. It respected competence, leverage, self-possession, the ability to get things done and to make others need you. It respected, above all, *usefulness disguised as effortlessness.*

Very well. Hermione Granger could be useful. Hermione Granger could be the single most useful person any of these overbred children had ever met, and she could make it look like she was barely trying, and she could do it all while being the exact thing they despised, until the despising and the needing tangled up so completely in their minds that they could no longer tell them apart.

She closed the book. She felt, for the first time since the Hat had shouted, **something other than dread \[cont: dread seems like the wrong emotion when she asked for Slytherin\]**. She felt *interested.*

"You've got a look on your face," said Millicent, who had followed her to the library because Millicent had decided, with the total commitment of the chronically overlooked, that Hermione was hers now. Millicent was eating an apple she had brought in violation of at least two library rules. "It's a scary look. What is it?"

"It's a plan," said Hermione.

"Oh, good," said Millicent, and bit the apple. "**I like when you have those. Things happen to Pansy. \[cont: that’s a funny line and I would have liked it, but Hermione hasn’t done anything to Pansy yet\]**"

The making of herself indispensable began, as most of Hermione's campaigns did, with homework.

It began specifically in Potions, a subject taught in the dungeons by the sallow man from the staff table, who swept into his first lesson like a bad mood given legs and informed a room of trembling eleven-year-olds that he could teach them to "bottle fame, brew glory, even stopper death," and then asked Harry Potter — a boy Hermione had by now placed as the black-haired Gryffindor everyone whispered about — a series of questions clearly designed to humiliate him.

Hermione, who was in the room because Potions was taught to Gryffindor and Slytherin together, knew every answer. She knew them because she had read the textbooks, obviously, but also because she had read six books that weren't the textbook. Under normal circumstances she would have had her hand in the air like a flag.

She kept it down.

This cost her something. It cost her, specifically, the thing she wanted most in the world, which was for a teacher to look at her and know she was clever. But she had watched Snape for three weeks now — watched him favor his snakes with a partiality so blatant it was almost restful, watched him despise sentiment and stupidity in exactly equal measure, watched him award and dock points on a private logic that had nothing to do with fairness and everything to do with *did this person waste my time* — and she had concluded that the way to earn Severus Snape's regard was not to wave your hand. Anyone could wave a hand. The way to earn Severus Snape's regard was to be *right* at the precise moment being right was useful to him, and silent the rest of the time.

So when Draco Malfoy, three cauldrons over, made a boils potion that was about to explode — she could see it, the wrong shade of orange, the porcupine quills added before the cauldron was off the fire, a mistake she'd read about in a footnote — she did not raise her hand. She leaned across the aisle, and in a voice pitched for exactly one person, she said, "Off the heat. Now. Before the quills."

Malfoy, who a moment earlier had been calling her a Mudblood under his breath, was eleven years old and did not want to explode. He took his cauldron off the heat.

The potion did not explode. Snape swept past, glanced into Malfoy's cauldron, and said, "Adequate, Mr. Malfoy," which from Snape was practically a knighthood, and Malfoy glowed, and then — because he was not, whatever else he was, entirely stupid — he glanced sideways at Hermione with an expression of dawning, resentful understanding.

She had saved him. In front of Snape. And she had done it silently, taking no credit, which meant the credit was now a thing she was holding, like a coin, that she could produce at a later date.

"You didn't have to do that," Malfoy said after class, in the corridor, in the tone of a boy who has been raised to find debts intolerable.

"No," Hermione agreed.

"I don't need help from a—"

"You needed it about ninety seconds ago," she said, "when your cauldron was going to take your eyebrows off in front of Professor Snape, and you'll need it again, because you don't actually read the footnotes, you just memorize what your father tells you the answer is, and footnotes are where all the interesting mistakes live. I don't want anything for it. I just want you to remember that it happened." She smiled at him. It was a perfectly pleasant smile and it made him deeply uneasy. "That's all. That you remember."

She walked away. Behind her, Draco Malfoy stood in the corridor with the specific discomfort of a boy who has just been given something he cannot pay back, and who has begun to suspect that this was the entire point.

---

By Christmas, Hermione Granger had become a small and quiet institution.

She did not have friends, exactly, in Slytherin — friendship implied warmth, and warmth was not a currency the house dealt in at the first-year level. What she had was better and stranger: she had become *the person you went to.* She had corrected a sixth-year's Ancient Runes translation in a way that saved his grade, and he had not thanked her but had also, pointedly, told the fourth-year who'd been transfiguring her quills into slugs to knock it off. She had shown Tracey Davis a way to memorize the twelve uses of dragon's blood that actually worked, and Tracey Davis had responded by simply ceasing to participate in Pansy's campaign, drifting into a neutrality that was, in the ledger Hermione kept, as good as an alliance. **She had done Theodore Nott's Astronomy chart when he was ill, asking nothing \[canon-cont: is doing favors and asking nothing really an effective way to win Slytherin over?\]**, and Theodore Nott — a watchful, bookish boy who trusted no one — had begun, very cautiously, to watch her the way one watches something one might eventually respect.

She was building. Slowly, quietly, one favor and one footnote at a time, she was building the thing that had no name yet, the web of small obligations that would one day span the entire castle. She did not think of it grandly. She thought of it the way she thought of a revision timetable: as a system, maintained daily, that would produce results if you simply refused to stop maintaining it.

And she cried, still, on her schedule — Sunday evenings, in a bathroom on the third floor that nobody used because it was haunted by a ghost named Myrtle, who turned out to be surprisingly good company once you stopped being frightened of her and started listening, which nobody ever had. Myrtle had died in that bathroom fifty years ago. Nobody had listened to her then either. Hermione listened. She filed away, without quite knowing why, everything Myrtle told her about the day she died — a pair of great yellow eyes, a boy's voice, the sink that had never worked right — because Hermione filed away everything, and because a girl who has been made to feel disposable recognizes another one across the distance of fifty years and a great deal of plumbing.

"You're not like the other Slytherins," Myrtle observed one Sunday, drifting upside down near the ceiling.

"No," said Hermione, drying her eyes on schedule, folding the sadness back into its box for another week. "I'm going to be much worse."

Myrtle brightened. Myrtle, who had spent fifty years haunting a toilet, deeply approved of a grudge with ambition.

*End of Chapter Two.*

## **Chapter Three: A Small Matter of a Very Large Dog**

The trouble with becoming the person everyone comes to, Hermione discovered that spring, is that eventually someone comes to you about a three-headed dog.

The someone was Millicent, and the way she came to Hermione about it was to sit down at breakfast, take four sausages, and say, "There's a monster on the third floor and Malfoy's going to get himself killed, and I thought you'd want to know because you like knowing things and also I think it would upset you if Malfoy died, which is strange, so I'm telling you."

Hermione put down her toast. "Start from the beginning. And more slowly. **And with fewer sausages in the way of the words. \[char: bro why are they so mean about Millicent eating\]**"

The story, assembled over the course of breakfast, was this: there was a corridor on the third floor that Professor Dumbledore had, at the start of term, forbidden everyone from entering on pain of "a very painful death," which Hermione had assumed was a figure of speech and had, being fundamentally law-abiding in the way of people who plan to break exactly the right rules later, avoided. But Draco Malfoy had gotten himself entangled in some idiotic midnight business with Harry Potter — a duel that wasn't a duel, a scheme to get Potter caught out of bed — and in the course of it had stumbled into that corridor, and had seen, behind a locked door, a dog. A very large dog. A dog, Malfoy had told everyone in the common room with the shrill overconfidence of the badly frightened, with *three heads.*

And it was standing, Malfoy had reported, on a trapdoor.

Hermione went still in the particular way she went still when a great many facts were arranging themselves into a shape.

A three-headed dog. Standing on a trapdoor. Guarding something. In a school. In the same year that — she had read this in July and filed it and never forgotten it, because she never forgot anything — the wizarding bank Gringotts had been broken into, on a vault that had, the *Prophet* said, been *emptied earlier that same day.* As though someone had moved something small and precious somewhere safer at the last possible moment.

Somewhere, say, with a dog on it.

"Hermione," said Millicent. "You've got the plan face again."

"I've got a *question* face," Hermione corrected. "The plan face comes later. Right now I have questions." She began, on a napkin, to make a list. She loved a list the way other children loved sweets. "Who moves something out of the safest bank in the world and into a school? Only someone who thinks the bank isn't safe enough. Who thinks that? Someone who knows a thief who could crack Gringotts. And what's worth guarding with a Cerberus and a trapdoor and, I'd bet, a great deal more underneath?"

"I don't know," said Millicent. "I don't know any of that."

"Neither do I," said Hermione, with relish. "Yet."

---

Here is what Hermione did *not* do, and it is the whole of the difference between her and the boy with the scar.

She did not sneak into the corridor. She did not attempt to fight the dog. She did not decide, on the basis of a strong feeling and a noble heart, that it was up to her to stop a thief, and then go charging down a trapdoor into a series of escalating obstacles with two friends and no plan.

Instead she went to the library and read about Cerberuses (they sleep to music — useful, filed) and about the Gringotts break-in (the vault number was public, and Hermione, who read the *Prophet* the way generals read maps, had the date memorized) and about a man named Nicolas Flamel, whose name she found in three separate footnotes and one chocolate frog card and who turned out to be the only known maker of the Philosopher's Stone, an object which produced both limitless gold and the elixir of eternal life and would therefore be, she reasoned, *exactly the sort of thing one might move out of a bank if one had recently learned that someone very dangerous wanted to live forever.*

It took her nine days. She did it mostly during meals and after lights-out, with Millicent standing guard and Myrtle running the occasional errand a ghost could run better than a girl. By the end of it she knew, with a certainty she could have defended to the Board of Governors, three things: what was under the trapdoor, who wanted it, and — this was the part that made her sit down rather suddenly on the floor of the library — that the professor most likely to be trying to steal it was not, as Malfoy insisted, Snape.

It was the nervous one. The one with the turban and the stammer, who taught Defense Against the Dark Arts and whom nobody suspected because he appeared to be frightened of his own shadow. Because Hermione had been watching Snape all year, the way she watched everyone, and Snape's behavior was consistent with a man *guarding* something and suspicious of a colleague — the limp, the muttering, the way he'd looked at Quirrell during the Halloween feast like a man watching a snake he'd told everyone was a stick. Snape was not the thief. Snape was the guard dog with two legs. She'd have staked her reputation on it, and she was beginning, quietly, to have a reputation to stake.

So Hermione, eleven years old, sat on the floor of the Hogwarts library holding the solution to a mystery that a trio of Gryffindors were, at that very moment, blundering toward from the opposite direction with all the subtlety of a marching band.

And she had a decision to make.

---

The Gryffindor way — she could see it clearly, could have written the essay — was to *act.* To go to a teacher, to be disbelieved, to take matters into her own hands, to go down the trapdoor herself and be brave and possibly die and certainly get a hundred points and a great deal of attention.

Hermione considered this option for exactly as long as it took to reject it.

Because what did she gain, going down the trapdoor? Glory. And glory, she had decided in her first week, was the most overrated currency in the entire wizarding economy — loud, dangerous, and impossible to save for later. The people who chased glory ended up in *Great Wizarding Disasters.* The people who *kept* things — kept knowledge, kept favors, kept the small quiet cards nobody knew you were holding — those people ended up running things while the glory-chasers were still recovering in the hospital wing.

She did not need to go down the trapdoor. She needed the *right person* to go down the trapdoor at the *right time,* and to be the person who had quietly made sure they knew what waited at the bottom.

So she wrote a letter.

She wrote it in a disguised hand, on ordinary paper, and it was addressed to Harry Potter, and it said, in full: *"It's Quirrell, not Snape. The dog sleeps if you play it music. The thing it guards is a Stone that makes gold and eternal life, which is why He wants it. You clearly already know most of this or you wouldn't be poking around. A friend who would rather you didn't get expelled or killed, as either would be inconvenient."*

She had Myrtle deliver it — a ghost can slide a note under a dormitory door with no one the wiser — and then she did the single most Slytherin thing she had done all year, which was: *nothing.*

She went to bed. She let Harry Potter, who was brave and reckless and constitutionally incapable of leaving a mystery alone, do what Harry Potters do. And when the whole thing came out — when Quirrell was found dead and the Stone destroyed and Harry Potter woke in the hospital wing a hero, when Dumbledore awarded Gryffindor a sackful of points at the leaving feast and everyone cheered and the story of the Boy Who Faced You-Know-Who Again became the only story anyone told for a month — Hermione Granger sat at the Slytherin table, applauding politely, holding a secret nobody knew she held.

*I solved it first,* she thought, clapping. *I solved it eleven days ago. And I let him have the noise, because the noise is worthless, and I kept the thing that isn't: I know exactly how I did it, and now so do I.*

Because that was the real prize, and only Hermione could see it. She had taken a genuine mystery, a real threat, the kind of thing that got Gryffindors killed, and she had solved it completely without ever once being in danger, purely by reading and thinking and refusing to be interesting to the wrong people at the wrong time. She had proven the thesis. The thesis worked.

Malfoy found her at the feast, drawn by some instinct he didn't understand. "You've got that look," he said suspiciously. "Like you know something."

"I always know something, Draco," said Hermione. "That's rather the whole of my personality." She watched Harry Potter laugh with his friends at the Gryffindor table, the firelight on the golden plates, the whole warm loud spectacle of being loved by supper. She did not envy it. She had checked, carefully, and she did not.

"I don't understand you," Malfoy said. It came out almost plaintive. It was the most honest thing he had ever said to her.

"No," Hermione agreed, not unkindly. "But you will. Everyone does, eventually. It just takes them a few years, and by then it's usually too late to do anything about it."

She went back to her clapping. The green light of the lake rippled somewhere far below, patient as a plan, and the first year of the war for the soul of Slytherin House came quietly to a close, with the Muggle-born girl one enormous secret richer, and almost nobody the wiser.

That, she felt, was rather the point.

*End of Chapter Three.*

## **Chapter Four: The Chamber, and Other Inconveniences**

The summer between first and second year, Hermione did three things.

She read the entire second-year curriculum, which everyone expected. She read the entire *third*\-year curriculum, which nobody expected but which surprised no one who knew her. And she wrote seventeen letters — to Millicent, to Theodore Nott, to Tracey Davis, to a Ravenclaw prefect named Penelope she'd helped with a Transfiguration proof, to a Hufflepuff girl she'd sat next to in Herbology who'd mentioned her uncle worked at the Ministry — chatty, warm, undemanding letters that asked after summers and mentioned interesting books and left, in each recipient, the faint pleasant residue of being *thought of* by the cleverest person they knew.

Seventeen letters. Seventeen threads, tended over a summer, so that when she stepped back onto the platform in September the web was a little wider and a little stronger and entirely invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for it, which was everyone.

She had also grown an inch, learned to keep her hair out of a book with a charmed clip of her own devising, and turned, at some point over the summer, into a person that even Pansy Parkinson had stopped openly tormenting — not out of kindness, but out of the dawning animal awareness that Hermione Granger was becoming someone it was strategically unwise to have as an enemy, and that the reasons for this were accumulating faster than anyone could quite track.

This was fortunate, because second year was the one that tried to kill her.

---

It announced itself in October, in blood-red paint a foot high on a stone wall: *THE CHAMBER OF SECRETS HAS BEEN OPENED. ENEMIES OF THE HEIR, BEWARE.* And beneath it, the caretaker's cat, hanging stiff as a board from a torch bracket, petrified.

The castle reacted with the measured calm of a henhouse discovering a fox. But it was in Slytherin, that evening, that the temperature genuinely changed — because the story that came down through the older students, half-boasted and half-whispered, was that the Heir of Slytherin was purging the school of those who did not belong. That the Chamber had been built by Salazar himself to finish the work of keeping the school *pure.* And the students it was hunting — the enemies of the Heir — were the Muggle-borns.

Of whom, in Slytherin House, there was precisely one.

Hermione sat in the green-lit common room that night and understood, with a cold and total clarity, that she was the single most exposed person in the entire castle. A Muggle-born, living in the belly of the house that was cheering the thing that wanted her dead. She did not sleep in her dormitory that night. She did not, in fact, sleep in her dormitory much at all that year.

And here the story might have gone very badly for her, and for a few weeks it teetered — this was the darkest stretch of her school life, and she would never afterward pretend otherwise. There was a night in November when she found her bed hexed so that the sheets tried to strangle her, and she lay on the floor of Myrtle's bathroom afterward shaking so hard her teeth clicked, and cried well over her weekly budget, and thought, for the first time, *I could ask to be resorted. I could go to McGonagall and ask, and she'd take me, and I'd be loved by supper, and I would be safe.*

She thought about it for a long time, on the cold tile, with a dead girl hovering worriedly above her.

And then she got up, and washed her face, and did not do it — not out of pride, though there was pride in it, but because of a calculation she made lying on that floor that she would carry for the rest of her life. *If I run now,* she thought, *I will have proven that they were right. That the Muggle-born couldn't take it. That the house was too much for me. And every Muggle-born who comes after me will inherit that. But if I stay — if I stay in the house that wants me dead and I don't just survive it, I* win *it — then I break the thing entirely. I make it impossible to say. Nobody will ever again be able to say a Muggle-born couldn't belong in Slytherin, because I will have belonged so thoroughly that they'll be quoting me in the 1743 book's next edition.*

It was, she recognized even at twelve, an insane amount of weight to put on a decision made on a bathroom floor. She put it there anyway. It held.

"You're staying," said Myrtle, reading her face.

"I'm staying," said Hermione. "And I'm going to find out what's doing this before it finds me. Because I've realized something, Myrtle." She stood, and the shaking had stopped, and in its place was the thing that would one day make grown wizards choose their words carefully around her. "The Heir wants me frightened and hiding. Which means the single most dangerous thing I can possibly be, to whoever this is, is *unafraid and looking.*"

---

She was, of course, extremely afraid. But she had learned the trick of that a year ago, in a dormitory full of girls pretending to sleep: the performance and the feeling are two different rooms. She let the fear live in its room and she went to work in the other one.

And Hermione Granger, working, was a force that the Heir of Slytherin — for all his ancient bloodline and his monster and his fifty years of planning — was frankly not equipped to handle. Because he was relying on terror and secrecy, and she countered with the two things no amount of dark magic can defend against: a library card and an inability to stop reading footnotes.

It came together the way it always did, from a dozen threads. From Myrtle, who had died fifty years ago in that very bathroom, of a pair of *great yellow eyes* — a detail Hermione had filed away in her first year without knowing why, because she filed away everything, and which now sat up and demanded attention. From the spiders she noticed fleeing the castle, marching out through every crack, away from something. From the fact that the victims were all *petrified* rather than killed — seen, she deduced, indirectly: through a camera, through a reflection in water, through a ghost, through the bottom of a poured drink. From a page she found — and here she permitted herself a single moment of pride — torn from a library book and copied in her own hand weeks before she needed it, because she had been researching for entirely other reasons and had simply happened to read the entry on *basilisks.*

A basilisk. A giant serpent, the King of Snakes, whose gaze killed directly and petrified indirectly, who spoke the tongue of serpents and had lived, perhaps, for a thousand years in a chamber beneath a school, summoned and controlled by an heir who spoke its language. That was the monster. That was what was hunting her through the corridors of her own house.

She wrote it on a piece of paper. She wrote the word *basilisk*, and beside it, *pipes — it moves through the pipes — that's why nobody's seen it — the plumbing,* and then, because a part of her was and always would be a girl who could not resist annotating a triumph, she wrote *"see also: Myrtle's sink, which never worked right,"* underlined it twice, and understood in a rush of cold that she knew where the entrance to the Chamber of Secrets was.

It was in a haunted bathroom on the third floor. It had been under her, and under everyone, the entire time. Fifty years, and the only person who'd ever bothered to listen to the dead girl who lived on top of it was a Muggle-born nobody was supposed to survive.

She sat with that for a moment. Then she folded the paper, and put it in her pocket, and — because she was Hermione Granger and not a hero in a story — she did not go down the pipe.

*End of Chapter Four.*

## **Chapter Five: How to Win a War Without Attending It**

There is a version of this story where Hermione Granger, having deduced the monster and located the Chamber, arms herself and descends into the dark to face the Heir of Slytherin alone.

It is a stirring version. It is also the version where she dies, and Hermione had read enough of *Great Wizarding Disasters* to recognize the opening pages of a chapter about herself.

So she did something better. She did the single most powerful thing a person in possession of a dangerous secret can do, which is: she made sure the *right people* knew it, in the *right order,* in a way that could not be traced back to her, so that the danger fell on those equipped to handle it and the credit fell wherever it liked, because credit was worthless and she had never wanted it.

She wrote three notes.

The first went to Professor Snape. It was unsigned, delivered by means she never disclosed, and it said only: *"The monster is a basilisk. It travels through the plumbing. The entrance is in the second-floor girls' bathroom — the out-of-order one. The victims lived because none of them looked at it directly. Whoever is opening the Chamber can speak to snakes. You are cleverer than I am and better placed to act; I am merely better read. — A well-wisher who would prefer the school not close, as I have exams."*

She had thought hard about sending it to Snape rather than Dumbledore, and had concluded, on the evidence of a year and a half of careful observation, that Snape was the teacher most likely to *act on information* rather than *ponder it meaningfully by a window.* This proved correct. She would maintain for the rest of her life that this was the most important single decision of her school career, and that she got no credit for it whatsoever, and that this was exactly how she'd designed it.

The second note went to Harry Potter, because Harry Potter was, she had by now concluded, a kind of weather system — you could not stop him from being at the center of things, so you might as well make sure that when he arrived at the center of things he arrived *informed.* Her note to him was briefer: *"Basilisk. Second-floor bathroom. Don't look it in the eye, and for Merlin's sake bring a teacher, you are twelve.*"

The third note she did not send. The third note she kept, and it was addressed to no one, and it was simply the complete solution, dated, in her own hand, sealed in an envelope and hidden in the lining of her trunk — her proof, her private record, the thing that let her sit through the chaos that followed with the serene interior calm of a person who has already filed the correct answer with a notary.

What followed was, by all accounts, dramatic. There was a petrified prefect, and a written warning, and a night when the whole school held its breath. There was a girl taken *into* the Chamber — a small, frightened Weasley first-year — and a descent, and a fight, and Harry Potter emerging from a pipe filthy and bleeding and holding a sword, having killed a thousand-year-old basilisk and destroyed the shade of a young Dark Lord, and being, once again, a hero.

Hermione was not there. Hermione was in the library, technically, though she had positioned herself near a window from which she could see the comings and goings, and she had made certain — this was the one active thing she did that night — that a very worried Professor Snape and a very grim Professor McGonagall had both been walking toward that bathroom a full twenty minutes before Harry Potter reached it, because a well-wisher's note had reached the staff table at exactly the right moment. She would never know for certain how much of the night she had bent. She suspected: a great deal. She suspected that a girl lived who might not have. She kept that suspicion in the same box as her Sunday sadness, and took it out, occasionally, and looked at it, and felt something she didn't have a tidy word for.

When it was over, when the school celebrated and exams were cancelled — *cancelled*, a genuine tragedy from Hermione's perspective, though she had the grace not to say so aloud more than twice — the story that everyone told was of Harry Potter and the sword and the serpent.

And under that story, invisible, ran another one. It ran through Snape, who received an anonymous note of impeccable deduction and never learned who sent it, but who began, that spring, to watch a certain second-year Slytherin with an attention that was not quite suspicion and not quite respect but shared a border with both. It ran through the small network of students who had, over two years, come to understand that Hermione Granger simply *knew things* — knew them early, knew them completely, and disbursed them with a generosity that always, somehow, left you feeling you owed her, though you could never point to the debt. It ran through Draco Malfoy, whose father's fingerprints were all over the year's disaster, and who came to Hermione in the last week of term, white-faced and furious and frightened, and said, "You knew. You knew the whole time. You could have said something and you didn't."

"I said something to the people who could act on it," Hermione replied. "I just didn't say it loudly, or with my name on it. Loud is for people who want to be thanked, Draco. I don't want to be thanked." She looked at him — really looked, the way she'd looked at him in the corridor a year ago, cataloguing. "Your father put that diary in that girl's cauldron. Didn't he. You don't have to answer. Your face just did."

Malfoy went, if possible, whiter.

"I'm not going to tell anyone," said Hermione. "I want you to understand that clearly, because it matters. I *could.* I know, and I could, and it would ruin your family, and I'm choosing not to. Not because I like you — the jury's frankly still out — but because a secret you *don't* use is worth more than a secret you spend. Right now, today, your family owes me its reputation and its liberty, and the only people who know it are you and me. That's the most valuable thing I own." She smiled. "So no. I won't tell. But you'll remember, Draco. Every time you look at me for the next six years, you'll remember exactly what I know and exactly what I chose to do with it. Which was nothing. Which was mercy. Isn't that a horrible thing to owe someone?"

It was, in fact, the single most Slytherin thing anyone had said to Draco Malfoy in his entire life, and it had come out of the mouth of a Muggle-born, and he understood in that moment — fully, finally, with a lurch of something that would take him years to admit was awe — that the Sorting Hat had not made a mistake at all. That it had, on the contrary, made the most dangerous decision of its thousand-year career, and dropped it, gift-wrapped, into the middle of the house least prepared to survive it.

"You're terrifying," he said. It was not an insult. It was the closest thing to a compliment he knew how to give.

"Thank you," said Hermione Granger, and went to pack her trunk, in the lining of which a sealed envelope lay against her folded robes like a loaded thing, and hummed, a little, because it had been, on balance, a very productive year.

*End of Chapter Five.*

## **Chapter Six: The Snape Problem**

By her third year, the problem with Hermione Granger — from the perspective of the Hogwarts staff, who discussed her more than they discussed any student since a boy named Riddle they'd all agreed to stop discussing — was that there was no obvious problem with her, and this was itself the problem.

She broke no rules that anyone could catch. Her marks were the highest in her year, in several years, in — Professor Flitwick had checked, out of sheer curiosity — the recorded history of several individual subjects. She was unfailingly polite. She was never at the center of any incident, and yet incidents had a way of *resolving themselves* in her vicinity, cleanly and quietly, as though someone had gotten there first and tidied up. Bullies in Slytherin had simply stopped bullying, not because they'd grown kind but because every campaign against her ended, somehow, with the bully worse off and unable to explain why. Pansy Parkinson had spent two years trying to destroy Hermione Granger and had emerged from the effort with worse marks, fewer allies, and a reputation, in her own house, as someone who "couldn't manage the Granger business" — a phrase that had entered the Slytherin vocabulary as shorthand for *taking on more than you can handle.*

"She's running the third-year girls," Professor McGonagall said in the staff room, in a tone of scientific interest. "Possibly the whole of the second through fourth years. I can't prove it. But when I need a message to reach a Slytherin student quietly, I've caught myself giving it to Miss Granger, and it arrives faster than the official channels."

"She's building something," said Professor Snape, from the corner, not looking up from a stack of essays he was defacing with red ink.

"Building *what?*"

"I don't know," said Snape. "That is what concerns me." He turned a page. "I have taught the children of every dark family in Britain. I know what ambition looks like when it is loud and stupid — it looks like Draco Malfoy. I do not know what it looks like when it is patient and clever and thirteen and has never once, in my hearing, said a single thing it did not mean to say." He set down his quill. "I taught a boy like that, once. We do not, as a rule, discuss how that turned out."

The staff room was quiet for a moment.

"She's a *Muggle-born,*" said Professor Sprout, as though this settled it.

"Yes," said Snape. "In Slytherin. Thriving. Ask yourself what kind of person that requires, Pomona, and then ask yourself whether you would like to be the one to make an enemy of her." He returned to his essays. "I have decided that I would not. I recommend the policy to the room."

---

What Snape did instead of making an enemy of her was, over the course of Hermione's third year, the closest thing to mentorship he was constitutionally capable of, which is to say he was continuously, elaborately horrible to her in a way that she came to understand was the highest form of attention he offered any living person.

It began with a returned essay. Hermione had written the assigned two feet on the Shrinking Solution and received it back covered in more red ink than any essay she had ever produced, culminating in a final comment at the bottom in Snape's cramped hand: *"Correct in every particular. Tedious in every other. You have written what the book says. I did not ask what the book says. I can read the book. Write what the book is too cowardly to say. — S.S."*

Hermione read this four times. Then, instead of being wounded — she noted, with interest, that she was no longer wounded by much of anything; the box she kept her feelings in had grown very large and very well-organized — she felt the specific electric thrill of a locked door being shown to her.

She rewrote the essay. She did not rewrite it correctly. She rewrote it *dangerously* — with speculations two years beyond the curriculum, with a footnote questioning the standard ingredient order and proposing, with evidence, a better one, with a closing paragraph that began *"The textbook is wrong about the following, and I suspect the author knew it."* She handed it in braced for a detention.

She received it back with a single word at the bottom. *"Better."*

It was, she would later say, the first time a teacher had ever actually taught her anything, as opposed to simply confirming that she had learned it. Snape did not want to know that she had read the book. Snape wanted to know what she *thought*, and — more than that — he wanted her to learn that being right was not the same as being *finished*, that the world was full of accepted answers that were merely the mistakes nobody had yet had the nerve to correct. It was the most valuable lesson anyone gave her at Hogwarts, and it was delivered entirely in the form of insults, and she treasured it accordingly.

They never became warm. Warmth was not on offer, and Hermione had learned to want the thing that was actually on the table rather than the thing she wished were. What they became was something rarer and, to both of them, more interesting: two people who despised waste and worshipped competence, circling each other across a classroom, each recognizing in the other a mind that did not slow down for anyone. He gave her books that were not on any list. She returned them annotated, arguing with the authors, and he read the annotations, and argued back in the margins, and neither of them ever once acknowledged aloud that this was happening.

"Why are you helping me?" she asked him once, at the end of a term, unable to resist the question because she catalogued everything and he was the one variable she couldn't fully resolve.

Snape considered her for a long moment. "I am not helping you," he said. "I am reducing waste. You are the most able student I have taught in a decade, and you are trapped in a house that will, if left to itself, either break you or make you cruel. I find both outcomes *inefficient.*" He turned back to his cauldron. "Also," he added, so quietly she almost missed it, "I know what it is to be clever and poor and unwelcome in a place full of people who were handed everything. I turned that into something I am not proud of. You appear to be turning it into something else. I am — curious — to see what."

It was the most he ever told her about himself, and it was enough. Hermione filed it away, in the box that held Myrtle and the third note and the thing she'd done the night of the Chamber, the box of things that were about people rather than facts, which was smaller than her other boxes but which she had begun, very slowly, over three years underground, to understand mattered more.

"Thank you, sir," she said, and meant it in a way she rarely meant anything.

"Get out of my classroom, Miss Granger," said Snape, "before either of us says something sincere," and she went, smiling, into the green-lit corridors of the only home she had ever made entirely by herself, out of nothing, in a place that had wanted her gone.

*End of Chapter Six.*

## **Chapter Seven: The Bank**

Somewhere in her fourth year, without any single day on which it happened, Hermione Granger stopped being a clever girl surviving Slytherin and became an institution that the rest of the castle quietly ran on.

The network had a shape by then, though only Hermione could see the whole of it, and even she kept most of it in her head rather than on paper, because paper could be found. It worked like this. She did people favors — real ones, useful ones, delivered early and without being asked. She found a Hufflepuff's lost heirloom. She quietly warned a Ravenclaw which prefect was reporting to which professor. She fixed a Gryffindor's disastrous essay the night before it was due, asking nothing, mentioning it to no one. And each favor deposited, in the person who received it, the same two feelings: gratitude, and a faint unpayable debt. She never called the debts in crudely. She simply let them sit, accruing, until the day she needed a thing — a message carried, a rumor started or stopped, a door left unlocked, a piece of information that only a Hufflepuff third-year happened to have — and then she would ask, lightly, and it would be done, gladly, by someone who felt they were finally getting to *repay* her, and who walked away feeling *better* for having done it.

It was, Hermione reflected, the perfect system, because everyone in it was happy. She had built, out of nothing, in a school that despised her blood, a bank. And she was the only one who could read the ledger.

"You're basically a Gringotts," said Millicent, who over three years had become the one person Hermione told nearly everything, and who had grown into a large, calm, watchful girl that the rest of the school had learned not to mistake for slow. Millicent was slow about nothing that mattered. "Except instead of gold it's secrets and homework, and instead of a dragon guarding it, it's me."

"You're much better than a dragon," said Hermione.

"I know," said Millicent, pleased. "I'm friendlier and I don't set things on fire unless you ask."

The Malfoy account, meanwhile, had matured beautifully. Draco had spent two years in a state of low-grade dread that Hermione would spend the secret of his father's diary, and had gradually, as she conspicuously did not, come to occupy the strangest position of anyone in her network: he was not her friend, not her ally, not her enemy, but her *creature* — bound to her by a debt so large and so quiet that he had reorganized his entire personality around not thinking about it. He was still a snob. He still called her Granger with a curl in his lip. But when it mattered — when she needed a pure-blood voice to say a thing that a Muggle-born voice could not safely say, when she needed the Malfoy name to open a door — Draco opened it, resentfully, thoroughly, and never once asked why, because he was terrified of the answer.

By the end of fourth year, when the Triwizard Tournament brought foreign schools and a poisonous, glittering season of politics to the castle, Hermione barely had to lift a finger to know everything happening in it. The information came to her. A Beauxbatons girl owed a Ravenclaw who owed Hermione; a Durmstrang boy had made the mistake of insulting Millicent, and had spent the rest of the year being subtly, comprehensively out-maneuvered in ways he never traced to their source. When Harry Potter was mysteriously entered into a tournament he was too young for, and the whole school turned on him, Hermione — who by now regarded Harry with a proprietary exasperation, the way one regards a beloved and accident-prone younger sibling one has never actually spoken to — knew within a week that he hadn't entered himself, knew within two that someone at the school was engineering the whole thing, and passed a series of anonymous, impeccably-sourced notes to the one adult she trusted to act on them.

She never once entered the arena. She simply made sure that the people who did were less likely to die, and collected, for her trouble, nothing but the private, unshareable satisfaction of a job done correctly and unseen.

"Doesn't it bother you?" Myrtle asked her — Hermione still visited, out of loyalty and because a ghost is the only truly unbetrayable confidant. "That nobody knows? That Potter gets the parades and you get a haunted toilet?"

Hermione considered this honestly, because Myrtle deserved honesty and because she had made a policy, years ago, of never lying to herself even when it would be more comfortable.

"It used to," she said. "In first year. I wanted so badly for someone to *see* how clever I was. And then I worked out that being seen is the most dangerous thing that can happen to you in this world. The people everyone can see are the people everyone can aim at. Harry Potter has been nearly murdered every single year since he arrived, Myrtle, precisely *because* everyone sees him. Whereas nobody's tried to murder me since second year, because as far as most of the castle is concerned I'm a very well-behaved girl with good marks." She smiled, and the green light of the lake moved on her face. "I don't want the parade. I want to be the person the parade doesn't know it's marching for. That's not a consolation prize, Myrtle. That's the actual prize. It just doesn't come with a rosette."

*End of Chapter Seven.*

## **Chapter Eight: The Year It Stopped Being Funny**

Voldemort came back at the end of Hermione's fourth year, and for a while the joke of her life stopped being quite so funny.

She had known it was coming — of course she had known; she had read the pattern in a hundred small signs the way she read everything — but knowing a storm is coming and standing in it are different kinds of knowledge. A boy died at the end of that tournament, an ordinary decent Hufflepuff named Cedric, killed for nothing, for being in the wrong place, and the Ministry decided it was more convenient to call it an accident than to admit what it meant. And Hermione, sitting in the Slytherin common room while the older students murmured, some of them frightened and some of them — she marked their faces, quietly, and never unmarked them — *excited,* understood that the game she had been playing was about to become a real one, with real stakes, and that being a clever Muggle-born in the most Voldemort-adjacent house in Britain was about to stop being an interesting challenge and start being a survival problem of the first order.

She was fifteen. She spent that summer, and the fifth year that followed, doing the coldest and most necessary calculation of her life.

Because everyone assumed — and this was the assumption she lived inside, and used — that when the war came, sides would be simple. You were for Dumbledore or for Voldemort. Light or dark. And a Muggle-born, obviously, was for the Light, was for Harry Potter, was destined to be one more brave body thrown at the darkness.

Hermione declined.

Not out of cowardice — though she'd have cheerfully accepted the charge, having decided long ago that "coward" was just the word brave people used for someone who intended to still be alive at the end. And not out of any sympathy for Voldemort, whose entire ideology was, from Hermione's position, both personally lethal and — this offended her almost as much — *stupid*, a great edifice of power built on the single dumbest possible metric, blood, when everyone with eyes could see that ability was the thing that actually mattered and that she personally out-abilitied every pure-blood in the country. No. Hermione declined the binary because she had spent four years learning that the binary was a trap, that the people who let themselves be sorted into "sides" became *soldiers*, and soldiers were spent, and Hermione Granger did not intend to be spent by anyone — not by the Dark Lord who wanted her dead, and not by the old Headmaster who, she had come to see with a clarity that unsettled her, was perfectly willing to raise children as weapons if the cause required it.

She would build a third thing. She had, in fact, already built it. She just had to survive long enough, and grow it wide enough, that when the two great powers finished tearing at each other, the third thing would be the one everyone still standing had to come to.

---

Fifth year was the year the Ministry occupied the school — a pink, smiling, sadistic woman named Umbridge installed to bring Hogwarts to heel, banning real magic, torturing students with a cursed quill, ruling by decree. It was a nightmare, and it was also, Hermione recognized with a thrill she had the grace to be slightly ashamed of, *the greatest opportunity of her life,* because nothing builds a network like a common enemy and a bad government.

While Harry Potter — bless him, she thought, watching from three tables away — responded to the tyranny the way Harry Potter responded to everything, by forming a brave illegal club and teaching thirty Gryffindors and Ravenclaws to fight, loudly, in a way that got several of them caught, Hermione responded the way Hermione responded to everything.

Quietly. Structurally. Permanently.

She did not form a club. Clubs get raided. She built a *supply line* — of information, of forbidden books photocopied by a Muggle-born's utterly illegal use of a device Umbridge's people didn't even know to look for, of small acts of sabotage that could never be traced, of an early-warning system that meant no student was ever caught by Umbridge's inquisitors without thirty seconds' notice that reached them through four houses in under a minute. She never signed anything. She never led a meeting. She simply became the thing that made the resistance *work*, the invisible architecture beneath the brave visible acts, and when it was over — when Umbridge was gone, carried off in a manner Hermione had absolutely nothing to do with and privately found hilarious — the students who had survived that year owed their survival, whether they knew it or not, to a system with no name and no face and a girl at the center of it who had never once raised her wand in anger.

And she had done something else that year, something she told no one, not even Millicent, not even Myrtle. She had, working alone in the dead of night, begun to understand *how Voldemort had survived.* Because she never forgot anything, and she had not forgotten a diary in a girl's cauldron three years before — a diary that held a piece of a soul, that had tried to come back to life. She had read, by fifth year, in books that were not supposed to exist, about the thing that made that possible. She had a word for it that she never said aloud. And she understood, alone in the dark with the worst secret in Britain, that the war everyone thought was about wands and armies was actually about *objects* — hidden, scattered, deniable — and that whoever understood *that* first would win it, and that she was, as usual, first.

She did not tell Dumbledore. She had learned not to hand her cards to old men with plans of their own. She simply began, quietly, to look — not to fight the war, but to understand it so completely that when the moment came, she would be the only person in the country holding the map.

*End of Chapter Eight.*

## **Chapter Nine: The Third Faction**

By sixth year the war was in the castle for real, and the castle was choosing sides, and Hermione Granger chose the side nobody knew was an option: hers.

It had a shape now, and members, though none of them would have described it as a faction and most of them didn't know the others existed. There was Millicent, her right hand, worth ten of anyone. There was Theodore Nott, the watchful pure-blood boy who had spent five years deciding he trusted exactly one person in the world and had settled, to his own surprise, on the Muggle-born. There was Draco Malfoy, whose family had by now fallen so far, so fast, into Voldemort's service and out of his favor that Draco spent sixth year a hollowed-out wreck of terror — and who, at the lowest point of his life, tasked with something monstrous and certain he would die whether he did it or failed, came to the one person he had never been able to explain his fear of, and found, instead of the enemy he'd been raised to expect, a chair, and a cup of tea, and a girl who said, "Sit down, Draco. Tell me what he's made you do. I'm the only person you know who owes you nothing and wants nothing from you, which makes me the only person you can afford to tell the truth."

He told her the truth. All of it. The Vanishing Cabinet, the impossible task, the threat to his mother. And Hermione — who could have handed him to Dumbledore, who could have handed him to the Order, who could have spent the secret a dozen ways — did what she had done with every important secret since she was twelve. She kept it. And she used it not as a weapon but as a *hook*, the deepest one yet, so that when the war reached its worst hour, Draco Malfoy belonged to her more completely than he had ever belonged to Voldemort, because Voldemort had offered him fear and she had offered him the one thing no one in his life ever had: someone who knew the very worst of it and did not flinch.

"Why," Draco asked her, hollow-eyed, that year, "are you being kind to me. After everything my family. After everything I. Why."

"I'm not being kind," said Hermione, which was their oldest joke by now, and almost true. "I'm being *strategic.* A frightened enemy is dangerous. A frightened person who owes you their sanity is an asset. I'm simply better than the Dark Lord at noticing which one you are." She refilled his tea. "Also," she added, more quietly, in the register she reserved for the small box of true things, "I know what it's like to be trapped in this house and made into something you didn't choose. He's doing it to you with fear. They tried to do it to me with cruelty. I decided a long time ago I wasn't going to become what this place wanted me to become. I'm offering you the same choice. That part's not strategy, Draco. That part's just the truth. Don't tell anyone. It'll ruin my reputation."

Draco Malfoy, seventeen years old and closer to breaking than he had ever been, laughed — a cracked, astonished sound — and something in him, some last brittle thing his father had built, quietly gave way and was replaced by something that would, in the end, keep him and his mother alive.

---

The night Dumbledore died — killed on the Astronomy Tower, the school broken open, Death Eaters loose in the corridors, Snape fleeing into the dark with a curse on his name and a secret in his heart that Hermione was one of perhaps three people alive to suspect the shape of — Hermione did not fight. She got seven students out. Through passages only her network knew, past dangers her early-warning system flagged in advance, she moved a cluster of the vulnerable and the marked to safety while the battle raged elsewhere, and not one of them died, and not one of them ever fully understood how they'd survived a night that killed better and braver people, and all of them, ever after, would do anything she asked.

And in the grey dawn after, when the castle counted its dead and the war stopped being a thing that was coming and became a thing that was *here*, Hermione Granger stood at a window in the highest tower she could reach — not to grieve, though she grieved, on schedule, later, thoroughly — but to *see.* To look out over a Britain that was falling to the darkness and understand, with the cold clarity that had never once failed her, that the old world was ending, and that in the gap it left, there would be room — for the first time in a thousand years — for something new to decide what came next.

She intended it to be her.

*End of Chapter Nine.*

## **Chapter Ten: The War of the Ledger**

Voldemort took Britain in a summer. The Ministry fell, the *Prophet* turned, and a Muggle-Born Registration Commission was established to catalogue, persecute, and dispose of people exactly like Hermione Granger.

She did not run, and she did not fight in any way that anyone could photograph.

She had spent six years preparing for precisely this, though she'd hoped never to need it, and when it came she was ready in a way that no one — not Voldemort, not the Order, not Harry Potter off hunting the scattered pieces of a dark soul with a determination Hermione respected and a plan she'd have improved in an afternoon — was ready. Because the new regime ran on *records*, and Hermione Granger understood records the way a spider understands a web. Within a month of the Ministry's fall, the Muggle-Born Registration Commission's files were, without anyone able to prove it, *wrong* — subtly, comprehensively, catastrophically wrong. Muggle-borns who should have been flagged were listed as pure-blood, with generations of invented and perfectly documented ancestry. Safe houses appeared in records as condemned buildings. Snatchers were sent, by impeccable official paperwork, to the wrong addresses, over and over, wasting the regime's time and fraying its nerves, and every thread of it ran back through favors and debts and quiet loyal people in small Ministry offices who owed a girl they'd known at school and were glad, in the darkest year of their lives, to finally pay her back.

She saved more people that year with a filing system than most of the Order saved with wands. She never learned the exact number. She stopped herself from trying to count, because she had discovered that the number, whatever it was, was the one thing that could crack the box she kept her feelings in, and she needed the box intact for a while longer.

And she did one more thing, the thing she'd been building toward since a diary in a cauldron when she was twelve. She found them. Not all of them, and not alone — but where Harry Potter's hunt for Voldemort's hidden soul-pieces proceeded by luck and courage and terrible danger, Hermione's proceeded by *deduction*, by cross-referencing property records and family histories and the psychology of a man she understood better than she liked, and twice — twice — an anonymous note reached the right people at the right moment, telling them where to look and what they'd find and how to destroy it without dying, and twice it was correct, and no one ever knew who sent it.

She and Harry Potter never met, not properly, not through all seven years and one war. They were the two halves of the same story, told in opposite keys — the seen and the unseen, the sword and the ledger, the hero the world would build statues of and the woman the world would never quite be able to name — and each of them, in the end, made the other's victory possible, and only one of them ever knew it.

*End of Chapter Ten.*

## **Chapter Eleven: The Battle, and the Bill**

The war ended at Hogwarts, as everyone had always somehow known it would, on a night of fire and falling stone.

Hermione Granger fought in that battle, because there are moments when even the most careful person must finally be seen, and she had judged — correctly, to the hour — that this was the one. She fought brilliantly and coldly and without a single wasted motion, and the curses she had spent seven years reading about in books that weren't on any list turned out to work exactly as advertised. But her real work that night was the work she'd always done: she was the one who knew which corridors were safe, which walls would fall, where the wounded could be moved and the children hidden. Her network, seven years in the building, became on that single night the nervous system of the entire defense — messages crossing the castle in seconds, no one caught unaware, the resistance moving like one organism because a girl at the center of it had spent seven years making sure it could.

And when Voldemort fell at last — killed by his own rebounding curse, in the Great Hall, in front of everyone, with Harry Potter standing over him in the dawn light as the hero the world needed — Hermione Granger was not standing in the Great Hall.

She was two floors up, in a quiet room, with Draco Malfoy and his mother and a dozen other people who had chosen, at the end, to survive rather than to die for a cause, and she was already — while the cheering rose from below, while the statues were as good as commissioned — thinking about *after.* About the vacuum. About who would fill the Ministry, and the *Prophet*, and the thousand offices where the real power lived, now that the loud men had finished killing each other. About the fact that she was nineteen and Muggle-born and the single most connected human being in magical Britain, owed by half the survivors and feared by the other half, holding a ledger of favors and secrets that reached into every institution the country had.

"They're going to give Potter an Order of Merlin," said Draco, listening to the roar from below. He said it without bitterness now; the bitterness had burned off him somewhere in the last two years, along with a great deal else. "First Class. Statues. His face on the chocolate frog cards."

"Good," said Hermione. "He earned it. And it'll keep everyone looking at him." She was already writing something on a small pad of paper, in her rapid orderly hand — a list, of course; it was always a list. "While they're all looking at Harry, someone's going to have to actually rebuild the country. Quietly. Correctly. Someone who knows where every body is buried, and who owes what to whom, and how the whole machine actually works underneath the speeches." She tucked the list into her pocket and looked up, and Draco Malfoy — who had known her for seven years and been terrified of her for six of them — saw the future arrange itself behind her eyes and understood, with a lurch of the old awe, that the war everyone thought had just ended was in fact merely *changing hands.*

"That's you," he said. "Isn't it. You're going to run it. All of it. And nobody's ever going to know."

"That's the whole point, Draco," said Hermione Granger, and smiled the smile that had unsettled him in a corridor when they were eleven years old. "I thought you'd have learned that by now. Loud is for people who want to be thanked."

*End of Chapter Eleven.*

## **Chapter Twelve: Respectable Terror**

Here is what became of Hermione Granger, told the way she'd have wanted it told, which is to say: mostly in what other people said, because she herself said very little on the record, ever, about anything.

She never held the highest office. She was asked — twice — and she declined, both times, gracefully, because she had understood since she was eleven that the person in the highest office is the person everyone can aim at, and she had spent her whole life declining to be aimed at. Instead she took a position three rungs down and slightly to the side, a position with a dull name and no glamour and, if you knew how to read the org chart the way she read everything, more actual power than the office above it. From there, over twenty years, she rebuilt the magical world of Britain into something it had never been: a place where a Muggle-born could be sorted into any house and thrive, where ability was, at long and grudging last, worth more than blood — not because she gave speeches about it, but because she quietly arranged the incentives until it simply became true, and then let everyone believe it had been their own idea.

She was, depending on who you asked, either the most respected witch of her generation or the most feared, and the interesting thing — the thing that would have delighted the eleven-year-old on the bathroom floor — was that these turned out to be the same people. The wizards who feared her respected her. The wizards who respected her were, if they were honest, a little afraid. She had proven, with her whole life, the thesis she'd worked out in the green underwater light of the Slytherin library at the age of eleven: that respect and fear are not opposites but neighbors, two doors off the same hall, and that a Muggle-born witch too competent to marginalize, too connected to purge, and too useful to cross could walk between them as she pleased.

They gave the medals to other people. She let them. She collected, instead, the thing she'd been collecting since first year — the quiet, total, unpayable regard of everyone who had ever needed her, which was, by the end, very nearly everyone — and she kept it in a ledger that no one else could read, and she never, not once in her whole life, spent it carelessly.

Snape, who survived the war — barely, and only because a certain former student had known enough of the shape of his secret to make certain the right people reached him in time with the right antivenins, a debt he understood the size of and never once mentioned, which was how he thanked her — became, in the years after, the Headmaster of Hogwarts, and the two of them corresponded until the end of his life in letters that consisted almost entirely of arguing about potions theory in the margins and never once said anything sincere, which was how they both preferred it, and which was, in its way, the truest friendship either of them ever had.

Millicent Bulstrode ran Hermione's affairs, guarded her back, and was, to the end, the one person alive who got the whole truth, and who could make her laugh, and who had offered her a seat at a table full of people hoping she'd cry, forty years ago, and had never once been made to feel she'd gotten the worse end of it, because she hadn't.

And Draco Malfoy — reformed, humbled, quietly grateful, raising a son of his own in a world his own choices had helped, in a small way, to change — sat that son down, on the eve of the boy's first year at Hogwarts, and gave him the single most important piece of advice he knew, the distillation of everything a lifetime had taught him, and it was this:

"Scorpius. Listen to me. You're going to meet all sorts at that school. Brave ones, clever ones, kind ones, cruel ones. And somewhere in your year, most likely, there'll be someone who doesn't say much, and always seems to know things a little before everyone else, and does you favors you didn't ask for. And you'll be tempted to think that person is harmless, because they're quiet, and because they're helpful."

"What do I do?" asked Scorpius.

Draco Malfoy looked at his son with the grave, hard-won solemnity of a man passing on the one truth that could save a life.

"Be *very* polite to them," he said. "Always. And whatever else you do, whatever it costs you, however clever you think you're being—" He gripped the boy's shoulder. "Do not, under any circumstances, get into a debt with a Granger."

*End of Chapter Twelve.*

## **Chapter Thirteen: Epilogue — Nineteen Years Later**

The Hogwarts Express let out its usual autumn shriek, and Platform Nine and Three-Quarters filled with the usual autumn chaos, and a woman with untamable hair going elegantly grey at the temples stood a little apart from the crowd, watching the children board, holding nobody's hand and needing to.

She was not famous. That was the first thing you'd have noticed, if you'd known enough to notice anything: that in a crowd where several people were being quietly pointed at — there was Harry Potter, over by the barrier, greying and kind and beloved, waving his own children off — this woman stood entirely unremarked, and seemed to prefer it that way, and yet the odd thing, if you watched long enough, was how many people *came to her.* A harried mother, murmuring a thank-you for something. A Ministry official, pale, asking a quick low question and going away steadier. A shopkeeper. A journalist, who approached with a question and left, oddly, having answered several of the woman's instead. They came to her the way water comes to the lowest point in a room, naturally, without seeming to decide to, and she received each of them with the same unhurried attention, and filed each of them, without appearing to, in the vast and orderly system behind her calm brown eyes.

A first-year near her was crying. A small boy, Muggle-born by the bewildered look of his parents, terrified of the train and the castle and the whole impossible world he'd been told he belonged to and did not, yet, believe it.

The woman crouched down to his level.

"They're going to sort you tonight," she said, conversationally, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world, which to her it was. "Into one of four houses. And if you end up somewhere you didn't expect — somewhere that doesn't seem to want you — I want you to remember something a very old hat once told me." She smiled, and the boy, who a moment ago had been inconsolable, found himself listening. "It said: *they have no idea what they've asked for.*"

"What does that mean?" said the boy.

"It means," said Hermione Granger, "that the places that don't want you are the places you can win completely, because no one there is watching for it. It means being underestimated is the most valuable thing you will ever own, and you should guard it like gold and spend it like a miser. It means" — she straightened his collar, briskly, the way she did everything — "that you are going to be absolutely fine, and quite possibly a great deal more than fine, and that the people who are unkind to you tonight will spend the rest of their lives being *extremely careful* around you, if you play it correctly. Which I will teach you to do, if you like. I run a sort of quiet club for exactly this. We don't have meetings. We don't have a name. We just look after each other, and we remember everything, and we are never, ever, the loudest people in the room."

The boy stared at her. "Who *are* you?"

The train shrieked again. Down the platform, Harry Potter's youngest was climbing aboard, off to be brave and beloved and the center of everything, exactly as it should be, exactly as it had always been. And two floors of Ministry and a generation of survivors and one reformed Malfoy and a portrait of a sharp-tongued former Headmaster who still argued potions theory with her in the margins of her days — all of it, the whole quiet architecture of a world remade — hummed on beneath the ordinary morning, invisible, load-bearing, hers.

"Nobody in particular," said Hermione Granger, and stood, and watched the Muggle-born boy climb aboard the train with his chin a little higher than it had been, off to a castle that would try to tell him what he was, off to find out, as she had, that it would be entirely up to him to decide.

The Express pulled away. She watched it out of sight — she always did; it was the one sentimental thing she permitted herself, on schedule, once a year — and then she turned, and walked back into the ordinary world she quietly ran, and nobody stopped her, and nobody pointed, and that, she reflected, as she had reflected for forty years, was rather the entire point.

*The End.*

---

*A note on the ending, for the record she'd never keep: The Sorting Hat, asked in its retirement about the most consequential decision of its thousand-year career, is said to have considered the question for four and a half minutes — the length, coincidentally, of a certain sorting — and then to have answered, "I put a girl who wanted to survive into the house that teaches you how. Everyone thought I'd made a mistake. I had never in my life been more certain. You don't hand ambition to the ambitious. That's just weather. You hand it to the one person clever enough, and hurt enough, and* patient *enough to use it correctly — and then you spend the rest of eternity very glad she decided to be kind about it."*

## **Plot Summary**

**Act One — The Wrong Sort of Snake (Year 1).** Hermione arrives in a house that regards Muggle-borns the way one regards a slug discovered in a salad: with horror and a strong desire to have it removed. She is hexed, iced out, and "accidentally" locked out of the common room on the third night. Her response is not to cry into her pillow (well — not *only* that). It is to *read the house rules*. Slytherin, she discovers, is not actually built on blood. It is built on power, leverage, and the appearance of unbothered competence. Blood purity is simply the currency the current management happens to favor. Currencies, Hermione knows, can be devalued.

She stops trying to be liked. She starts becoming *indispensable*. She corrects a professor's arithmancy in front of the Board of Governors' visiting nephew. She quietly saves Draco Malfoy from failing Potions — then lets him owe her. She befriends the one person nobody thinks to weaponize: **Millicent Bulstrode**, who is lonely, fiercely loyal, and can lift a first-year over her head. Instead of a troll-in-the-dungeon rescue, the year turns on Hermione outmaneuvering a cursed object smuggled into the school — solving it two days before Harry Potter (a distant Gryffindor rumor to her) blunders into the same mystery, and letting *him* take the credit because credit is loud and she prefers leverage.

**Act Two — The Currency of Fear (Years 2–4).** As the Chamber of Secrets opens, being Muggle-born in Slytherin becomes genuinely dangerous, and here the tone darkens in flashes: Hermione is a target of the Heir, and her own housemates would not mourn her. But she has spent a year making herself useful to exactly the people who can protect her, and she flips the crisis into her ascendance — she's the one who deduces the basilisk (she just doesn't get petrified, because she's the one holding the mirror). By fourth year she has a reputation the older students can't quite name: the Muggle-born who somehow always knows the thing you needed and hadn't asked for yet, who never raises her voice, and whom it is *unwise* to cross. The Malfoy debt has compounded. Snape, who despises sentiment but worships competence, becomes a grudging, acidic mentor — the running joke being that he can't decide whether he's proud or appalled, and settles on both.

**Act Three — Respectable Terror (Years 5–7, compressed).** The wider war arrives. Hermione's position is genuinely perilous — a Muggle-born who is *also* the most connected student in the house most aligned with the coming darkness. Rather than flee to the obvious side, she builds something new: a quiet network across all four houses, run on favors and blackmail-shaped kindness, that becomes the most effective intelligence operation in the castle. She is never Dumbledore's soldier and never Voldemort's; she is the third thing everyone eventually has to negotiate with. Feared? Yes. Respected? Also yes — the two turn out to be the same door with different signs on it.

**Ending.** Hermione doesn't win the war with a wand duel. She wins it with a filing system, a decade of receipts, and the fact that she was the only person in Britain everyone owed and no one could afford to have as an enemy. She ends the series not as a hero in the Gryffindor mold, but as something the wizarding world has never had a word for and badly needs one: a Muggle-born witch too competent to marginalize, too dangerous to purge, and too useful to kill — who used the House of ambition exactly as intended and turned its own snobbery into the ladder she climbed. The last line of the series is Draco Malfoy, now middle-aged, telling his son: *"Whatever you do, do not get into a debt with a Granger."*

**Tone.** Comedy of manners with fangs. The humor lives in Hermione's deadpan, the absurdity of eleven-year-olds performing aristocracy, Snape's exhausted sarcasm, and Millicent's literal-mindedness — punctuated by real stakes so the jokes land against something solid.
